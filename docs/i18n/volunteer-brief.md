# Translating nidhi: who you are writing for, and how it should sound

Thank you for helping. This page is the short version of what you need before you translate or review a word of nidhi. The full reference is [`../brand-identity.md`](../brand-identity.md); if the two ever disagree, that page is right and this one gets fixed.

## What nidhi is

nidhi (nidhi.today) helps ordinary people understand their money: short lessons, free calculators, and a planner being built. It does not sell financial products and does not tell anyone what to do with their money. It explains, shows the evidence and its limits, and leaves the decision to the reader.

## Who you are writing for

One site, for a whole working life:

1. **Someone in their first job** with no idea how to manage money. Nobody taught them, and the words are intimidating.
2. **Someone a few years in**, with bigger questions: retiring early, saving for goals, planning for children, what-ifs.
3. **Someone close to retirement**: turning savings into income, wills and estates, and the mistakes that are expensive to make late.
4. **Someone retired**, who reads the site to judge it, and should find it sound enough to send to their children, nieces and nephews.

**The test for every page:** someone in their first job can follow it, and a retired reader would trust it enough to forward it. For every sentence: would a 22-year-old feel welcome, and would a 70-year-old feel respected? If not, it needs another try.

## How it should sound

**A polite friend a few steps ahead.** Someone who has done the reading and remembers not knowing; a friend who is also a good teacher. Warm and at eye level, never stiff, and never rude, careless or overfamiliar. The authority comes from knowing the subject, not from distance.

In practice:
- **Plain, everyday words.** Explain every term before it is used. Never "basic", "obvious", "easy" or "just".
- **Polite always.** If a sentence would be wrong to say to a stranger's grandparent, rewrite it. No slang, no emoji, no exclamation marks in teaching text.
- **Teach, and leave money decisions with the reader.** Explain the options, the evidence, its limits and what depends on circumstances. Describe common practice ("a common starting point is...") rather than instructing ("Start with...", "You should..."). Neutral actions ("Enter the amount you borrowed") are fine.
- **Hard subjects plainly and gently.** Death, debt and family conflict are named, without blame, drama or euphemism.
- **Feelings once, conditionally.** "This can feel overwhelming", never "I know how you feel". No guaranteed outcomes, and no reassurance in place of explanation.
- **Humour is rare and gentle**, about systems or complexity, never the reader. None in tools, or around debt, distress, illness, bereavement, estates, family conflict, fraud, losses, errors or warnings. If a joke does not work in your language, drop it; nothing depends on it.

## What this means for your language

**How to say "you".** Use the form, natural in your language, that a knowledgeable, polite friend would use with an adult of any age: warm and respectful, never intimate and never stiff. Don't copy another language's level of formality. If no single form does both, choose the one an older reader would not find disrespectful, and carry the warmth through how the sentences are written. Follow your language's glossary where the choice is agreed; where it is open, you advise and the owner decides.

So far:

| Language | "You" |
|----------|-------|
| Hindi | आप |
| Spanish | *tú* |
| Czech | *vy* (lowercase) |
| Marathi | तुम्ही |

These were each chosen for their own language; they are a reference, not a rule for yours.

**Never give the reader or the founder a gender.** The founder writes nidhi and speaks as "I" now and then. If your language marks gender in verbs, adjectives or pronouns, reword: the existing glossaries show how each language does it.

**What stays as it is:**
- **Names**: nidhi, product and company names, and people's names stay in their own script.
- **Lessons** are written once, in English, and are not translated. A page in your language lists them and says they are in English.
- **Amounts, sources and data caveats** stay as the English has them: translate, don't localise.
- **No em dashes or double dashes.** Use a colon, a comma, or reword.

## How your advice is used

You advise; the owner decides. Your language's glossary in this folder (`<code>-glossary.md`) lists the open questions and draft terms; your verdicts and reasons go into it as decisions are made. One person's view is not proof of what every reader prefers, so if you are unsure about something, say so, and it stays open. Glossaries record decisions and reasons, never volunteer names or other identifying details.

Questions you can always ask: does this sound like a polite friend in my language? Would my parents' generation find it respectful? Would my younger cousins find it stiff?
