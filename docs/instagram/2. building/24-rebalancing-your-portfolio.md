# Rebalancing: How to Keep Your Portfolio on Target

## Caption

You set 70 percent stocks and 30 percent bonds. A year passes. Stocks had a strong run. You're now at 78/22, account value up. Your risk profile has quietly changed. You're holding a more aggressive portfolio than the one you chose, without ever deciding to.

That's drift. Rebalancing is the discipline of pulling the portfolio back to its target on a schedule, regardless of what markets are doing.

Three methods work. Calendar, once a year. Threshold, when any class drifts more than 5% from target. Contribution-based, redirect new money to whatever's underweight. More frequent rebalancing does not improve returns; research consistently lands on annual or 5% bands as the sweet spot.

The point of rebalancing is risk control, not return enhancement. Boring, automatic, and one of the few investment activities that actually matters.

## Slide 2 (prose)

title: Rebalancing pulls the portfolio back to target

Trim what's grown above target. Top up what's lagged. On a schedule, regardless of markets.

The point is risk control, not return enhancement.

---

## Slide 3 (list)

title: Three rebalancing methods that work

1. Calendar: once a year, same date
2. Threshold: when any class drifts more than 5% from target
3. Contribution-based: redirect new money to the underweight class

Pick one. Combining two is fine. Changing methods every quarter is not.

## Slide 5 (prose)

title: Rebalance tax-advantaged first, taxable last

Inside tax-advantaged accounts, trades trigger no current tax event. Rebalance freely.

In a taxable account, selling appreciated positions can trigger capital gains tax that erodes the rebalancing benefit. Use new contributions to rebalance instead.

If the same allocation lives across both wrappers, do the work in the tax-advantaged side first. Touch taxable only when the tax-advantaged side alone can't close the gap.

---

## Slide 6 (stat)

label: What the evidence says about rebalancing frequency
hero: Annual or 5% bands
caption: More frequent rebalancing does not improve returns.
note: After transaction costs and taxes, monthly or quarterly rebalancing typically underperforms. Markets have short term momentum; rebalancing too often sells winners too early and buys losers too soon.

source: Industry and academic research consistently lands on annual or 5% bands as the sweet spot. Past performance is not a forecast.

## Slide 7 (list)

title: The mistakes that cost the most

1. Rebalancing emotionally in response to news
2. Ignoring drift for years until the portfolio bears no resemblance to the plan
3. Selling appreciated positions in taxable, ignoring the tax bill
4. Treating rebalancing as a market timing tool in disguise
5. Following the rule for a quarter, then overriding it on a feeling
