The post on [when the default plan doesn't fit you](/blog/when-the-default-plan-does-not-fit-you/) set out a simple audit. For every financial decision, ask what the default assumes about you, and whether that holds. Most defaults assume a household of one adult, or two who are legally a pair.

This post is for the households where that fails in a particular way. There are three or more adults, and their money is tied together.

That covers a lot of people. Friends sharing a rented flat. A housing co-operative where members own shares in a building. A cohousing community with private homes and shared spaces. An intentional community or a large shared house that has lasted decades. Three or more partners, whether or not they all live together. Some of these households have romantic ties between members and many have none. The money problems are close to identical, so this post covers them together and marks where they part.

Here is the situation that shows why it matters.

Mira and Tobias are married. Kai has been their partner for six years. The three of them bought a house together. The mortgage broker offered the couple joint ownership, so the title names Mira and Tobias as joint tenants. Kai put in €60,000 of the deposit and has paid a third of every mortgage payment since. None of it is written down.

Mira dies suddenly.

Under a joint tenancy, Tobias now owns the entire house automatically. Kai has no automatic right to any of it. At best there is a claim to argue in court, with nothing in writing to support it. The €60,000 and the payments look, on paper, like a gift. If Tobias then dies without a will, the house goes to Tobias's relatives.

Nobody planned that outcome. It is what the defaults produce when a legal system can see two owners and the household has three.

This post covers the everyday money of a shared household first, then the exit test, then the places where the law stops seeing you and the documents that fill each gap. It teaches the categories, because leases, property law and family law differ in every country. For the documents themselves, use a professional where you live.

## Part one: splitting costs on purpose

Shared households often start with a number picked in the first week and never revisited. Each method below has consequences, so it is worth calculating them.

Take a household of four adults with these monthly net incomes:

| Person | Net income |
|---|---|
| Alma | €5,000 |
| Ben | €3,000 |
| Chidi | €2,000 |
| Dana | €1,000 |

Shared costs are €3,200 a month: €2,400 rent, €300 utilities, €500 food.

**Method A: equal split.** Everyone pays €800.

**Method B: income-based split.** Everyone pays the same share of their income. Total income is €11,000, so the shared costs take 29.1% of it. Each person pays 29.1% of their own income.

| Person | Method A: equal | As % of income | Method B: income-based | As % of income |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alma | €800 | 16% | €1,455 | 29% |
| Ben | €800 | 27% | €873 | 29% |
| Chidi | €800 | 40% | €582 | 29% |
| Dana | €800 | 80% | €291 | 29% |

The equal split feels simple and fair. In this household it takes 16% of Alma's income and 80% of Dana's. The gap in how heavy the cost feels is five to one. The income-based split levels the burden, and it asks each person to share their income with the group, which some households will not accept.

Other methods sit between these:

- **Room-based.** The rent is weighted by room size, light, or a private bathroom. Utilities and food are split equally or by use.
- **Hybrid.** Rent by room, everything else by income. Or an equal base amount with an income-based top-up for larger shared goals.
- **Capped.** Everyone pays an equal share up to a ceiling, and the higher earners cover whatever is left.

There is no correct answer here, and a household may reasonably choose any of them. What tends to go wrong is a method that nobody chose, which surfaces as resentment about who bought what.

### What to write down

- **The method and the date to revisit it.** Incomes change. Yearly, or after anyone changes jobs, is a common rhythm.
- **What counts as a shared cost.** Rent, utilities and internet usually do. Food, cleaning supplies, a shared car and the repair fund depend on the household.
- **A threshold for shared decisions.** A new sofa and a new roof are not the same kind of decision. Many households set an amount above which everyone must agree, and an emergency exception below which anyone can act.
- **How you handle surprises.** A broken boiler, a sudden rent rise, someone's job loss.

### Keeping the money separate

A common structure is separate personal accounts plus one shared account for the bills. Each person sets up a standing order to the shared account on payday, and the rent and bills come out of it.

The risk sits in the shared account itself. Any holder can usually withdraw the entire balance, and each holder can be liable for an overdraft. Keeping only the month's expected bills in it, with a small buffer, limits what one person could do or lose. Some banks limit the number of holders on a joint account, so check before assuming it will work for four.

This ties directly to the post on [account consolidation](/blog/account-consolidation-and-financial-data-hygiene/). Fewer accounts are easier to track, and a shared account is a case where one more is worth having.

### The conversation underneath

Two people's money habits already clash. Add a third, a fourth and a fifth, and the [money scripts](/blog/money-scripts/) (the unconscious beliefs about money that people carry from childhood) multiply. One housemate thinks unpaid bills are an emergency. Another thinks they are a suggestion.

Agreeing how open you will be helps. Some households share full incomes. Others share only what is needed to calculate a split. Both work when everyone knows which one it is.

## Part two: the exit test

A household of four is more stable than a household of two, because more people share the fixed costs. It is also more exposed to change, because there are more people who might leave, and each departure can hit the rest.

The **exit test** is a calculation. For each person, ask: if this person left next month and the room stayed empty, what would the household cost the people remaining?

Using the same household:

- With all four, the €3,200 in shared costs is 29% of total income.
- If Alma leaves, the remaining three earn €6,000. Rent and utilities stay at €2,700. Food falls by about a quarter, to €375. Shared costs are now €3,075, which is 51% of their income.
- Under the income-based method Alma was paying €1,455. The household saves €125 on food and has to find the other €1,330 every month for as long as the room is empty.

Now set that beside the post on the [emergency fund](/blog/emergency-fund/). In a shared household, the fund has a second job. It covers your own loss of income, and it also covers the months after someone else leaves.

A few things follow from running the test:

- **Whose departure hurts most.** Usually the person paying the largest share. That person is the one whose plans matter most to everyone else.
- **How long finding a replacement takes.** Even a few weeks of an empty room adds up.
- **Whether the lease lets you replace a tenant.** Some leases need the landlord's approval. Others let tenants find their own replacement.
- **Notice periods.** A shared agreement can set one, so that leaving takes a fixed number of weeks and gives the others time to adjust.

Written down, the results are a plain summary of the household's shared risk. It is the cash runway question from the post on [cash flow forecasting](/blog/cash-flow-forecasting/), how long could you cover your costs if income stopped, asked of the group instead of an individual.

## Part three: where the law stops seeing you

The law has ready-made frameworks for individuals, for married couples, and for companies. A group of friends or partners sharing a home is none of those. It falls back on ordinary contract and property law, and that law looks at names on documents. This series calls the distance between how a household works and what the law can see a **recognition gap**. A household of three or more has one by construction.

Four places to check.

### 1. The lease

In many places, a shared tenancy makes each tenant liable for the whole rent. The landlord can pursue any one of you for the full amount. This is **joint and several liability**. If one housemate leaves, or stops paying, the rest carry their share, and recover it from them afterwards if they can.

Read the lease for how it describes the tenants. Ask whether individual tenancy agreements per room are available. If the utilities are in one person's name, that person is liable to the supplier, and the others' payments to them are only a private arrangement.

The deposit belongs in the record too. Who paid, how it is returned, and whether a leaving tenant is repaid on departure or when the next one arrives.

### 2. Buying together

When three or more people buy a home, how the title is held decides what happens on a death, a departure or a dispute. The two main forms in common-law countries, also covered in the post on [unmarried couples](/blog/unmarried-and-cohabiting-couples/):

**Joint tenancy with right of survivorship.** All owners hold the whole property together. When one dies, the survivors automatically take that share. In a group of three or more, the last owner alive ends up with the entire property, and no will changes it.

**Tenancy in common.** Each owner holds a stated share, equal or unequal. Each share passes under its owner's will, or to relatives without one.

For a group, tenancy in common is more often the fit, because it records who put in what and lets each person choose who receives their share. Neither form is right for everyone.

The loan is a separate question from the title. Many lenders limit how many people can be named on one mortgage, and everyone who is named is liable for the whole debt, not a share of it. Someone who pays toward a mortgage without being on the loan or the title, as Kai did, has the weakest position of all: the cost without the ownership.

Civil-law countries use other concepts, such as co-ownership by defined shares, and often add rules on pre-emption (whether the other owners get first refusal on a sale) or forced heirship. The questions are the same everywhere: who owns what fraction, what happens to a share on a death, and what happens if someone wants out.

### 3. A co-ownership or household agreement

A written agreement covers what the law does not. It is a contract among the owners or the residents. The parts that matter most:

- **Contributions.** What each person put in, as a deposit, as improvements, and as continuing payments. Kai's €60,000 belongs here.
- **The buy-out formula.** How a leaving owner's share is valued and paid, and how fast. Options include an independent valuation, an agreed formula, or a right for others to buy first.
- **Non-payment.** What happens if someone stops paying their share. Who covers it, and how it is repaid.
- **Sale.** Whether one owner can force a sale, and the notice needed.
- **Death and incapacity.** How a share passes, and who acts for someone who cannot.
- **Disputes.** A route to resolve disagreement before a court, such as mediation.

Agreements like this are more likely to hold when each person has had independent advice. Enforceability differs by country.

### 4. Co-operatives, land trusts and cohousing

Some communal households do not hold property personally. They use a structure that holds it for the group.

- **A housing co-operative.** The building is owned by a co-operative and members hold shares or membership rights that entitle them to a home. (Known as a *bytové družstvo* in Czechia, a *Genossenschaft* in Germany, and a housing co-op in the UK and US.)
- **A community land trust.** A non-profit owns the land, and residents own or lease the buildings, usually with limits on resale price so that homes stay affordable.
- **Cohousing.** Households have private homes and share facilities such as a kitchen or garden. The legal form varies, from co-operative to condominium to company.
- **A company or association holding the property.** Members hold shares in an entity that owns the building.

Each of these changes the answers to the same questions. What do you actually own, a share, a membership right or a lease? Can you sell it, to whom, and at what price? What happens on a death or a departure? Read the founding documents for the answers, because the label alone will not tell you.

## Part four: when partners are more than two

For multi-partner households, everything above applies. One more layer sits on top: in most countries the law recognises at most one partner as a spouse.

Marriage is limited to two people in most legal systems. Where a country does allow a man more than one wife, under civil, religious or customary law, it has its own rules for property and inheritance, and those marriages frequently stop being recognised when a family moves abroad. Everywhere else, the result is that a set of automatic protections is available for one partner and for no one else in the household. The post on [unmarried couples](/blog/unmarried-and-cohabiting-couples/) covers that bundle in full. For a group of partners, the question is not only "what do we replace?" but "who holds the one slot that exists, and how do the others get equivalent cover?"

| Default | Who gets it by law | What extends it to others |
|---|---|---|
| Inheritance with no will | The spouse and blood relatives | A will naming each partner |
| Pension survivor benefits | Often the spouse only | Check whether the scheme allows a nominee; otherwise cover with insurance or savings |
| Medical decisions | The spouse, then relatives | Healthcare proxies, with named alternates in order |
| Hospital access and information | Legal family | A written designation, kept where staff can find it |
| Joint tax treatment | Often one pair | Not extendable; plan around it |
| Tax-free transfers between partners | Often spouses only | Lifetime gifts within allowances; insurance for expected tax |
| A partner visa for a move abroad | Usually one partner | Not extendable; each partner needs their own route |

### Who is the legal spouse

Some households have a married pair plus other partners. Some have no marriage at all. Some have a married pair who did it for tax or immigration reasons. Whichever it is, the spouse role carries real rights and real exposure, and it is worth deciding who holds it and why.

If one partner holds it, the others' cover depends entirely on documents. The partner with spouse status is, by default, everyone's next of kin. It also means a couple within the group can override arrangements the other partners assume are settled. That is one reason to record agreements in writing and not in memory.

### The documents, for more than two

- **Wills.** Each partner's will names each other partner by their full legal name, with the share intended. Where forced heirship applies, a will can direct only the part of the estate that is free to give.
- **Beneficiary designations.** Most pension, insurance and investment forms allow several beneficiaries with percentages. Check each one, and check whether the pension scheme pays survivor benefits only to a spouse.
- **Healthcare proxies.** Usually one person at a time, plus alternates in order. A household of several partners can name each other in a sequence that matches how they would want it.
- **Financial powers of attorney.** They name who manages your money if you cannot. Naming a different person from your healthcare proxy is common and can be sensible.
- **A trust.** Where several people share a home or an investment, a trust (a legal arrangement in which a trustee holds assets for named beneficiaries) can hold the asset and spell out who receives what and when. Trusts are complex and tax-sensitive, so this is a topic for a lawyer where you live.

### Children

If children are part of the household, parentage is a legal question separate from love or care. The law of most countries names two legal parents at most. A later post in this series covers non-biological co-parents and stepchildren, and how the financial gaps appear. For this post it is enough to know that the same rule applies: what the household treats as settled, the law may not.

### Taxes

Tax systems assess individuals and, in some countries, married or registered pairs. There is generally no group filing. Each adult is taxed on their own income, and benefits or allowances that a couple can share are available to at most one pair. A dependant can usually be claimed only once, so the household needs to agree who claims and keep records. What your rates and allowances are, and how a shared home or a gift between partners is taxed, is a question for your own tax authority or adviser.

## Counting a shared asset in your own plan

One practical question comes up for every shared household. What do you put in your own net worth?

A common approach is to count your own share only. If you own 40% of a house, count 40% of its value and 40% of any mortgage attached to it. If you contributed €60,000 to a home you are not on the title of, that is a claim rather than an asset, and it is worth only what you can enforce. That is one more reason to write it down.

A shared bills account is not your money in the same sense. Count the buffer you personally put in and treat the rest as the household's.

## Growing older together

A household that plans to age together carries an assumption that nobody has tested. If you expect to look after each other in old age, the question is what the arrangement covers, and what happens when the person expected to help is the one who needs care.

- Survivor pensions and spousal benefits run through a legal spouse and stop there.
- Care that a household provides informally has no pay and no pension credit. The post on [solo agers](/blog/solo-agers-and-single-income-households/) covers planning for later life when a spouse is not the backstop.
- A shared home may need a plan for a member who needs different housing or care.
- Households that grow older together often benefit from writing down who takes which role, and what the group does if someone can no longer contribute.

The post on [chosen family](/blog/chosen-family-and-next-of-kin/) covers the documents that name the people you rely on when legal next of kin would otherwise default to relatives. The post on [multi-generational households](/blog/multi-generational-household-economics/) covers households that pool across ages, with the same shared-cost questions.

## Cross-continent notes

**England and Wales.** Shared rentals commonly use one joint tenancy agreement, which makes each tenant liable for the whole rent. Owners in a group typically hold as tenants in common and record their shares in a declaration of trust.

**Germany.** Shared flats (*Wohngemeinschaften*) are common. Tenants on one lease are usually all liable together. Housing co-operatives and the *Mietshäuser Syndikat* model are established routes to collective ownership.

**Czechia.** Housing co-operatives (*bytové družstvo*) are a long-standing form, and co-ownership by defined shares (*podílové spoluvlastnictví*) lets several people own a property in stated fractions.

**Denmark and the Netherlands.** Cohousing began in Denmark and is well established in northern Europe. Legal forms vary by community.

**United States.** Options include tenancy in common, limited liability companies holding a property, housing co-operatives and community land trusts. Rules differ by state, and a few cities have created domestic partnership registrations for more than two people, which usually cover local matters only.

**England and Wales, again.** No more than four people can be named on the legal title to a property. A larger group holds through a trust or a company.

**Countries that permit polygamous marriage.** Several in Africa, the Middle East and Asia do, under rules that differ by country and by religious or customary law. Such marriages are often not recognised elsewhere, which leaves later spouses in the position described in the post on [marriages that are not recognised](/blog/when-your-marriage-is-not-recognised-abroad/).

**Most other places.** Marriage is limited to two, so multi-partner households rely on contracts, wills and designations. Rules on forced heirship, survivor benefits and joint tenancy vary, and it is worth checking each in your own country.

## Getting started

- **Tonight, for free:** write down the current cost-split method, and who agreed to it. Read your lease for the words "jointly and severally".
- **This month:** run the exit test for each person and note the result.
- **This month:** check the beneficiary on every pension, policy and account.
- **This quarter:** a household agreement, and a co-ownership agreement if you own together, with independent advice for each person.
- **This quarter:** wills, healthcare proxies and powers of attorney that name the people you rely on.
- **Yearly:** revisit the cost split, the shared-decision threshold and the exit test.

Go back to Mira, Tobias and Kai. A tenancy in common with stated shares, a one-page record of the €60,000, and three wills would have cost a small fee and an afternoon. That is what separates a house Kai can stay in from one Kai has to leave.

The household you have is a real household. The plan for it takes the same steps as any other, with one more name on every form.
