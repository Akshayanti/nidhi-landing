Your net worth is €150,000. Is that good? It's impossible to say without context. A 25-year-old with €150,000 and €5,000 of liabilities is in a radically different place than a 55-year-old with €150,000 and €400,000 of mortgage left. Same net worth, completely different situations.

Net worth is a snapshot. Ratios are the diagnosis. A handful of them, together, tell you whether your finances are healthy, where they're drifting, and what to adjust while the fix is still small.

This post goes through the six metrics that do most of the work for individuals, plus how to interpret them. None of these are targets to optimize toward a single number. They're zones: a healthy range, a cautionary range, a range that should trigger action.

## Why ratios beat single numbers

A single number in isolation gives you very little. €30,000 in savings is great if your expenses are €1,500 a month; it's a thin buffer if your expenses are €6,000 a month. A €250,000 mortgage is comfortable if your net worth is €800,000 and income is €90,000; it's alarming if your net worth is €50,000 and income is €45,000.

Ratios handle this automatically by setting one number against another. The result is a comparable, transferable signal. The same ratio means roughly the same thing whether your scale is thousands or millions.

## Metric 1: Debt-to-asset ratio

**Formula:** Total liabilities ÷ total assets

**What it measures:** How leveraged your balance sheet is. High debt relative to assets means you depend heavily on borrowed money; a shock to either side (falling asset values or loss of income) hits harder.

**Rough zones:**

| Debt / assets | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| 0-20% | Very low leverage. Lots of flexibility |
| 20-40% | Moderate, common for homeowners with a mortgage |
| 40-60% | High, but manageable if income is stable and rates are fixed |
| 60-80% | Concerning, especially with variable rates or unstable income |
| 80%+ | Highly stressed; a modest asset drop can push you underwater |

A young person with a mortgage naturally starts high (a 90% loan-to-value mortgage means a debt-to-asset ratio close to 0.9 early on). That's not automatically bad. It's the expected shape, and it falls over time as the mortgage is paid down and other assets accumulate. The direction matters more than the absolute level for young homeowners.

**What to do if it's drifting up:** Slow down borrowing, focus on paying down high-interest debts (see [snowball vs. avalanche](/blog/how-to-get-out-of-debt)), avoid taking on consumer loans that don't acquire real assets.

## Metric 2: Liquid asset percentage

**Formula:** Liquid assets ÷ total assets

Liquid assets are cash, savings, money market funds, and easily sold investments. Illiquid assets include real estate, private investments, pension assets locked until retirement, and business interests.

**What it measures:** Your flexibility. How much of your wealth could you access within days without selling at a bad price?

**Rough zones:**

| Liquid % | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Under 10% | Very low flexibility; a forced sale could be devastating |
| 10-25% | Low but not uncommon for property-heavy households |
| 25-50% | Healthy for most households |
| 50%+ | Very flexible; typical for early accumulators or debt-averse households |

A high-net-worth homeowner with 90% of net worth in property and 10% liquid is still structurally fragile. Losing the ability to access any of the 90% quickly (because a property takes months to sell) means the 10% has to cover every shock.

**What to do if it's drifting down:** Rebuild cash reserves, avoid loading more into illiquid assets until the liquid buffer recovers, be cautious with large down payments or paid-off primary residences that spike illiquidity.

## Metric 3: Emergency fund coverage

**Formula:** Liquid emergency fund ÷ monthly essential expenses

**What it measures:** How many months of core expenses your emergency fund would cover if income stopped today.

**Rough zones:**

| Coverage | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Under 1 month | Highly fragile; a single paycheck gap creates real risk |
| 1-3 months | Building; adequate for most short shocks |
| 3-6 months | The most commonly recommended range |
| 6-12 months | Conservative; appropriate for single-earner households, variable income, or industries with longer unemployment durations |
| 12+ months | Very conservative; beyond this, further cash is usually better invested |

The [emergency fund post](/blog/emergency-fund) goes into sizing in more detail. Worth noting: coverage is a moving metric. Expenses drift upward as life expands, and an emergency fund that covered six months a year ago may cover four today. The ratio, not the absolute amount, is what to track.

**What to do if it's drifting down:** Rebuild before resuming aggressive investing. Review what's driving expense growth.

## Metric 4: Savings rate

**Formula:** (Income - expenses) ÷ income

**What it measures:** The single most predictive metric for long-term outcomes, as the [FIRE post](/blog/introduction-to-financial-independence) covered. More than any other metric, savings rate controls how quickly wealth builds.

**Rough zones:**

| Savings rate | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Under 10% | Minimal; below this, wealth accumulation is very slow |
| 10-20% | Common baseline; sufficient for retirement over a long career |
| 20-35% | Strong; accelerates retirement and opens earlier options |
| 35-50% | High; puts FI within reach within a couple of decades |
| 50%+ | Very high; FI achievable in roughly 15 years or less |

This metric is also the most volatile month to month, so track it as a rolling 3- or 6-month average rather than reacting to single months.

**What to do if it's drifting down:** Look at the [cash flow categories](/blog/cash-flow-101). Has lifestyle inflation crept into discretionary spending? Are recurring subscriptions growing? Did a large one-off expense hit? Revisit the [budgeting](/blog/budgeting) structure.

## Metric 5: Debt-to-income ratio

**Formula:** Total monthly debt payments ÷ monthly gross income

**What it measures:** How much of your income is already committed to debt service before anything else. Lenders use this to evaluate creditworthiness, and it's a useful self-check even if you're not borrowing.

**Rough zones:**

| Debt-to-income | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Under 20% | Comfortable; strong capacity to save, invest, or take on new obligations |
| 20-35% | Manageable; most lenders consider this healthy |
| 35-45% | Stretched; little room for surprises |
| 45%+ | Stressed; a lost income stream could tip into distress quickly |

This metric becomes especially important before considering large new borrowing, like a new [mortgage](/blog/real-estate-as-investment) or other major loan. Lenders and self-check alike should weigh the *total* picture after the new obligation, not the current one.

**What to do if it's drifting up:** Slow new borrowing. Prepay high-interest debt where possible (see the [understanding loan terms post](/blog/understanding-loan-terms) on amortisation and prepayment power).

## Metric 6: Income replacement ratio

**Formula:** Current passive / portfolio income ÷ current expenses

**What it measures:** How close your existing asset base is to funding your current lifestyle without work. Equivalent to the "crossover point" from the [passive income post](/blog/passive-income-streams) expressed as a ratio.

**Rough zones:**

| Income replacement | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Under 10% | Early accumulation phase; work covers almost all expenses |
| 10-30% | Progress visible; passive income starts noticeably contributing |
| 30-60% | Meaningful independence; partial FI feasible |
| 60-99% | Coast / partial FIRE territory |
| 100%+ | Full financial independence; work is optional |

This metric grows slowly and is easy to miss without deliberate tracking. It's also enormously motivating over long periods. A chart that rises from 3% to 30% over a decade tells a more concrete story than any single-point net worth number.

> The 100% threshold assumes you can sustain that level of passive income indefinitely. That in turn depends on a safe withdrawal rate. 4% is the US-derived anchor from the Trinity Study; in higher-inflation or lower-return markets (parts of Europe, India, Japan, most emerging economies), 3 to 3.5% is more defensible. At a 3.5% SWR, "financial independence" means passive income covers expenses with a portfolio size closer to 28-33× annual expenses rather than 25×.

## A simple health snapshot

Put them all together and you have a one-page health check:

| Metric | Healthy zone | Your value | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Debt-to-asset ratio | <40% (lower is usually better, context matters) | | |
| Liquid asset % | 25-50% | | |
| Emergency fund coverage | 3-6 months | | |
| Savings rate | 20%+ (higher is better) | | |
| Debt-to-income ratio | <35% | | |
| Income replacement ratio | Growing over time | | |

Run this once a year at minimum; quarterly is better once you have the habit. The goal isn't to be in the ideal zone on all six simultaneously. That's rare for real people at any stage of life. It's to know where you are, which direction each metric is trending, and to catch drift early.

## How to read the whole picture

Any single metric can be misleading in isolation. The honest read comes from reading them together.

- **Healthy savings rate but low liquid %** → Probably locked into illiquid assets. Flexibility could bite you in a crisis
- **Low debt-to-income but also low savings rate** → You're paying for your lifestyle from income, not debt, but you're not building wealth
- **High emergency fund coverage but high debt-to-asset** → The cash is effectively canceling out the leverage; consider whether some emergency fund should be used against high-interest debt
- **Strong metrics across the board but low income replacement** → You're set up well but early in the accumulation journey. Time will do the work
- **High income replacement but weakening savings rate** → You may be close to crossover and naturally scaling back; or you may be experiencing lifestyle creep. Which story matches?

The metrics tell you where to look. They don't tell you what to do. The combination does.

## Common mistakes

- **Chasing ideal numbers instead of zones.** Most ratios don't have a single right answer. Pushing for a perfect ratio often creates distortion elsewhere
- **Ignoring direction.** Current level matters, but trend matters more. A metric moving from healthy toward unhealthy deserves attention even before it crosses the line
- **Measuring once, reacting forever.** Ratios drift. Check them regularly
- **Comparing to other people without context.** Someone with a higher savings rate may have different income, living costs, or family situation. Your trajectory matters more than anyone else's snapshot
- **Over-engineering.** Six metrics do the job. Adding fifteen doesn't improve the diagnosis; it dilutes attention
- **Using ratios to judge, not to improve.** The metrics exist to surface drift early. Treating them as scorecards invites guilt, which invites avoidance

## What you can do

1. **Calculate each metric today.** A single afternoon gives you a baseline. Most metrics need only your [net worth statement](/blog/how-to-calculate-net-worth), your monthly [cash flow](/blog/cash-flow-101), and your liabilities
2. **Write the numbers down.** On paper, spreadsheet, anywhere, as long as you can compare to next year
3. **Identify one or two that are drifting.** Almost nobody is in the healthy zone on all six. The useful question is: which one matters most to fix in the next 12 months?
4. **Set one adjustment to move the weakest metric.** A specific monthly change, not a vague ambition. "Add €150 to emergency fund each month for six months" beats "save more"
5. **Rerun at your next annual review.** Minimum. Quarterly if you want faster feedback
6. **Don't panic over single-quarter movement.** Ratios move with life events, market swings, and one-off spending. Trends over a year are the signal
7. **Use the combination, not the individual readings.** Each metric is informative; the pattern across all six is what tells the real story

A dashboard tells you what's happening. Health metrics tell you what it means. Together they're the operational layer of personal finance: the monthly and annual habit that catches problems early, marks progress clearly, and quietly converts good intentions into compound outcomes.

The metrics are only useful if you actually look at them, on a regular schedule, in a format that surfaces drift instead of noise. That's the job of a financial dashboard: a small set of numbers, reviewed at known cadences, designed to catch problems early without inviting daily checking.
