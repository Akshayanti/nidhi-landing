Marcus, 34, earns €54,000 a year before tax, about €3,300 a month after it, and pays €300 a month on a car loan. His bank says he can borrow €330,000 for a flat. A colleague says no more than about €280,000. A friend in Mumbai says closer to €120,000. All three are using a real rule. They are answering different questions.

The [understanding loan terms post](/blog/understanding-loan-terms/) covered how to compare loan offers by their total cost. This post asks the question before that one: how large a loan an income can carry.

## A lender's maximum is not your budget

A bank's offer tells you the most it will lend within its own risk limits and the rules its regulator sets. It answers "will this loan probably be repaid?", using your income, your existing debts, the property's value and, in some countries, a higher test rate.

It does not know what else your money has to do: saving for retirement, an [emergency fund](/blog/emergency-fund/), children, a career change, a parent who may need help. So many people treat the approval as a ceiling and decide separately how much of it to use. Marcus's €330,000 would cost about €1,482 a month at 3.5% over 30 years, about 33% of his gross pay before the car loan, and about 45% of what reaches his account.

## Every borrowing rule is built from the same ratios

Almost every rule, from a rule of thumb to a national regulation, is built from the same four measures:

- **The payment share.** The housing payment as a percentage of income.
- **The total debt share.** All loan and card payments, housing included, as a percentage of income: the [debt-to-income ratio](#term-debt-to-income-ratio), or in some countries the [debt service ratio](#term-debt-service-ratio).
- **The deposit.** How much of the price you pay yourself, which sets the [loan-to-value ratio](#term-loan-to-value-ratio).
- **The term.** How many years the loan runs. A longer term lowers the payment and raises the total interest.

Two details change the answer more than people expect. Whether a percentage is of [gross or take-home income](#term-gross-and-take-home-income): in a country with high taxes and social contributions, 30% of gross pay can be 40% of take-home. And whether a loan is measured by its payment or by its size: the [loan-to-income ratio](#term-loan-to-income-ratio) ignores the interest rate entirely.

## The same income gets very different answers

Here is what each rule, limit or offer implies for Marcus, at 3.5% over 30 years unless the rule sets a shorter term. The amounts are illustrative, computed from his numbers; the national caps apply in their own countries and currencies, shown here only to compare how they work.

| Rule, limit or offer | What it caps or tests | Amount for Marcus |
|---|---|---|
| A 50% cap on all debt payments (the UAE's limit) | All repayments at most 50% of gross pay | about €434,000 |
| A 40% cap on all debt payments (South Korea's bank limit, once household borrowing passes ₩100 million) | All repayments at most 40% of income | about €334,000 |
| His bank's offer | The bank's own model | €330,000 |
| 28/36 (US rule of thumb) | Housing at most 28% of gross, all debt at most 36% | about €281,000 |
| 4.5 times income (the UK's high loan-to-income threshold) | The share of new lending at or above it, not each borrower | €243,000 at the threshold, not a maximum |
| 20-30-40 (Indian rule of thumb), on take-home pay | Instalment at most 30%, all debt at most 40% | about €220,000 |
| 5-20-30-40, the version with 30% down | Price at most 5 times take-home income, 20 years | about €139,000 |
| 5-20-30-40, the version with 40% down | Price at most 5 times take-home income, 20 years | about €119,000 |

The gap between the top and the bottom is more than three to one. The rules give different answers because they measure different risks and make different assumptions, and none of them is evidence that a particular loan is affordable. The high caps are lending limits, meant to stop the riskiest loans, not to describe a comfortable one. The strictest rules of thumb tend to assume a larger deposit, and some use take-home pay, so they leave a wide margin.

Because it is applied here to take-home pay, €3,300 a month, while 28/36 uses gross pay, €4,500. Thirty percent of €3,300 is €990 a month; 28% of €4,500 is €1,260. Applied to gross pay, 20-30-40 would allow about €301,000.

## A stress test shows the loan that still works

Rates and incomes move. A [stress test](#term-stress-test) asks whether the payment would still be affordable if they moved the wrong way, which is why some regulators require lenders to run one (Canada and Australia among them, below).

On a 30-year loan of €280,000, Marcus pays about €1,257 a month at 3.5%. At 5.5% he would pay about €1,590, and at 6.5% about €1,770: from 28% to 39% of his gross pay, before the car loan. On the bank's €330,000, a rise to 5.5% takes the payment to about €1,874, and together with the car loan to 48% of gross pay.

Income is the other half. If his pay fell by a fifth for a year, the €330,000 loan's payment alone would take 41% of the smaller gross income.

Commonly described stress checks include:

- **Rate up by 2 to 3 points**, or to the rate at the next reset if the rate is fixed for only a few years.
- **Income down by a fifth**, or by the amount of a second income if a couple borrows on two salaries.
- **Room left over:** saving and an emergency fund still fit, which matters more with [uneven income](/blog/gig-and-informal-economy-work/).
- **Currency:** a loan in a currency other than the one you earn in moves with the exchange rate, as the [currencies post](/blog/managing-money-across-currencies/) covered.

If a loan passes on today's numbers but fails these checks, whether it stays affordable depends on rates, income and other costs staying favourable for 25 or 30 years.

<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: a loan's payment is 30% of gross pay today. The rate is fixed for five years and then resets. What does a stress test look at?</summary>

The payment at a plausible rate when the fix ends, not only today's. If that payment would pass 40% of gross pay, or the remaining budget no longer covers saving, the loan depends on rates being low again in five years.

## Where the popular rules come from, and where they break

Each rule people quote has a home, and each breaks somewhere.

- **28/36** is a US lender guideline: housing costs, including taxes and insurance, at most 28% of gross monthly income, and all debt payments at most 36%. It is not a regulation, and many US lenders approve above 36%. Because it uses gross pay, it is looser than it looks in countries where tax and social contributions take a larger share.
- **20-30-40** is quoted by Indian lenders: at least 20% down, an instalment (EMI) of at most 30% of income, all debts at most 40%. The sources found do not say whether income means gross or take-home pay.
- **5-20-30-40** adds a house price of at most five times annual income and a term of at most 20 years. One widely shared version says 30% down and an instalment of up to 40% of take-home pay; others say an instalment of up to 30% and 40% down. The same name, two different rules.
- **20/4/10** is a US car-buying rule: at least 20% down, a loan of four years or less, and car costs of at most 10% of gross monthly income. Sources disagree on whether the 10% covers only the loan or also insurance and running costs.

Where they break:

- **Expensive cities.** A five-times-income price cap can rule out buying wherever prices are many times local incomes.
- **Two incomes.** A couple borrowing on both salaries can meet any ratio and still be stretched if one income stops.
- **Low or high rates.** A payment rule allows a much larger loan when rates are low, and that loan can become hard to carry when they rise; a loan-to-income cap does not move with rates.
- **Uneven income.** A ratio on an average month hides the thin months.
- **Everything else.** None of the rules sees childcare, care for a parent, or a plan to retire early.

## What regulators cap where you live

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Borrowing limits in Canada, the UK, the Netherlands, India, South Korea, Australia, the UAE and South Africa</summary>

- **Canada.** Uninsured mortgages must qualify at the higher of the contract rate plus 2 points or 5.25%, so a 3.5% loan is tested at 5.5%. Moving an uninsured mortgage to another lender at renewal, without borrowing more or stretching the term, has been exempt since November 2024 (OSFI).
- **United Kingdom.** The Financial Policy Committee limits mortgages at 4.5 times income or more to about 15% of new lending. From October 28, 2026 that limit applies to the market as a whole rather than to each lender, so individual lenders can do more of it (PRA and FCA, October 2026).
- **Netherlands.** The maximum mortgage is set by law from income tables (Nibud's figures, updated each January) and by the home's value, at most 100% of it, with a little more allowed for energy-saving work.
- **India.** The RBI caps the loan at 90% of the property's value for loans up to ₹30 lakh, 80% up to ₹75 lakh, and 75% above that; stamp duty, registration and documentation charges are normally left out of the value, though banks may include them for homes costing up to ₹10 lakh (RBI master circular, April 2025). Payment limits are set by each lender.
- **South Korea.** Once household borrowing passes ₩100 million, total repayments are generally capped at 40% of income at banks and 50% at other lenders, and since July 2025 a stress rate is added for variable-rate loans.
- **Australia.** Banks and other deposit-taking lenders regulated by APRA must assess new borrowers at a rate at least 3 points above the loan's rate (held at 3 points in May 2026); other lenders are overseen differently.
- **United Arab Emirates.** All loan repayments are capped at 50% of gross salary and regular income, and lenders are told not to treat the maximum as automatic (Central Bank of the UAE).
- **South Africa.** Lenders must assess affordability, including the borrower's discretionary income and all existing debt repayments, before lending (National Credit Act and its affordability regulations).

In many other countries there is no binding cap, and the lender's own model decides. The ideas above apply across countries, though definitions and caps differ.

</details>
