faq:
  - question: "What is the simplest test for whether to refinance?"
    answer: "The usual first test is the break-even: closing costs divided by monthly savings equals the number of months you need to stay in the loan to recoup the costs. A common guideline is a hold period materially longer than the break-even, often 2x or more. If the hold period is close to or shorter than the break-even, the refinance usually does not pay. Example: closing costs of €3,600 on a refinance that saves €200 per month means break-even at 18 months. Someone expecting to keep the loan 5 or 6 more years is comfortably past break-even; someone who might sell in 12 to 18 months probably never recovers the costs. Term changes, penalties and tax treatment can shift the picture, so the test is a starting point."
  - question: "Is the 0.5 to 1.0 percent rate drop rule still valid?"
    answer: "As a rough heuristic, yes, but it is not universally correct. The rule of thumb assumes typical closing costs (about 2 to 5% of loan value in the US, varying widely elsewhere) and a long remaining hold period. It breaks down in three cases: very low closing costs make smaller rate drops worthwhile; very high closing costs require larger rate drops; and short remaining hold periods (planning to sell within 3 years, near the end of the original term) may make any refinance uneconomic regardless of rate drop. A break-even calculation with your actual closing costs and hold period is more reliable than the heuristic alone. The heuristic works reasonably for the middle case (typical costs, long hold); the arithmetic works everywhere."
  - question: "What is a no-cost refinance and is it a good deal?"
    answer: "A no-cost refinance rolls the closing costs into either a higher interest rate or a higher loan balance, so you write no cheque at closing. It is neither inherently better nor worse than a paid-cost refinance; it just moves the cost from the front to the ongoing. For borrowers with short expected hold periods (planning to sell or refinance again within a few years), no-cost refinances often come out ahead because the upfront cost would not be recovered in time. For borrowers with long hold periods, paying closing costs upfront for the lower rate usually comes out ahead because the ongoing savings accumulate. Running both variants through the break-even calculation shows which applies. The fine print matters too: some no-cost refinances lock you into a longer term or prepayment restrictions that partly offset the ostensible savings."
  - question: "Should I refinance federal student loans to a private lender for a lower rate?"
    answer: "The deciding factor is often not the interest rate. Federal student loans in the US, and government loans in some other countries, carry protections that private loans do not have: income-driven repayment plans that cap payments as a percentage of discretionary income, forbearance and deferment options for periods of financial hardship, sometimes forgiveness after a set number of qualifying payments or a set period. Refinancing to private gives up all of these permanently in exchange for a lower rate. Whether the trade is worth it depends on the size of the rate difference, the expected career trajectory, and how much you value the optionality. Households with stable high income, low probability of needing the protections, and a large balance may find the private refinance comes out ahead. For many others, the protections act like insurance worth more than the rate saving, which is why many keep them. Program rules change, so checking the current terms matters."
  - question: "Rates have dropped a bit but might drop further. Should I wait?"
    answer: "Waiting is a bet against the rate market. The professional bond market already prices expected cuts into current rates, so waiting only pays if rates fall by more than the market expects; assuming you know that is a form of overconfidence. If today's refinance passes the break-even test with a comfortable margin, refinancing now starts the payment reduction, and a later refinance remains possible if further drops clear a second round of closing costs. If rates rise instead, waiting has given up the payment savings available from the earlier offer while postponing its closing costs; the full result also depends on how the two loan balances change. That is why many advisers see waiting as a weak trade when the break-even is comfortably inside the hold period, though someone close to a planned move or with very high closing costs faces different arithmetic."

Eva, 31, could [refinance](#term-refinancing) her mortgage, which means swapping it for a new loan at a lower rate. Switching costs about €4,000 up front in [closing costs](#term-closing-costs). After that, her payment drops by €200 a month.

The question is whether she keeps the new loan long enough to win that €4,000 back. She might move in two years. Rates might also fall further.

The [understanding loan terms post](/blog/understanding-loan-terms/) covered how to compare loan offers. This post takes a loan you already have and asks when replacing it pays.

## A refinance pays only after its break-even month

Think of the €4,000 as a hole Eva starts in. Each month, the lower payment fills it by €200. After 20 months it is filled: that is her [break-even month](#term-break-even-month). From then on, every month leaves her €200 ahead.

The quick sum is costs ÷ monthly saving. For Eva, €4,000 ÷ €200 = 20 months.

So what decides it is how long she keeps the new loan: her [hold period](#term-hold-period), from the switch until she sells, pays it off or refinances again. People often keep a loan for less time than they expect, so a common rule of thumb is a hold period of at least twice the break-even month. The free <a href="/free/loan-comparison/" data-attr="post-inline-tool-loan-comparison">loan comparison calculator</a> sets a current loan against a refinanced one and shows the break-even month.

The estimate is only as good as what goes into it:

- **All the costs,** not just the lender's fee: valuation, legal and title fees, any points paid up front for a lower rate, and any [early repayment charge](#term-early-repayment-charge) for leaving the old loan. In the US they often run 2 to 5% of the loan; elsewhere they vary widely.
- **The real saving.** If the new loan runs longer than what is left on the old one, part of the lower payment comes from paying for more years, and the total interest can go up. The quick estimate fits when both loans end around the same time; otherwise, compare what would still be owed on each as well.
- **An honest hold period.**

<details class="quick-check">
<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: break-even is month 20 and Eva expects to keep the loan 24 months. Is that a comfortable margin?</summary>

No. Four months of savings, about €800, on a €4,000 outlay, and a slightly earlier move would wipe it out. A hold period of about 40 months or more would leave room for plans to change.

</details>

## The rate drop rule of thumb and where it breaks

The traditional rule says a refinance is worth a look when rates have dropped 0.5 to 1 percentage point. It works for typical costs and a long hold, and breaks at the edges:

- **Smaller drops can work when costs are low.** €1,000 of costs and €100 a month saved breaks even in 10 months.
- **Larger drops are needed when costs are high.** Heavy transfer taxes or notary fees can mean €12,000 of costs; at €200 a month that is 60 months, longer than many people keep a loan.
- **Near the end of a loan, even a meaningful drop may save little,** because little interest remains; unusually low costs or a very large drop can still change the result.
- **Some refinances make sense without a rate drop:** shortening the term, putting cash in to reach a [loan-to-value](#term-loan-to-value-ratio) threshold, or dropping mortgage insurance where it applies.

The rule is a starting point; a break-even with your own numbers is more specific.

## On a small balance, a good rate drop can take years to pay back

On a €50,000 mortgage with roughly 20 to 25 years left, refinancing from 4% to 3% lowers the payment by only about €25 to €30 a month if the remaining term is kept comparable. With €2,500 of costs, break-even is about seven to eight years, often beyond the real hold period.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Other times a refinance works against you</summary>

**A sale within two or three years:** the costs are unlikely to come back. **An early repayment charge** on the current loan, common in the first years or on promotional rates, belongs in the costs. **Rate lock timing:** between application and closing, often 30 to 60 days, rates can move, and some lenders charge to lock. **A recent late payment or income disruption** can mean a worse rate than expected. **Too little equity:** if prices have fallen, a high loan-to-value ratio can block the best rates or add mortgage insurance that cancels the saving.

</details>

## Waiting gives up the savings available today

Rates fall from 5% to 4.25%. Refinancing would save €200 a month, break even at month 18, and the household expects to keep the loan eight years. The news says the central bank may cut again, so they wait. Six months later rates have risen to 4.75%. Compared with refinancing at once on the stated terms, waiting gave up €1,200 of potential payment savings, while also postponing the closing costs, and the opportunity shrank. A quick first measure is monthly saving × months waited; the full comparison also includes when the costs are paid and any difference in the loan balances.

If today's refinance passes the break-even test with room to spare, acting captures known savings; if rates fall much further, a second refinance is possible once the extra saving clears another round of costs. Someone whose rate is already near historic lows, or who faces very high costs that rule out a second refinance, may reasonably wait a short while for a widely expected cut, but it is still a bet. Many people check once a year and act when the test clears comfortably, rather than trying to time rates.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Why waiting is a bet against the market</summary>

Interest rates are set by central banks and by bond markets whose full-time job is pricing what rates will do. Today's rate already includes the market's expectation of future cuts, so waiting only pays if rates fall by more than the market expects. Assuming you know better than the bond market is a form of overconfidence: a household with a mortgage is not better placed than a bond trader to forecast rates. The option to wait has some value, but in most cases it is smaller than the savings given up while waiting.

</details>

<details class="quick-check">
<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: a refinance would save €150 a month and you wait four months for a hoped-for cut. What has waiting cost so far?</summary>

About €600 of payment savings compared with refinancing at once (€150 × 4). That is not automatically a €600 net loss, because the closing costs were postponed too and the two loan balances may have changed differently.

</details>

## What no-cost, cash-in and cash-out refinances each do

- **No-cost:** the costs are folded into a slightly higher rate (often 0.125 to 0.375 points) or a bigger balance, so nothing is paid at closing. It tends to suit short hold periods; for long ones, paying the costs for the lower rate usually comes out ahead. Some no-cost deals add a longer term or repayment restrictions.
- **Cash-in:** you bring cash to lower the balance at the refinance, often to cross a loan-to-value threshold (below 80% to drop private mortgage insurance in the US, for example). It is the [invest or pay off debt](/blog/invest-or-pay-off-debt/) decision at a single moment.
- **[Cash-out](#term-cash-out-refinance):** a new, larger loan, with the difference paid to you. It is equity extraction, not a saving. People use it to consolidate costly debt (rolling 20% cards into a 5% mortgage cuts the rate but turns unsecured debt into debt secured on the home, and only helps if the cards are not run up again), to renovate, or for business or investment, which adds risk on top of the home. Using it for a car or a holiday turns a short-term purchase into decades of mortgage payments.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">The opportunity cost of closing costs</summary>

Closing costs are also money that could have been invested: €5,000 at 6% a year before inflation would be about €8,950 after ten years, though that return is not guaranteed. A fuller test asks when the refinance recovers its cost plus what that cost could have earned elsewhere. For a long hold it is a small correction; for a borderline case, such as a six-year break-even, it can flip the answer. A no-cost refinance ties up no cash, so its equivalent cost is the higher ongoing rate.

</details>

## Student, car and personal loans

**Student loans.** Government student loans in several countries come with protections private loans lack: repayments tied to income, pauses in hardship, sometimes forgiveness. Refinancing to a private lender gives those up permanently for a lower rate. For many borrowers the protections are worth more than the saving; households with stable high income, little chance of needing them and a large balance may find the private rate worth it. Programme rules change, so current terms matter. **Car and personal loans** usually have low or no costs and short terms, so break-even comes quickly, but balances fall fast and the interest left to save shrinks; the biggest gains tend to come in the first year or two. A home equity line, usually variable, can be fixed by converting it to a fixed-rate second mortgage, which follows the same break-even test.

## Refinancing where you live

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Refinancing in the US, UK, eurozone, India, Australia, Canada, Brazil and China</summary>

- **US.** Closing costs typically 2 to 5% of the loan. 30-year fixed mortgages are standard, so refinancing is the main way to change the rate. Federal student loans carry substantial protections.
- **UK.** Fixed rates usually last two to five years, then revert to a standard variable rate; remortgaging to another lender at the end of the fix is the usual route. Costs often £1,000 to £2,500, and product fees (a higher fee for a lower rate) compare directly with the break-even test.
- **Eurozone.** Mechanics vary widely. Some countries have early repayment penalties that make refinancing unattractive, and notary and transfer fees can reach 5 to 10% of the balance, pushing break-even out many years. Offset mortgages exist in a few countries.
- **India.** A balance transfer to another lender is the usual route, with processing fees often 0.5 to 1% of the balance, so the rate gap needed is usually higher.
- **Australia.** Refinancing is common; costs are discharge fees on the old loan and application fees on the new one. Offset accounts give a similar effect without a formal refinance.
- **Canada.** Terms usually last five years and renew; refinancing mid-term can trigger an interest rate differential penalty that can be very large on fixed rates, so many people calculate it before deciding.
- **Brazil.** Moving a loan, a mortgage included, to another bank (credit portability) is a right under central bank rules, and the costs of the transfer between the two banks cannot be passed to the borrower. A property valuation, registry office fees and the new bank's fee for a new customer can still apply, so the comparison is the new loan's total effective cost against the break-even month. The new loan cannot have a larger balance or a longer term.
- **China.** In October 2024 banks lowered the rates on most existing home mortgages in one batch, with a floor of the benchmark loan prime rate minus 0.3 percentage points, without borrowers having to apply; housing provident fund loans and commercial property loans were left out. Since November 2024 borrowers can also ask their bank to renegotiate the margin over that benchmark, so cheaper borrowing usually comes through the existing bank rather than through refinancing elsewhere.

</details>
