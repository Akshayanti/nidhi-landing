  - question: "How do I decide what insurance I actually need?"
    answer: "The common framework asks two questions for each risk type: what is the maximum plausible loss, and could I absorb that loss from cash and portfolio without material harm? Where the answer is yes, people often self-insure, skipping the cover or choosing a high deductible. Where it is no, insurance covers the portion they cannot absorb. The answer changes as net worth grows: a household with €20,000 in savings cannot self-insure a €50,000 medical event or a €200,000 liability claim, so insurance does real work. A household with €500,000 in liquid assets can absorb a moderate loss but not a catastrophic one; coverage narrows to the catastrophic scenarios. A household with €5 million in assets and no dependants can self-insure most standard events; coverage narrows further to specific-catastrophic-only. The insurance basics post covered the five types generically; this post is about right-sizing as your ability to self-insure grows."
  - question: "Should I ever buy whole life insurance?"
    answer: "For pure protection, the case is usually weak. Whole life insurance combines a term-life death benefit with a savings component (cash value that grows over time). The combined product can carry substantial fees and commissions. Whether it finishes behind term insurance plus separate investing depends on the policy's guarantees, illustrated values, tax treatment, investment return and whether the premium difference is actually invested. The exceptions are narrow: specific estate-planning strategies for large estates in jurisdictions where the tax treatment of whole life provides unique benefits, or specific situations where forced savings through the insurance premium is the only way to accumulate savings for a specific person. For most buyers whose goal is protection, term-and-invest-the-difference has come out ahead on the numbers, provided the difference actually gets invested. Whole life is often sold with large commissions, which is a reason to ask how the seller is paid. Products and tax treatment differ by country (endowment and unit-linked plans in India, for example), so the comparison is worth running on local terms."
  - question: "How much liability insurance do I need, and why is it so cheap?"
    answer: "Liability insurance pays what you owe someone else if you injure them or damage their property: a cyclist you hit, a guest who falls in your home, a leak that ruins the flat below. Serious injury claims can reach hundreds of thousands of euros, and in some countries millions. Cover is cheap because serious claims are rare. Where it comes from depends on the country: motor liability is compulsory almost everywhere, personal liability is often a separate low-cost policy (as in Germany and the Netherlands) or part of home or contents insurance (as in the UK and France), and in the United States and Canada an umbrella policy adds a layer above home and motor limits. A common rule of thumb is liability limits at least equal to net worth, though what a claim could reach depends on the country's legal system. Not having personal liability cover at all, or having limits far below your net worth, is a common gap among households whose other policies are adequate."
  - question: "When should I drop life insurance?"
    answer: "Life insurance exists to replace income that dependants would lose if you died prematurely. Once dependants are financially independent (no children needing support, spouse can maintain lifestyle from portfolio and their own income, no mortgage the surviving spouse could not cover), the case for life insurance largely fades. When that happens varies widely with family timing and savings. Past that point, many people let term cover lapse rather than renew or convert it, since the premiums buy a hedge they no longer need. One caution: cover dropped is hard to get back if health changes, so people often wait until they are confident the dependency has truly ended. The exceptions: households with special-needs dependants who will need ongoing support, business owners with buy-sell agreements that require life insurance funding, estate-planning situations where illiquid assets (business, real estate) would be difficult to divide without a life-insurance liquidity source. Outside these specific cases, life insurance is a temporary tool with a defined end date."
  - question: "Which cover is usually a poor place to cut?"
    answer: "Health insurance is usually a poor place to cut in jurisdictions where a serious medical event can produce catastrophic uninsured costs. This is most acute in the US, where uninsured serious illness can produce six-figure medical bills that would wipe out most household net worth. It is less acute in jurisdictions with strong public healthcare (most of Europe, UK, Canada, Australia, Japan), where the marginal difference between higher and lower private supplemental coverage is small. Where serious illness can be catastrophic, going without health coverage in the hope of self-insuring leaves the household exposed to the worst outcomes, which is why it is rarely where people cut. Liability limits are similar: a serious auto accident or premises liability claim can produce judgments that exceed even substantial net worth. Where umbrella cover is sold (mainly the US and Canada) it is cheap, and many households with meaningful assets consider going without it a false economy."

Eva set up her insurance at 25, with a €5,000 emergency fund and a starter salary. At 45 she has a €500,000 portfolio and her children are grown, and she still has the same policies, plus a few added when someone offered them. Like many mid-career households, she is over-insured in some places and under-insured in others.

The [insurance basics post](/blog/insurance-basics/) covered the main kinds of cover. This post covers fitting them to a growing net worth, using one question for each type: what is the largest plausible loss, and could savings and investments absorb it without serious harm? Products, legal minimums and public safety nets differ a lot by country, so the answers depend on where you live and who depends on you.

## The losses you can self-insure grow with your net worth

Insurance loses money on average, because premiums have to cover claims, costs and the insurer's profit. Its value is turning an unpredictable large loss into a predictable small cost, so the common rule is to insure what you cannot afford to lose and use [self-insurance](#term-self-insurance) for what you can. What changes over a lifetime is what you can afford to lose. Cover that is essential at €20,000 of net worth can be optional at €200,000 and unnecessary at €2 million.

That does not mean dropping all insurance. Some losses are too large for almost any household: a catastrophic illness where healthcare is private, a multi-million-euro liability claim, losing an uninsured home. The line moves out with wealth, but it does not disappear.

## Health cover is rarely the place to cut back

Health insurance has an extreme tail in some countries: in the US private system, one serious illness can produce six-figure bills, and out-of-network rules can leave large costs even for insured patients. Very few net worths can absorb that, which is why people rarely go without. Where public healthcare covers most costs, the difference between more and less private top-up cover is much smaller.

There is still room to choose within health cover: the deductible, the type of plan, high-deductible plans with a US health savings account, and separate dental or vision cover. The riskiest moment is a gap between old cover ending and new cover starting, such as leaving an employer plan before US Medicare at 65 (the [income replacement ratio post](/blog/income-replacement-ratio/) covered it) or moving between countries ([geographic arbitrage](/blog/geographic-arbitrage/) covered that). People switching systems usually arrange the overlap in advance.

## Term life usually costs less than whole life for protection

Life insurance replaces income that dependants would lose if you died early. [Term life insurance](#term-term-life-insurance) pays only if you die during a set period, often 10, 20 or 30 years, so it is cheap: perhaps €300 to €500 a year for €500,000 of 20-year cover at 30, depending on health. [Whole life insurance](#term-whole-life-insurance) pays whenever you die and builds a cash value, so premiums are often 10 to 20 times higher for the same payout, and sellers' commissions on it are large.

Take €500,000 of 20-year term at €400 a year against whole life at €5,000. Investing the €4,600 difference at the start of each year at an illustrative 6% before inflation would give roughly €180,000 after 20 years. That result is not guaranteed and depends on investing the difference every year. A fair comparison uses the policy's guaranteed and illustrated cash values, fees, tax treatment and access rules; depending on the product, cash value may be reached through withdrawals, loans or surrender, each with possible costs or effects on the death benefit. Whole life has narrower uses: some estate-planning strategies for large estates where its tax treatment helps, business buy-sell agreements, and people who would not otherwise save.

Life cover exists for financial dependence, not marital status. Once nobody would struggle without your income (children working, a partner able to live on their own income and the portfolio, the mortgage paid off), many people let term cover lapse. Because cover is hard to get back if health changes, people often wait until they are confident the dependence has ended. While cover is in place, a legal spouse is often the default on the [beneficiary form](#term-beneficiary-form), and an unmarried partner usually has to be named deliberately.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Disability cover: when it matters most, and when it fades</summary>

Disability or income protection insurance replaces income if illness or injury stops you working. It matters most early in a career, when future earnings are large and savings small: for a 30-year-old, the gap between expected lifetime earnings and current savings can be €1 to €3 million, which is why many look at cover replacing 60 to 70% of income where state benefits would not be enough. For a 55-year-old with €1.5 million of liquid assets and ten years to planned retirement, the possible loss is far smaller, and many drop the cover once the portfolio could absorb a permanent loss of income.

**What to compare:** employer group cover is usually cheaper than individual policies, though group benefits are often capped (commonly 60% of salary up to a limit); own-occupation cover pays if you cannot do your own job and costs more than any-occupation cover, which pays only if you cannot work at all; and a longer waiting period before benefits start lowers the premium but needs cash to bridge the gap.

</details>

## Liability limits in line with what you own

Most insurance protects something you own. [Liability insurance](#term-liability-insurance) protects you from what you might owe someone else: a guest injured in your home, a cyclist you hit, a leak that ruins the flat below. Damages for a serious injury can reach hundreds of thousands of euros, in some countries millions. Because serious claims are rare, large limits are cheap; few products give as much protection per euro. If a covered judgment exceeds the policy limit, the uninsured balance may be pursued from income or assets, subject to local enforcement and asset-protection rules: a €500,000 judgment against a €300,000 limit leaves €200,000 uninsured, and with no personal liability cover every euro is uninsured. Many households with assets keep limits at or above their net worth as a rough guide, though possible claim sizes, protected assets and future income matter too.

Where cover comes from depends on the country. **Motor liability** is compulsory almost everywhere; EU minimums for personal injury run to several million euros per accident, while legal minimums in much of the US are low. **Personal liability** for everyday life is usually a separate policy costing well under €100 a year in Germany and the Netherlands, with limits of €10 million or more, and part of home or contents insurance in the UK and France, often up to one or two million. An [umbrella policy](#term-umbrella-policy), sold mainly in the US and Canada, adds a layer above home and motor limits for a few hundred dollars a year per million. The gap people most often find when they check is no personal liability cover at all.

<details class="quick-check">
<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: net worth €400,000, personal liability limit €1 million. Is the household exposed?</summary>

A covered claim up to €1 million is paid, subject to the policy's terms; a larger judgment could still leave an uninsured balance even though the limit exceeds the net worth. With a €250,000 limit, the uninsured part of a claim would start above €250,000.

</details>

## Deductibles, old cars, and cover many people drop

A higher [deductible](#term-deductible) (the part of a claim you pay yourself) lowers the premium. If moving from €500 to €2,500 saves €300 a year, seven claim-free years save €2,100, more than the extra €2,000 you would pay on a claim. With low claim frequency and savings to cover the higher amount, higher deductibles have tended to save money, and moving from €500 to €2,500 is a common change once the emergency fund is full. On an old car, collision cover can cost more than it is likely to pay: €400 a year to protect a car worth €4,000, and falling, is €1,200 over three years, which is why many owners drop it if they could replace the car from savings. The liability part of the same policy is different: it protects net worth, not the car, and is compulsory in most countries.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Cover many households pay for without needing, and home insurance details</summary>

- **Extended warranties** on electronics and appliances: high premium relative to the item, few claims, and some countries' consumer law already covers faults for a period.
- **Disease-specific riders,** such as cancer-only cover: narrow, and often duplicating health insurance.
- **Credit card payment protection:** expensive for the balance protected, with frequent exclusions.
- **Whole life insurance** outside the narrow cases above.
- **Rental car damage waivers:** often duplicating your own motor cover or card benefits.
- **Mortgage life insurance:** often dearer than an ordinary term policy for the same cover, with cover falling while the premium stays flat.
- **Cancellation-only travel cover:** the loss is usually absorbable; medical cover abroad is a different matter.
- **Dental discount plans,** which are discount schemes rather than insurance.

Each is profitable for the seller, and any can still suit a specific person; the test is the same two questions.

**Home insurance:** building cover is usually set to the cost of rebuilding, not the market value, which includes land; valuables such as jewellery, art or instruments are often listed separately; and where umbrella cover is sold, home liability limits are usually set to fit under it.

</details>

## A yearly review shows which risks have changed

A common routine takes about an hour a year: list every policy (life, disability, health, motor, home or contents, liability, any riders and extended warranties still running); for each, ask what loss it covers and whether savings could absorb it; then, for any loss that is not covered, find the cheapest way to close the gap. Sometimes one higher liability limit closes several gaps, and sometimes a higher deductible pays for it.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Insurance where you live: the US, UK, eurozone, India, Australia and Canada</summary>

- **US.** Employer health cover is typical before 65, then Medicare. High liability exposure makes umbrella cover common among households with assets. Term life is widely available; whole life is heavily marketed; default motor and home liability limits are often low.
- **UK.** The NHS covers essentials; private health cover is optional. Life and critical illness cover come through employers and individually. Personal liability is usually in home insurance up to £1M or £2M.
- **Eurozone.** Public healthcare in most countries, with private top-ups varying. Personal liability, separate or in home policies, commonly has limits of €1M to €10M.
- **India.** Health insurance matters because private costs for uncovered events are significant. Term life is cheap; endowment and unit-linked (ULIP) plans are heavily marketed and often criticised for high costs compared with term cover plus separate investing. Personal accident cover is a distinct product.
- **Australia.** Medicare covers essentials; private cover is popular. Compulsory third-party injury cover for vehicles, often with third-party property cover added. Home insurance commonly includes personal liability up to $20 million.
- **Canada.** Provincial health insurance covers essentials, with workplace or private top-ups. Motor insurance is regulated by province; umbrella cover is available.

</details>
