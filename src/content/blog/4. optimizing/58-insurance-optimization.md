Insurance sold to a 25-year-old with a €5,000 emergency fund and a starter salary is almost always different from insurance appropriate to that same person at 45 with a €500,000 portfolio and adult children. The default is to keep whatever was set up early, add pieces as they are marketed, and re-examine only after a specific event exposes a gap or an excess. The result, for many mid-career households, is a mix that is simultaneously over-insured in some places and under-insured in others.

The [insurance basics post](/blog/insurance-basics/) covered the five insurance categories for early-stage readers. This post covers the fine-tuning: how coverage needs tend to change as net worth grows, which policies people commonly drop, keep or add, and how to think about the tradeoff between transferring risk and self-insuring it.

The framework is one question applied to each type: what is the maximum plausible loss, and could I absorb it from cash and portfolio without material harm? The answer shapes the coverage, alongside what your country's public system already covers.

## The self-insurance frontier

The [insurance basics post](/blog/insurance-basics/) gave the rule: insure what you cannot afford to lose, and self-insure what you can. Insurance is a losing bet on average, since premiums have to cover claims, costs and profit; its value is turning an unpredictable large loss into a predictable small cost. What changes over a lifetime is what you can afford to lose.

**The self-insurance frontier.** As net worth grows, the set of losses you can absorb grows with it. Coverage that was essential at €20,000 net worth becomes optional at €200,000 and often obsolete at €2 million. Insurance optimisation is the process of tracking the frontier: dropping coverage that no longer meets the self-insurance-can't-cover-it test, adding coverage for the specific catastrophic scenarios that remain uncovered, and choosing deductibles and coverage levels that align with the current frontier.

This does not mean the goal is to drop all insurance. Some catastrophic losses are large enough that no reasonable net worth self-insures them: a catastrophic health event in a private-insurance jurisdiction, a multi-million-euro liability judgment, a total home loss without homeowners' coverage. The frontier moves out but never disappears entirely.

## Health insurance: rarely the place to cut

Health insurance sits in its own category because the downside distribution has an extreme tail: in some jurisdictions, a single serious medical event can produce costs that exceed almost any household's net worth. This is most acute in the US private-insurance system, where individual medical bills can reach six figures without insurance, and where surprise-billing and out-of-network rules can produce large uncovered costs even for insured patients. It is less acute in universal-public-healthcare jurisdictions.

**Why people rarely cut it.** Going without health coverage in the hope of self-insuring leaves the household exposed to the catastrophic tail, which very few net worths can absorb in a private-insurance system.

**Where optimisation still exists.** Within health-coverage choices, meaningful optimisation is possible. Deductible level, coverage type, HSA-eligible high-deductible plans in the US, supplemental coverage on top of public in mixed systems, dental and vision separately. For most working-age households in employer-sponsored coverage, the plan choice happens during annual enrolment and is one of the higher-leverage decisions of the year.

**Retirement and moving abroad.** The [income replacement ratio post](/blog/income-replacement-ratio/) covered the US gap between leaving employer cover and reaching Medicare at 65, and the [geographic arbitrage post](/blog/geographic-arbitrage/) covered cover across borders. The insurance point for both is the same: the gap between old cover ending and new cover starting is where an uninsured event does the most damage, which is why people moving between systems usually arrange the overlap in advance.

## Life insurance: why term is the usual comparison

Life insurance replaces income that dependants would lose if you died prematurely. Two structurally different products exist: term life (pure insurance for a defined period) and whole or permanent life (insurance combined with a savings or investment component).

**Term life.** Pays a defined death benefit if the insured dies during the term (typically 10, 20, or 30 years). Premiums are low because most people do not die during the term; the insurer collects premiums and pays out only in the minority of cases where death occurs. For a 30-year-old buying 20-year €500,000 term coverage, premiums might be €300 to €500 per year depending on health.

**Whole life or permanent.** Combines a death benefit (paid whenever the insured dies) with a cash-value component that grows over time. Premiums are much higher than term (often 10 to 20 times higher for the same face value) because the insurer must fund both the eventual guaranteed payout and the growing cash value. Marketed heavily by insurance salespeople because commissions on whole-life policies are large.

**The math.** Compare a household buying €500,000 of 20-year term at €400 per year versus €500,000 of whole life at €5,000 per year. The whole-life household pays an extra €4,600 per year in premiums for the cash-value feature and the eventual death benefit guarantee. If the term-life household invests the €4,600 difference in a diversified portfolio at (say) 6% a year before inflation, after 30 years the investment account is worth roughly €385,000, though that return is not guaranteed and depends on actually investing the difference every year. This typically exceeds the cash value of the whole-life policy at the same point, often by a wide margin. The term-plus-invest strategy also provides flexibility (the investment is accessible; the whole-life cash value is only accessible through loans or surrender, both of which have costs).

**When whole life might make sense.** Narrow cases: specific estate-planning strategies for large taxable estates in jurisdictions where life-insurance proceeds have favorable tax treatment; buy-sell agreements for business partners requiring a specific funding vehicle; households who cannot mentally separate "insurance" from "savings" and would otherwise not save the difference. Outside these cases, it has generally been an expensive way to buy protection.

**When to drop life insurance.** Once dependants are financially independent, life insurance stops serving its purpose. Common crossover points: children finishing university and starting careers, spouse able to maintain lifestyle from portfolio, mortgage paid off. The test is financial dependence, not marital status, so it applies equally to a partner you are not married to. One difference matters while the cover is in place: a legal spouse is often the default beneficiary of a policy, whereas an unmarried or legally unrecognised partner usually is not and has to be named deliberately. When this happens varies widely. Past that point, many people let term policies lapse rather than renew or convert them, since the premiums buy a hedge they no longer need. Because cover is hard to replace if health changes, the timing is worth being sure of.

## Disability insurance: scale down as portfolio grows

Disability insurance replaces income if you become unable to work due to injury or illness. It is often provided as a group benefit through employment; some employees also carry supplemental individual policies.

**When it matters most.** Early to mid-career, when future earning power is substantial and the portfolio is small. The maximum plausible loss from a career-ending disability at 30 is the difference between projected lifetime earnings and current savings, which is often €1 to €3 million. That is why many people at this stage look at cover replacing 60 to 70% of income, where state disability benefits would not cover enough.

**When it matters less.** As the portfolio grows and years-to-retirement shrink, the maximum loss from a disability shrinks. A 55-year-old with €1.5 million in liquid assets and 10 years to planned retirement faces a much smaller loss from disability than a 30-year-old with €30,000 in liquid assets and 35 years of working life ahead. The disability coverage that mattered a great deal at 30 may matter much less at 55.

**Coverage considerations.**

- Employer group coverage is usually cheaper than individual policies for equivalent coverage, which is why many people take what the employer offers.
- Individual supplemental coverage tends to come up when employer coverage has low caps (typical group policies replace 60% of salary up to a cap that is limiting for higher earners) or when portability across employers matters.
- Own-occupation coverage (pays out if you cannot perform your specific job) is more valuable than any-occupation coverage (pays out only if you cannot work at all); it is also more expensive.
- Elimination period (waiting period before benefits start) trades premium against out-of-pocket. Longer elimination period = lower premium, but you need enough cash reserves to bridge the gap.

**When people drop it.** Usually once the portfolio could self-fund a permanent income loss without material harm, which for many happens in the years before planned retirement.

## Liability: the cover that protects everything else

Most insurance protects something you own. Liability insurance protects you from what you might owe someone else: a guest injured in your home, a cyclist you hit, a leak that starts in your flat and ruins the one below. Damages for a serious injury can run to hundreds of thousands of euros, and in some countries to millions. That is enough to wipe out a lifetime of savings.

**Where your cover comes from.** It depends heavily on the country, which is why the first step is to check what you already have.

- **Motor liability** is compulsory almost everywhere. Across the EU, the legal minimum for personal injury runs to several million euros per accident, and some countries require unlimited cover. Elsewhere, including much of the United States, the legal minimums are low.
- **Personal liability** covers everyday life outside the car. In Germany and the Netherlands it is usually a separate policy costing well under €100 a year, with limits of €10 million or more. In the UK and France it is usually part of home or contents insurance, often with limits of one or two million.
- **Umbrella liability** is an extra layer above your home and motor limits, sold mainly in the United States and Canada, typically for a few hundred dollars a year per million of cover. Where the underlying limits are low, it is the standard way to raise them.

**Why it is cheap.** Serious liability claims are rare, and the underlying policies handle most small ones. A large amount of cover therefore costs little. Few products give as much protection per euro of premium.

**When it matters.** Once your assets exceed your liability limit, everything above the limit is exposed. A household with €500,000 net worth and a €300,000 liability limit is exposed for €200,000 or more of any large claim. A household with no personal liability cover at all is exposed for every euro.

**Checking it.** Find the liability limit on every policy you hold: motor, home or contents, and any standalone personal liability. Having no personal liability cover at all is the gap people most often find. Where limits sit below net worth, the usual ways to raise them are a higher limit on an existing policy, a standalone personal liability policy, or an umbrella layer where one is sold. Because the cover is cheap, many people lean towards higher limits.

## Property and auto: adjust deductibles as savings grow

Property (homeowners, renters) and auto insurance protect specific assets. As savings grow, the tradeoff between premium and deductible shifts.

**The deductible tradeoff.** Higher deductible = lower premium. A €500 deductible might cost €X per year; a €2,500 deductible might cost €X minus €300 per year. The extra €2,000 you would pay out of pocket for a claim comes with a €300 per year premium reduction; over 7 years without a claim, you save €2,100 in premiums, more than the deductible increase. If you have a claim in that period, you pay more for that specific claim. On average, if claim frequency is low, high deductibles save money.

**When people raise deductibles.** Usually once cash reserves comfortably cover the higher deductible without disruption. For many households with a full emergency fund, moving from a €500 to €2,500 deductible is a common adjustment.

**When people drop collision coverage.** On older vehicles where the potential payout (vehicle value) is small enough that the collision premium exceeds the risk-adjusted value. A 10-year-old vehicle worth €4,000 with a €400 annual collision premium: over three years without an accident, you spend €1,200 on coverage protecting an asset worth €4,000 that will likely be worth less by then. For many owners, dropping collision at some point comes out ahead, provided they could replace the car from savings.

**Why liability cover is different.** Collision protects your car; the liability part of the same policy protects your net worth, as the section above explained, and is compulsory in most countries. Cutting it saves little and exposes a lot.

**Home insurance considerations.** Dwelling coverage (rebuild cost) is usually set to what it would actually cost to rebuild, not the market value (which includes land). Personal property coverage typically defaults to a percentage of dwelling coverage; valuable specific items (jewellery, art, musical instruments) are often scheduled separately or added by rider. Where umbrella cover is sold, home liability limits are usually coordinated with it.

## Insurance to consider dropping

A partial list of coverage that many households pay for without needing:

**Extended warranties on electronics and appliances.** The premium is typically a large percentage of the item cost; the claim rate is low; the maximum plausible loss is the item cost itself. Many people self-insure instead, letting savings absorb the rare item failure; some countries' consumer law already covers faults for a period.

**Cancer or specific-disease insurance riders.** These pay out only for the specific disease and typically have benefits that overlap with your primary health insurance. Marketing appeals to fear; the actual coverage is often narrow and duplicative.

**Credit-card payment protection.** Insurance that promises to pay your card balance if you become disabled or lose your job. Coverage is expensive relative to the small balance protected; the frequent exclusions make claims difficult.

**Whole-life insurance beyond narrow estate-planning cases.** As covered above.

**Rental car damage waivers.** Often duplicative with your auto insurance and credit-card benefits. Check both before buying at the counter.

**Mortgage life insurance (declining term to pay off the mortgage on death).** Often more expensive per unit of coverage than a normal term-life policy of the same value; the coverage declines while premium stays flat.

**Flight-only travel insurance.** For cancellation-only cover, the maximum plausible loss (flight cost plus small incidentals) is typically absorbable. Medical cover abroad is a different matter, particularly in countries with expensive private healthcare.

**Dental discount plans (not the same as dental insurance).** Marketed as insurance, actually discount schemes with limited value.

Each of these is profitable for the seller, which is worth remembering when one is offered. Any of them can still make sense for a specific person; the test is the same two questions.

## The three-step annual insurance audit

Once a year, run through this quick checklist.

**Step 1: List every insurance policy.** All of them. Life, disability, health, auto, home or renters, personal liability or umbrella, any specialty (jewelry rider, business liability, travel), any extended warranties still active.

**Step 2: For each policy, ask the two frontier questions.** What maximum loss does it cover? Can I absorb that loss without material harm? If yes to the second, consider dropping or reducing.

**Step 3: For each risk not adequately covered.** What is the cheapest way to close the gap? Sometimes one higher liability limit closes several gaps at once. Sometimes raising a deductible to pay for higher liability cover is a net improvement.

The full audit takes an hour a year. Over a career, it can save multiples of what any given policy costs.

## Cross-continent notes

The framework is universal; the specific product structures vary widely.

- **US.** Employer health coverage typical pre-65; Medicare from 65. High liability exposure makes umbrella coverage common among households with assets. Term life widely available at competitive rates. Whole life aggressively marketed. Auto and home liability limits often low by default, so worth checking.
- **UK.** NHS covers essentials; private health optional. Life and critical-illness insurance available through employer and personal channels. Umbrella coverage less common; personal liability included in most home insurance up to £1M or £2M. Product structures generally simpler than US.
- **EU / eurozone.** Public healthcare in most countries; private supplemental variable by country. Liability included in home policies (called "personal liability" or similar) with limits of €1M to €10M common. Umbrella-equivalent products vary by country.
- **India.** Health insurance widely considered important (private-market cost significant for uncovered events). Term life available at low cost; endowment and unit-linked (ULIP) products aggressively marketed and often criticised for high costs compared with term cover plus separate investing. Public liability less litigious; umbrella coverage less common. Personal accident coverage a distinct product.
- **Australia.** Medicare covers essentials; private cover popular. Compulsory third-party personal injury cover for vehicles; many add third-party property cover on top. Personal-liability included in home insurance to $20 million typical.
- **Canada.** Provincial health insurance covers essentials; workplace or private supplemental typical. Auto insurance regulated provincially; umbrella coverage available.

In every jurisdiction, the framework applies: what maximum loss cannot I self-insure, and what is the cheapest way to transfer that specific risk? The specific products and thresholds are local; the arithmetic is universal.

## Getting started

Run the three-step audit above once, and put the next one in the calendar for a year from today. If there is only time for one part, liability is a common place to start: write down your limits next to your net worth and see whether there is a gap. Households with dependants and no life cover often also compare term quotes sized to the years until those dependants are independent. What to buy, if anything, depends on your country's public safety net, your savings and who relies on you.
