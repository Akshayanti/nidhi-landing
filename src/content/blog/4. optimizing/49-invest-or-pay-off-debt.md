Every personal-finance forum, every year, cycles through the same question: I have some extra money each month, and I have debt, and I want to invest. Which should I do first? The question is asked most often about a mortgage, a student loan, or a moderate-rate car loan, and the answers received are usually some combination of "always pay off debt first" and "always invest, debt is fine" and "it depends," none of which is quite right.

The actual answer is arithmetic with two adjustments. The arithmetic gets you 80% of the way; the two adjustments (employer match, tax treatment) refine it. And a third ingredient (the sequencing of multiple debts simultaneously) turns a two-way comparison into a priority list. This post lays out the whole framework.

## The core comparison

Every euro of extra cash flow can go to one of three broad destinations: paying down debt, investing in taxable accounts, or investing in tax-advantaged accounts. Each has an expected rate of return. The rational allocation goes to whichever destination has the highest expected after-tax return, adjusted for risk.

**Debt payoff return.** When you pay down a debt, you avoid the interest you would otherwise have paid. The return is exactly the debt's interest rate. If a debt carries 6% interest, paying it off is a 6% return, guaranteed and immediate. This is what the [get out of debt post](/blog/how-to-get-out-of-debt/) called out as one of the highest-quality returns available in personal finance: no market risk, no counterparty risk, no time delay.

**Investment return.** Diversified equity portfolios across developed markets have returned about 4 to 6% a year after inflation since 1900, with meaningful variation by country and period (the [investing 101 post](/blog/investing-101-asset-classes/) covers the asset classes). A debt's interest rate is quoted before inflation, so the like-for-like comparison is the return before inflation too: about 6 to 8% a year if inflation runs around 2%. The examples below use 7%. Any specific projection is an assumption, not a fact. The expected return is a range, not a point; and it is not guaranteed year-to-year even if the long-run average holds.

The comparison, in its cleanest form, is straightforward:

- Credit card at 22% versus expected investment return of 7%. Pay the credit card. This is not close.
- Mortgage at 3% versus expected investment return of 7%. Invest. This is also not close on expected value; behavioural considerations may still justify accelerated mortgage payoff for some households, but the math clearly favours investing.
- Auto loan at 6.5% versus expected investment return of 7%. This is close enough that either answer is defensible and the tie-breaker is preference, tax treatment, and risk tolerance.

Most of the confusion in this question comes from the middle range. The two clear cases (very high-rate debt, very low-rate debt) are actually easy. The middle needs the two adjustments below.

## Modifier one: the employer match

The single largest factor in this decision, for anyone with access to it, is the employer match on retirement contributions. This is often overlooked in the "invest or pay off debt" framing because the match sits inside a different mental compartment.

**Why the match dominates.** A full employer match on retirement contributions is typically 50 to 100% of the contribution up to a cap. That is a 50 to 100% return on the matched euro, immediately, with no market risk. Even at the low end (50% match), it dominates every consumer debt rate. Even 24% credit card debt is far below 50%.

**What "capture the match" means.** Contribute to your employer-matched retirement plan up to whatever percentage of salary triggers the full match. If your employer matches 100% of contributions up to 6% of salary, you contribute 6%. If they match 50% up to 8%, you contribute 8%. Anything less leaves free money on the table, and the free money is worth more than any debt payoff.

**The one exception.** If a debt is at genuinely default-risk levels (unable to make minimum payments, facing wage garnishment, credit-collection action), the immediate cash-flow crisis takes precedence over even the employer match. In practice this is rare; if minimum payments on debts are manageable, the match still wins.

**The urgency.** Employer match is contractually available in the current year only. The match you did not capture in year one is not carried forward. Every year you contribute below the match cap, that year's match evaporates permanently. This is unlike debt payoff, which is available to you next month or next year at the same rate.

The rule: once minimum payments are covered and a one-month cash buffer is in place, capture the full employer match before anything else with extra cash flow. Then compare remaining debt with remaining investment options.

## Modifier two: tax treatment of debt

Some debts carry tax-deductibility on the interest paid. This lowers the effective cost of the debt for comparison purposes.

**Where this shows up.** Mortgage interest is the most common example. In some jurisdictions (US historically, with recent caps; India for owner-occupied and rental property under specific sections; many EU countries with variations), a portion of mortgage interest paid is deductible against taxable income. Student loan interest is sometimes partially deductible. Investment-related interest (margin loans, business-purpose loans) has its own rules.

**How to adjust.** If a debt has interest that is deductible against a marginal tax rate of, say, 30%, then each euro of interest paid saves 30 cents of tax. The effective interest rate is the nominal rate multiplied by (1 minus marginal tax rate). A mortgage at 4% with a 30% deduction has an effective after-tax rate of 4% × (1 minus 0.3) = 2.8%.

Compare after-tax debt rate with after-tax investment return, not nominal rate with pre-tax return. Investment returns in taxable accounts also face taxes (dividends, capital gains); investment returns in tax-advantaged accounts do not. The tax-advantaged comparison is closer to nominal-to-nominal; the taxable comparison needs both sides adjusted.

**The caveats.** Tax deductibility rules change. Historical mortgage-interest deductions have been capped, phased out, or removed in various jurisdictions. Do not build a long-term plan around a specific deduction without checking that it currently applies to your situation. If in doubt, use the nominal rate as the comparison point; the tax deduction is a marginal adjustment, not a first-order driver.

**Investment tax treatment mirrors.** Assets in tax-advantaged retirement accounts grow tax-deferred or tax-free depending on account type. The [tax-advantaged accounts post](/blog/tax-advantaged-accounts/) covered the categories. When comparing investing versus debt payoff, investing inside a tax-advantaged account has a slight expected-return advantage over investing in a taxable account, because the tax drag is reduced.

## The multi-debt sequencing framework

Most real households have more than one debt simultaneously. A mortgage at 3.5%, a car loan at 5.5%, a student loan at 6%, and revolving credit card usage at 20% is a common enough combination to make the pairwise decision inadequate. The framework needs to handle multiple debts and multiple investment destinations at once.

The widely-cited priority sequence below reflects the general default in personal-finance literature. It is not a personal recommendation for any specific reader; legitimate reasons to deviate exist and are noted at the end of the section.

**1. Emergency fund minimum.** Before any optimisation, hold at least one month of expenses in cash (the mini emergency fund from the [emergency fund post](/blog/emergency-fund/)). This is not about return; it is about not being forced to take on new high-rate debt to cover an unexpected expense that would otherwise wipe out the entire optimisation.

**2. Capture the full employer match.** As above.

**3. High-rate debt payoff.** Aggressively pay any debt whose after-tax rate clearly exceeds your expected after-tax investment return. In most conditions, this means anything above 8 to 10%. Credit cards, personal loans, some auto loans, and some private student loans fall into this bucket.

**4. Complete the full emergency fund.** Bring the safety buffer up to the three-to-six-month level per the [emergency fund post](/blog/emergency-fund/).

**5. Contribute to tax-advantaged accounts.** Personal retirement accounts, tax-advantaged education savings, health-savings accounts, or their local equivalents per the [tax-advantaged accounts post](/blog/tax-advantaged-accounts/). Up to the annual contribution limit or whatever fraction of the limit is realistic.

**6. Address remaining debt in order of after-tax rate, highest first.** This is the avalanche method from the [get out of debt post](/blog/how-to-get-out-of-debt/) applied to the remaining debts.

**7. Invest the surplus in taxable accounts.** Anything left after the priority above goes to a taxable brokerage.

This sequence is not a personal recommendation about which specific instruments to buy; it is a general priority order derived from the mathematics of return comparison. Legitimate reasons to deviate exist (a specific near-term liquidity need, jurisdictional quirks, income-driven repayment structures on some student loans, an active life event that changes the risk picture). The point is to have a deliberate priority rather than allocating cash to whichever debt or account feels most immediately worthwhile.

## Worked example

To make the sequencing concrete, work through a plausible household situation.

**Setup.** A dual-earner household with €80,000 combined gross income, able to redirect €1,200 per month of extra cash flow once the payroll contributions in step 2 have started. They have:

- Credit card balance: €4,000 at 22% interest, minimum payment €120 per month.
- Auto loan: €18,000 at 5.5% interest, monthly payment €340.
- Mortgage: €280,000 at 3.4% interest, monthly payment €1,250. Interest partially deductible in their jurisdiction, effective rate ~2.6% after tax.
- Employer-matched retirement plan available at both jobs: 100% match on first 4% of salary. Neither contributes.
- No emergency fund yet.
- No taxable investing.

**Applying the sequence.**

**Step 1 (emergency fund minimum).** One month of expenses, say €3,500. Redirect the €1,200 per month for three months to the cash buffer.

**Step 2 (employer match).** Combined 4% of €80,000 = €3,200 per year of contributions, matched with €3,200 of employer money. That is €3,200 of free money per year. Direct contributions from payroll, not from the €1,200 monthly cash redirect. They reduce take-home pay by the after-tax equivalent, so the €1,200 used in the steps below is what is left after that deduction. The return is a 100% instant match plus tax-advantaged growth.

**Step 3 (high-rate debt).** Credit card at 22%. Redirect the full €1,200 per month to the card until it clears. €4,000 at 22% with €120 minimum plus €1,200 extra clears in roughly 3 to 4 months. This is a 22% guaranteed return on every euro directed here.

**Step 4 (complete emergency fund).** Once the card is cleared, redirect the €1,200, plus the €120 card payment it no longer needs, to the safety buffer for about another 11 months, until it reaches 5 months of expenses (about €17,500).

**Step 5 (tax-advantaged accounts).** Increase retirement contributions beyond the match cap up to whatever the tax-advantaged limit allows and the household can afford. Split contributions between the two earners based on their marginal tax rates.

**Step 6 (remaining debt in rate order).** Auto loan at 5.5% versus mortgage at 2.6% after tax. Auto loan wins because the rate is higher. Once the emergency fund and tax-advantaged contributions are handled, extra cash flow accelerates the auto loan.

**Step 7 (taxable investing).** Once the auto loan clears, the household is in a strong position: no consumer debt, full emergency fund, full employer match, meaningful tax-advantaged contributions. Extra cash flow goes to taxable investing (or, for the deeply debt-averse, additional mortgage principal payments; the math favours investing, the behavioural preference varies).

The key insight from the worked example: the priority sequence is not a monolithic "do all of this before starting any of that." Some steps run in parallel (the employer match runs continuously through steps 3 and 4). But the ordering of the primary cash-flow directive matters.

## The mental accounting trap

The [mental accounting post](/blog/mental-accounting/) covered how humans compartmentalise money based on arbitrary mental categories, and how this creates predictable financial mistakes. Nowhere is this more visible than in the invest-versus-debt decision.

**The "biggest debt first" trap.** A household with a €4,000 credit card at 22% and a €280,000 mortgage at 3.4% often reports feeling "not able to breathe" until the mortgage is smaller, and directs surplus cash to prepay the mortgage while the credit card revolves. The math is clear: paying an extra €500 per month on the mortgage saves 3.4% on €500 (€17 per year); directing that €500 to the credit card saves 22% on €500 (€110 per year, until the card clears). The mortgage feels bigger (higher absolute number, bigger emotional weight), so it feels more urgent. The math cares only about rate, not size.

**The "invest and forget the debt" trap.** The mirror-image mistake. A household reads that equities have historically returned around 7% and concludes that all extra cash flow should go to investing, while a credit card keeps revolving. That is a mistake: 22% is 22% every year, guaranteed; 7% is a long-run average with high year-to-year variance. Directing surplus cash flow to investing while a credit card revolves at 22% is a systematic loss.

**The "extra mortgage payment is safe" trap.** A specific version of the first mistake. Prepaying a mortgage is often felt as a very safe move (reducing a large obligation), and there is a legitimate risk-reduction benefit. But the return on the extra mortgage payment is limited to the mortgage rate. Compared with tax-advantaged retirement investing, which typically has expected return well above most mortgage rates, mortgage prepayment is often the lower-return option. The right question is not "is prepayment safe?" but "does prepayment beat the next-best use of the same money?"

**The debt-free-first tradition.** A popular school of personal-finance advice advocates for paying off all debt aggressively before any investing beyond a minimal emergency fund, regardless of rate. This advice is behaviourally powerful for households caught in a debt spiral where any spare cash tends to leak back into consumption; the psychological benefit of visible debt reduction can drive genuine behaviour change. Mathematically, it costs meaningful money for households whose behavioural discipline is not the binding constraint. Both truths coexist; know which one applies to you.

## When behavioural preference legitimately dominates

Sometimes the math says invest and the household chooses to prepay debt anyway. This is not always wrong.

**Sleep-at-night factor.** If holding a mortgage prevents you from taking a lower-paying job you would love, or from reducing hours to spend more time with a young child, or from weathering an income disruption without panic, paying off the mortgage buys real optionality that the pure expected-value math does not price.

**Concentration in a single asset.** For a household whose net worth is heavily concentrated in a single asset (typically the home), reducing the debt against that asset reduces leverage and reduces the size of a downside scenario. This is a genuine risk-reduction benefit, distinct from the pure-return comparison.

**Approaching retirement.** In the 5 to 10 years before retirement, the risk of a bad market sequence (see the [safe withdrawal rate discussion in the FIRE post](/blog/introduction-to-financial-independence/)) makes debt-carry-plus-investing more painful in a bad sequence than in an average sequence. Some households appropriately shift toward debt reduction as retirement approaches even when the pure-expected-value math still favours investing.

**Simplicity value.** For someone who genuinely dislikes managing complexity, paying off debt reduces the total number of moving parts in the financial picture. This is a personal preference, not a financial mistake, as long as the alternative choice is understood.

The rule: know what the math says. Then choose deliberately, with a specific reason, if you want to depart from it. The most expensive mistake is departing without knowing where you started.

## Cross-continent notes

The framework is universal; some debt categories vary.

- **US.** Mortgage-interest deduction is capped but real for eligible households. Federal student loans have unique payment plans (income-driven repayment, public service forgiveness) that change the aggressive-payoff calculation. HELOC (home equity line of credit) interest may be deductible in specific use cases.
- **UK.** Mortgage-interest deductions largely removed for personal mortgages; retained for buy-to-let with restrictions. Student loans are effectively an income-based graduate tax with automatic write-off after a period; aggressive payoff is often the wrong move for standard borrowers.
- **EU / eurozone.** Mortgage-interest deductibility varies widely by country (still generous in some, removed in others). Consumer credit rates typically lower than in the US.
- **India.** Mortgage-interest deduction available under specific sections with caps. Education loan interest deductible with limits. Credit card rates typically 30 to 42% annually; high-rate debt takes precedence over almost every other decision.
- **Australia.** Mortgage interest not deductible on owner-occupied; deductible on investment property. HECS-HELP student loans work like the UK model, indexed but not compound-interest; aggressive payoff usually not optimal.
- **Canada.** Mortgage interest not deductible on primary residence; deductible on investment property. The Smith Manoeuvre and similar structures convert non-deductible mortgage interest to deductible investment-loan interest, changing the calculation for households who use them.

In each case, the sequencing framework applies. The specific numbers and deductibility rules are local; the priority order applies broadly.

## Getting started

Three concrete steps to run the analysis on your own situation.

- List every debt with its exact interest rate. Note any tax-deductibility that applies to your specific situation, and calculate the after-tax effective rate.
- Confirm whether you are capturing the full employer match on retirement. If not, that is the first change to make once minimum payments and a one-month cash buffer are covered.
- Run through the seven-step sequence with your actual numbers. Identify the current step you are on. The next month's surplus cash flow should go there.

The next post in this series covers refinancing timing, which is a specific case of the invest-versus-debt question applied over time. Refinancing is often the highest-leverage optimisation available to any household with a large debt, and the timing decision has its own arithmetic worth understanding.
