Eva, 31, has €500 a month to spare. She has a €280,000 mortgage at 3.4%, a €4,000 credit card balance at 22%, and a workplace pension that matches what she puts in. The mortgage feels like the thing to attack: it is the biggest number in her life. The arithmetic points somewhere else.

## The rate sets a debt's cost, not its size

Paying down a debt avoids the interest you would otherwise pay, so it earns a return equal to the debt's rate: certain and immediate. Investing earns an [expected return](#term-expected-return) that is higher on average and not certain. Diversified developed-market shares have returned about 4 to 6% a year after inflation since 1900 (Dimson, Marsh and Staunton). A debt's rate includes inflation, so the like-for-like figure is the return before inflation: about 6 to 8% a year if inflation runs near 2%. The examples here use 7%.

For Eva, €500 extra on the mortgage avoids about €17 of interest in a year. The same €500 on the card avoids about €110. Size does not set the cost; the rate does.

A common way to read the rate, in low-inflation economies:

- **Above about 8 to 10% after tax** (most credit cards, many personal loans): many people clear it first, because the saving is both larger and certain.
- **Below about 4%** (many mortgages): investing has usually come out ahead on expected value, though the average has bad decades inside it.
- **Between 4 and 8%:** a judgement call, covered below.

Where inflation is higher, mortgages at 8 to 9% can be normal and the bands move up; the comparison is made against local inflation and returns.

## An employer match can be a 100% return on the matched euros

Where a workplace plan matches contributions, the [employer match](#term-employer-match) may add 50 cents or €1 for every euro contributed, up to a cap: an immediate 50% or 100% uplift before any market movement. It is not perfectly comparable with a card's yearly rate, because the matched money may be locked up or subject to vesting, but it is large enough that many people take the available match while also reducing the card. Capturing it means contributing at least the share of salary that triggers the full match: 6% of salary if the employer matches 100% up to 6%. In many US-style plans, match room not used during the plan year cannot be recovered later, while delaying a debt payment generally means another month of interest and, on a variable rate, a rate that may change.

That is why many people take the match early, alongside paying down high-interest debt, and some even before clearing a card. The limits: matched money is usually locked until retirement age, some matches only [vest](#term-vesting) after a period of service, and someone struggling with minimum payments may need the cash now. Elsewhere, employer contributions may be fixed by law or absent.

<details class="quick-check">
<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: before signing up, what four things does a match check ask?</summary>

Is there a match; are you contributing enough to capture all of it; when does it vest; and when can you reach the money? A match that vests only after years of service, or that locks money away when cash is tight, changes the comparison with paying down a card.

</details>

## How tax relief changes a debt's rate

Where interest on a debt is tax-deductible, most often a mortgage, the [effective rate](#term-effective-interest-rate) is the rate times (1 minus your [marginal tax rate](#term-marginal-tax-rate)). A 4% mortgage with interest deductible at 30% costs about 2.8% after tax. The like-for-like comparison is the after-tax debt rate against the after-tax investment return: investments in a taxable account are taxed too, while those in a tax-advantaged account mostly are not. Deduction rules change and have been capped or removed in many countries, so many people check that one applies before counting on it; it usually shifts a decision by a point or two, not more.

## A common order for several debts

Most households have several debts at once. A common default order, after minimum payments on everything, has seven steps:

1. **A starter emergency fund** of about one month of costs, so a surprise does not create new card debt.
2. **High-interest debt,** roughly above 8 to 10% after tax in low-inflation economies.
3. **The employer match,** often taken alongside step 2 or before it.
4. **A full [emergency fund](/blog/emergency-fund/),** commonly three to six months of costs.
5. **Tax-advantaged investing,** up to the yearly limit or what is realistic (the [tax-advantaged accounts post](/blog/tax-advantaged-accounts/) covers the categories).
6. **Remaining debt by rate, highest first:** the [avalanche method](#term-avalanche-method) from the [get out of debt post](/blog/how-to-get-out-of-debt/).
7. **Taxable investing** with what is left.

It is a default, not a rule. Income-based student loan repayment, a purchase in the next year or two, unstable income, high local inflation or a strong wish to be debt-free can all justify another order. Its value is having a deliberate priority rather than paying whichever balance feels most urgent.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">A household works through the order</summary>

A two-earner household on €80,000 combined has €1,200 a month to redirect once payroll contributions for the match have started. They have a €4,000 card at 22% (minimum €120), an €18,000 car loan at 5.5%, a €280,000 mortgage at 3.4% (about 2.6% after a partial interest deduction), a 100% match on the first 4% of salary that neither uses, and no emergency fund.

- They start the match on day one: 4% of €80,000 is €3,200 a year, matched with €3,200, deducted from payroll before the €1,200.
- About three months of €1,200 build the €3,500 starter fund.
- The €1,200 then clears the card in three to four months, earning a certain 22% on every euro.
- The €1,200 plus the freed €120 builds the full buffer, about five months of costs (€17,500), over roughly 11 months.
- Retirement contributions rise past the match, then the car loan (5.5%, in the judgement band) comes next by rate; some would split cash between it and investing.
- With no consumer debt left, extra cash can go to taxable investing or, for the debt-averse, extra mortgage payments: investing has the higher expected value, prepaying the certain return, and preference decides.

Some steps run in parallel; the order sets where the main flow of cash goes.

</details>

## In the 4 to 8% band, either choice is defensible

Overpaying a 6.5% car loan saves 6.5% on every overpaid euro, for certain. Diversified shares have averaged about 7% a year, with bad years inside that average. That is close enough for either choice to be reasonable. Common tie-breakers are certainty against expected return, then tax treatment, how much risk the household can carry, and peace of mind.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">When peace of mind outweighs the arithmetic</summary>

Sometimes the arithmetic says invest and a household prepays debt anyway, and that is not always wrong. A paid-off mortgage can make a lower-paid job you would love, fewer hours with a young child, or a spell without income easier to face. Reducing debt against a home that holds most of your net worth reduces leverage. In the five to ten years before retirement, a bad run of markets hurts more while debt is outstanding (the [financial independence post](/blog/introduction-to-financial-independence/) covers sequence risk), so some households shift toward paying debt down. And fewer moving parts has value for someone who dislikes complexity. Knowing what the arithmetic says first makes any departure a deliberate choice.

</details>

## The traps that cost the most

The [mental accounting post](/blog/mental-accounting/) covered how people sort money into mental boxes. Two mirror-image traps follow. **Paying the biggest debt first:** sending spare cash to a 3.4% mortgage because it feels heavier, while a 22% card keeps compounding. **Investing while a card revolves:** 22% a year is certain; 7% is a long-run average with large swings, so investing while carrying a 22% card is very likely to lose money against clearing it.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">The debt-free-first school, and the "safe" mortgage overpayment</summary>

**Extra mortgage payments feel safe.** Reducing a large obligation does lower risk, but the return is limited to the mortgage rate. Where mortgage rates are low, tax-advantaged retirement investing has a higher expected return; where mortgage rates are high relative to expected returns, the comparison can flip. The useful question is how prepaying compares with the next-best use of the same money, including its certainty.

**Debt-free first.** A popular school of advice clears all debt before any investing beyond a small buffer, whatever the rate. It is behaviourally powerful for households in a debt spiral, where visible progress changes behaviour; for households whose discipline is not the constraint, it costs real money. Which applies depends on the household.

</details>

## How the rules differ by country

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Debts and deductions in the US, UK, eurozone, India, Australia and Canada</summary>

- **US.** The mortgage-interest deduction is capped but real for eligible households. Federal student loans have income-driven repayment and forgiveness plans that change the case for paying them off fast. Home-equity line interest may be deductible in specific uses.
- **UK.** Mortgage-interest relief is largely gone for homes you live in, and restricted for buy-to-let. Student loans work much like an income-based graduate tax with a write-off after a period; many borrowers never repay in full, so extra payments can go to a balance that would have been written off, depending on the plan and expected earnings.
- **Eurozone.** Mortgage-interest deductibility varies widely by country. Consumer credit is usually cheaper than in the US.
- **India.** Home-loan and education-loan interest are deductible within limits under specific sections. Card rates of 30 to 42% a year are common, which is why many treat clearing them as an early priority. Home loans at 8 to 9% are normal and are judged against India's higher inflation.
- **Australia.** Interest is not deductible on a home you live in, but is on investment property. HECS-HELP student loans are indexed rather than charged interest and repaid by income, so extra repayments are often weighed against other uses of the money.
- **Canada.** Interest is not deductible on a primary home, but is on investment property. Structures such as the Smith Manoeuvre turn non-deductible mortgage interest into deductible investment-loan interest for those who use them.

</details>
