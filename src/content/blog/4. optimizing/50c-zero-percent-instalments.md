Jiri, 38, needs a €1,200 laptop. The shop offers twelve payments of €100 at 0%, or €1,140 if he pays now. The instalments look free, and the payment fits his month easily. They are not free: paying twelve €100 instalments at the end of each month instead of €1,140 now costs him €60, about 10% a year on the money he is effectively borrowing.

The [how much can you borrow post](/blog/how-much-can-you-borrow/) looked at large loans and the checks lenders run. Depending on the country and provider, small instalment plans may involve lighter checks than large loans, so the arithmetic is still worth doing yourself.

## When it is free, someone else pays

Money borrowed for a year costs someone something. When a plan charges you nothing, the cost lands elsewhere, and where it lands decides whether the plan is free for you.

- **The shop pays a [merchant fee](#term-merchant-fee).** The instalment provider or bank pays the shop the full price, less a fee. Shops accept that because instalments sell more. If the price is the same as for paying in full, the plan can be genuinely free for you.
- **The price is higher.** The fee is built into the price for everyone, or into the instalment price only. In Mexico, the consumer finance regulator Condusef warns that the price for "meses sin intereses" can be higher than the [cash price](#term-cash-price), and that discounts for paying cash or by debit do not apply.
- **You give up a discount.** That is Jiri's case. Paying €100 a month for a year instead of €1,140 now works like a loan of €1,140 at about 10% a year. Brazil allows exactly this split: since 2017 a shop may set a lower price for paying now than for "parcelado sem juros", as long as it shows the discount.

## The costs that turn free into credit

Even when the price is the same, several costs can turn a free plan into an expensive one:

- **Processing fees and tax on interest.** With an Indian [no-cost EMI](#term-no-cost-emi), the bank usually still charges interest and the shop gives an equal discount. The cardholder still pays 18% GST on that interest, and often a processing fee. On €1,200 over a year at 15% interest, the GST alone is about €18; a 1% processing fee with its GST adds about €14. The Reserve Bank of India said in 2013 that in zero percent EMI schemes the interest is often passed on to customers as a processing fee.
- **[Deferred interest](#term-deferred-interest).** Some store cards charge nothing if the whole balance is paid by a deadline, and interest from the purchase date if it is not. Paying €90 a month on €1,200 leaves €120 after a year; at 25% a year, the interest charged back to the first day is about €176, more than the balance left.
- **Late fees and lost status.** A missed payment can bring a fee, and on some cards turns an interest-free instalment into an interest-bearing one, as some Korean card terms do.
- **Payments that fail.** Instalments taken automatically from a card or account can fail on a thin month, and a failed payment can trigger a fee, depending on the provider.
- **Several plans at once.** Small plans are easy to stack. In a US regulator's 2025 report, 63% of [buy now pay later](#term-buy-now-pay-later) borrowers had several loans running at the same time at some point in a year, and a third had them with several providers.
- **Refunds and returns.** A returned item can take time to cancel the plan, and payments may still fall due meanwhile.
- **Your credit record.** Some providers report to credit bureaus, some do not, and a later lender may still ask about them. The [credit scores post](/blog/credit-and-credit-scores/) covered how records are built.

## Paying later makes it easier to spend more

The arithmetic is only half of it. Several studies find that the option to pay later raises how much people buy:

- A US study of consumers' transaction data found that access to buy now pay later raised people's total spending, not only the timing of it (Di Maggio, Williams and Katz, NBER).
- A study of merchants found that offering it raised sales by about 20%, driven by customers with weaker credit (Keil, Berg, Burg and Puri, Journal of Financial Economics, 2025).
- In an experiment by the Central Bank of Ireland, participants spent on average 4.39% more when they could pay later than when they paid by debit card (2025).

The evidence has limits: the studies come from a few countries and a few years, and they do not show that every user spends more or that the effect is the same in every market. The mechanism is the one the [mental accounting post](/blog/mental-accounting/) described: €100 a month feels smaller than €1,200, even when it adds up to more.

## A check before choosing instalments

Questions many people ask before choosing a 0% plan:

1. **Is the price the same as for paying now?** If not, the difference is the cost.
2. **What is the total?** Every instalment, plus any processing fee, tax, insurance or account fee.
3. **What happens if one payment is late or fails?** A fee, interest back to day one, or the plan turning into an interest-bearing one.
4. **How many plans are already running?** Their payments land in the same month.
5. **Would I buy this, at this price, if I had to pay today?** The question the payment plan is designed to soften.

## What the rules say where you live

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Instalment plans in India, Mexico, Brazil, the EU, the UK, the US, Australia, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, South Korea and South Africa</summary>

- **India.** No-cost EMI is offered by banks, card issuers and lenders; GST at 18% applies to the interest and to any processing fee. The Reserve Bank of India's 2013 circular criticised zero percent EMI schemes that hid interest in processing fees.
- **Mexico.** "Meses sin intereses" are paid on top of the card's minimum payment; Condusef notes that the price for them can be higher than the cash price and that cash or debit discounts may not apply.
- **Brazil.** Shops may charge different prices for paying now and for "parcelado sem juros" (since 2017), and must show the discount.
- **European Union.** The revised Consumer Credit Directive applies from November 20, 2026 and brings most buy now pay later and interest-free credit under consumer credit rules, including a creditworthiness check, with narrow exemptions. National rules apply it.
- **United Kingdom.** Since July 15, 2026 the FCA has regulated interest-free buy now pay later repaid in up to 12 instalments within 12 months, where the lender and the shop are different businesses: lenders must check affordability and explain the terms, complaints can go to the Financial Ombudsman Service, and refund protection under section 75 applies. Plans offered directly by the shop itself are outside these rules.
- **United States.** Buy now pay later lenders are not required to report to credit bureaus. Deferred-interest store cards charge interest back to the purchase date if a balance remains at the deadline.
- **Australia.** Buy now pay later has been regulated as credit since June 10, 2025: providers need a credit licence, follow lending and hardship rules, and fees for missed payments are capped.
- **Indonesia.** The financial regulator OJK requires paylater users of finance companies to be at least 18 or married and to earn at least Rp3 million a month, for new customers and renewals by January 1, 2027 at the latest.
- **Saudi Arabia.** The Saudi Central Bank licenses buy now pay later providers; the main providers describe their plans as interest-free and Sharia-compliant.
- **Turkey.** The banking regulator limits how many instalments a card purchase can be split into, by type of purchase, and has cut those limits in the past.
- **South Korea.** Card companies offer interest-free instalments as promotions; in "partial" plans the cardholder pays the first months' fees, and a late instalment can switch the plan to the interest-bearing rate.
- **South Africa.** Whether buy now pay later counts as credit under the National Credit Act is unsettled, so its users may not have the act's protections, such as affordability checks.

Elsewhere, the questions above work the same way, whatever the local name for the plan.

</details>
