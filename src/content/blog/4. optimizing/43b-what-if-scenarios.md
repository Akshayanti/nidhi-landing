Eva took a €4,000 holiday last year and felt guilty about it for months. The same year she moved to a nicer flat that costs €150 a month more, and has not thought about it since.

Her projection has an opinion about which of those mattered. Over 30 years, invested at 6% real growth instead of spent, the holiday would have become about €23,000. The rent would have become about €146,000. The decision she worried about was the small one.

That comparison is a what-if scenario. The [post on financial projections](/blog/financial-projections/) built the baseline: today's numbers, played forward under a range of growth assumptions. This post uses that baseline to test decisions before you make them.

## The baseline and the delta

A what-if scenario is your projection with one input changed to answer a specific question. Your baseline uses your current inputs. The scenario asks: what would the trajectory look like if this one thing were different?

The value is not the ending number in the alternate scenario. Ending numbers in year 30 are always uncertain. The value is the **delta**: how much this specific change moves the trajectory compared with the baseline. That delta is what makes the tradeoff visible, and it holds up better than either ending number, because both runs share the same uncertain assumptions.

## The five inputs that move the most

Most scenarios revolve around five inputs. Getting these right captures most of what scenario analysis can tell you.

**Monthly savings.** Often the most sensitive input you control. €200 a month more for 30 years at 6% real growth is worth roughly €195,000 in ending balance. €500 a month is worth roughly €487,000. This is compounding on recurring contributions, and it is much larger than most people expect.

**The growth assumption.** The projections post showed that two percentage points of growth add roughly half again to a 30-year result. Scenarios often reveal that the decision depends more on which growth rate you assumed than on the decision itself, which is why every scenario should be run at a low and a middle rate, not only one.

**A large one-off event.** Buying a house. A wedding. A child's university. A career break. These shift the trajectory at a specific year. Model the event as a one-off at the right year, not as a recurring cost, and look at how the line recovers afterwards, or does not.

**The retirement date.** Retiring at 55, 60 or 65 changes both when contributions stop and when withdrawals start. A five-year difference is usually much larger than intuition suggests, because those are often the highest-contribution and highest-compounding years of the whole plan.

**Where you live.** Moving countries changes your cost of living, your currency, your tax treatment, and sometimes which tax-advantaged accounts you can use. "Location" is really a bundle of inputs, which makes it one of the most valuable scenarios to run, and one of the easiest to get wrong. A later post in this series treats it in depth.

Smaller inputs (a €30 subscription, a modest side income) are worth modelling for this year's cash flow, but rarely change the 30-year picture much. Spend scenario time on the five above.

## Change one input at a time

The most common mistake in scenario work is changing three things at once and looking at the combined result. That answers "should I make this whole life change?" but says nothing about which part of the change matters.

Say you are thinking about moving from Berlin to Lisbon, taking a 10% pay cut, and keeping the same savings rate as a share of pay. That is three inputs moving together. Run them separately: the pay cut alone, Lisbon's cost of living with your current pay, and then both together. Compare the three deltas. Usually one effect dominates; sometimes they cancel; occasionally they compound. You cannot tell without pulling them apart.

## The intuition traps

Some scenario results surprise people every time.

**Recurring changes are much bigger than they look.** €150 a month (a nicer flat, a car payment, higher childcare) over 30 years at 6% real growth is worth around €146,000 in ending balance. Asked to guess first, most people say something nearer €50,000.

**One-off changes are much smaller than they feel.** Eva's €4,000 holiday is worth roughly €23,000 at year 30, invested instead of spent. Real money, but dwarfed by the €146,000 the rent costs. Guilt tracks how noticeable a purchase is; the arithmetic tracks how often it repeats. The [mental accounting post](/blog/mental-accounting/) covers why the two come apart.

**Time is asymmetric.** The projections post showed the cost of small delays. It applies to every scenario: a €200 monthly increase started five years late produces roughly 30% less by year 30, because the years you lose are the ones with the most time to compound. That is why starting small early often comes out closer to starting big late than people expect.

## Run the bad case first

A plan that only works in the base case is a fragile plan. A plan that survives a bad scenario is one you can actually commit to.

A practical order: baseline first (your current numbers, the middle growth rate), then the downside (low growth, lower income, an unplanned large expense), then the upside (higher growth, a promotion, side income). If the downside still lands somewhere acceptable, the plan is robust. If it does not, that is useful information about the plan, and arguing with the assumptions until the downside looks better hides it. What counts as acceptable depends on your buffers, your job security and who depends on you.

Many people run the upside first because it feels good. The upside is pleasant to see, but it rarely changes a decision. The downside tells you whether you can sleep.

## Getting started

- **Write down the decision you are actually facing.** A job offer, a move, a bigger home, a higher savings rate. One decision, stated as a change to one input.
- **Run it against your baseline at a low and a middle growth rate.** Note the delta, not the ending balance.
- **If the decision bundles several changes, split them.** Run each one alone, then together.
- **Run the bad case before you decide.** Ask whether you could live with it. If not, that is worth knowing before the decision, not after.

A scenario is decision support before the decision, not a justification after it. Used that way, it turns "can we afford this?" into a number you can look at, and it moves your attention from the purchases you feel bad about to the ones that repeat.
