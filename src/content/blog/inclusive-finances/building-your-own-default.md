Eva sits down for an hour and writes one line for each thing her plan takes for granted. "My partner inherits my share of the home": no, they are not married and have no wills. "My pension pays my partner if I die": unknown, she has never read the scheme rules. "Someone could pay my bills if I were in hospital": no, nobody has authority. None of these was ever decided. They were simply assumed.

This series opened with [two households with identical money](/blog/when-the-default-plan-does-not-fit-you/) and different outcomes, and three questions: what is this assuming about me, does that hold, and if not, what do I put in its place on purpose? Since then it has applied them to shared homes, work, borrowing, borders, living alone, partnership, separation, care, disability, recognition, friendship, parents, stepfamilies and loss. This closing post hands the method over: a map of the assumptions, and a practice for keeping them checked.

## Six families of assumption most financial plans rest on

Looked at together, the [default assumptions](#term-default-assumption) in this series fall into six families, and a plan can rely on several of them without stating them:

| Family | What a standard plan assumes | Where this series tested it |
|---|---|---|
| Relationship | You are in a partnership the law recognises, or single with relatives to fall back on | [Unmarried couples](/blog/unmarried-and-cohabiting-couples/), [unrecognised marriages](/blog/when-your-marriage-is-not-recognised-abroad/), [more than two partners](/blog/multi-partner-households/), [separation](/blog/divorce-and-separation-finances/), [a partner's death](/blog/widowhood-and-sudden-single-income/) |
| Family | The people who matter are your legal relatives, and children have two legal parents in one home | [Chosen family](/blog/chosen-family-and-next-of-kin/), [blended families](/blog/blended-and-non-traditional-families/), [households of friends](/blog/shared-households-money-for-three-or-more/), [homes owned by a group](/blog/owning-a-home-with-more-than-two-people/), [households across generations](/blog/multi-generational-household-economics/) |
| Income | An employer pays you regularly, withholds tax, funds a pension, and your working record has no gaps | [Gig and informal work](/blog/gig-and-informal-economy-work/), [caregiving](/blog/caregiving-and-the-career-break-wealth-gap/), [one income](/blog/solo-agers-and-single-income-households/) |
| Country | Your money, legal status and history are all in one place | [Moving countries](/blog/immigrants-expats-and-cross-border-households/), [unrecognised marriages](/blog/when-your-marriage-is-not-recognised-abroad/) |
| Health | You can work, make your own decisions and do not rely on means-tested support | [Disability](/blog/financial-planning-with-a-disability/), [one income](/blog/solo-agers-and-single-income-households/) |
| Belief | Products built on interest are acceptable to you | [Interest-free finance](/blog/interest-free-and-sharia-compliant-finance/) |

Many people sit outside at least one row. The International Labour Organization estimates that most of the world's workers are in informal employment, without the payroll a standard plan assumes; one-person households are among the most common kinds in many cities; and living together without marrying is ordinary in many countries. The "default household" is the one the paperwork was written for, not necessarily the typical one. And the rows interact: an unmarried partner who is also a migrant on a dependent visa, a carer with a broken pension record who then separates, a disabled adult whose parents leave an inheritance the ordinary way. The hardest situations in this series were rarely one failed default; they were two at once.

## The series uses three kinds of response

Read across the posts, most responses used in the series fit three broad groups:

- **A [designation](#term-designation):** a will, a beneficiary form, a power of attorney or an agreement. A pension scheme may treat its form as binding or only as a guide, and its survivor pension may follow different rules from a lump sum. Most designations are cheap, and several are free.
- **A structure:** a different way of holding money so a rule does not bite, such as joint ownership, a trust, a protected account, a pension in the carer's own name, or a holding account that turns uneven income into a salary.
- **A workaround,** for gaps no document closes: insurance sized to replace a benefit you are excluded from, a larger reserve standing in for a safety net you lack, assets kept where your status is recognised.

Any new situation is likely to be some mix of the three. That is the portable part.

## An assumption register keeps your plan checked over time

The [financial dashboard post](/blog/financial-dashboard/) described a few numbers worth tracking; an [assumption register](#term-assumption-register) is its companion for things that are not numbers. It is one page, with five parts to each line:

| Assumption | True today? | How I know | If it fails | Last checked |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| My partner inherits my share of the home | No: we are not married and have no wills | Looked up the inheritance rules | Will, plus owning the home jointly | This month |
| My pension pays my partner if I die | Unknown | Have not read the scheme rules | Beneficiary form, and whether the scheme treats it as binding; life cover if it refuses or only treats the form as a guide | Never |
| My employer provides my health cover | Yes | Contract | Cost of a policy in my own name is €210 a month | January |
| Someone could pay my bills if I were in hospital | No | Nobody has authority | Financial power of attorney, naming my sister | Never |
| My credit history is visible to lenders here | Partly: two years | Requested my credit file | One card, paid in full monthly | March |
| My income arrives every month | Yes | Employed, permanent contract | Six months of expenses held in cash | January |

These are illustrative entries, not universal fixes: whether a will, joint ownership, a beneficiary form, a power of attorney, insurance or a particular cash reserve has the intended result depends on local law, provider terms and the household's circumstances. "Unknown" is a valid answer and the most useful one, because it is the next thing to find out. The fourth column is the plan; it need not be in place yet, and writing down what would replace a default is most of the way to arranging it. Ten to fifteen lines are enough for many households.

A register is built by going through the six families in turn. **Relationship:** who inherits, who each pension and policy pays, who decides for me? **Family:** who would look after my children and manage their money, and who would the law call? **Income:** what stops if my job stops, and what if I could not work for a year? **Country:** is my status, or my partner's, tied to a job, and would my documents work where I live? **Health:** who acts if I cannot, and does any support I receive depend on what I earn or own? **Belief:** is there anything in my accounts I would not hold if I looked?

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">When to review it: yearly, and after life events</summary>

The [anti-bias post](/blog/building-an-anti-bias-financial-life/) argued for scheduled reviews over decisions made in the moment, and the register belongs in that schedule. Once a year, reading down the page finds the one line that has changed among the many that have not. Straight away after any of these: a new relationship, a marriage, or the end of either; a birth, an adoption, a new stepchild; a death in the family; a new job, a redundancy or a move to self-employment; a move to another country; a new diagnosis, for you or someone you support; an inheritance or other large sum. These are the moments a default changes without announcing it.

</details>

## Defaults drift, so this matters even if they fit you today

A reader who is married, employed, healthy and living where they were born might think none of this was written for them. Two reasons it was. **Standard households have unchecked assumptions too:** an out-of-date beneficiary form, no will, no power of attorney, one person who knows where everything is and has never written it down. **Defaults drift:** few people stay the default household for life. A marriage ends, a partner dies, a job goes, a posting abroad resets a credit file, an illness changes what someone can earn. Each crosses from one side of this series to the other, often in a week. A plan checked only against the default works until the day it is needed.

<details class="quick-check">
<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: which line in a register is the most useful one to find?</summary>

An "Unknown". It marks an assumption nobody has checked, and it is the next thing to find out.

</details>

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">What travels between countries, and what does not</summary>

**The categories travel; the specifics do not.** A will, a beneficiary form, a power of attorney, a trust, a protected account: some version of each exists almost everywhere, but what it is called, what it can do and how it must be signed is local. **Inheritance freedom, relationship recognition and the tax unit** differ most between countries and matter most to these plans, so they are the first to check after a move. **Rules change:** recognition of relationships, benefit thresholds and pension credits have shifted in many countries in the past decade and will again, which is what the "last checked" column is for.

</details>

A default is someone else's guess about your life, often made long before you were born and written into law. For a long time it may be close enough. The work of this series, and of any good financial plan, is to find out where it is not, and to put your own decision there.
