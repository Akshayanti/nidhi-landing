The last post of the main curriculum, The Complete Picture, draws the whole journey as one connected system: net worth, cash flow, investing, projections, independence, retirement, and what you leave behind. It ends on the idea that revisiting a plan every year matters more than getting it perfect once.

That system has a layer underneath it that a plan rarely shows. Every calculation rests on things assumed to be true. That a spouse inherits. That an employer exists. That a credit history counts. That someone will step in.

This series has been about that layer.

It opened with [two households with identical money](/blog/when-the-default-plan-does-not-fit-you/) and different outcomes, and the three questions that explain the difference: what is this assuming about me, does that hold, and if not, what do I put in its place on purpose.

Since then the questions have been applied to shared homes, work, borrowing, borders, living alone, partnership, separation, care, disability, recognition, friendship, parents, stepfamilies, and loss.

This post hands the method over. It has two parts: a map of the assumptions, and a practice for keeping them checked.

## Part one: the map

Looked at together, the defaults in this series fall into six families. Almost every financial plan rests on all six, mostly without saying so.

| Family | What a standard plan assumes | Where this series tested it |
|---|---|---|
| Relationship | You are in a partnership the law recognises, or you are single with relatives to fall back on | [Unmarried couples](/blog/unmarried-and-cohabiting-couples/), [unrecognised marriages](/blog/when-your-marriage-is-not-recognised-abroad/), [more than two partners](/blog/multi-partner-households/), [separation](/blog/divorce-and-separation-finances/), [a partner's death](/blog/widowhood-and-sudden-single-income/) |
| Family | The people who matter to you are your legal relatives, and children have two legal parents in one home | [Chosen family](/blog/chosen-family-and-next-of-kin/), [blended families](/blog/blended-and-non-traditional-families/), [households of friends](/blog/shared-households-money-for-three-or-more/), [homes owned by a group](/blog/owning-a-home-with-more-than-two-people/), [households across generations](/blog/multi-generational-household-economics/) |
| Income | An employer pays you regularly, withholds tax, funds a pension, and your working record has no gaps | [Gig and informal work](/blog/gig-and-informal-economy-work/), [caregiving](/blog/caregiving-and-the-career-break-wealth-gap/), [one income](/blog/solo-agers-and-single-income-households/) |
| Country | Your money, your legal status, and your history are all in one place | [Moving countries](/blog/immigrants-expats-and-cross-border-households/), [unrecognised marriages](/blog/when-your-marriage-is-not-recognised-abroad/) |
| Health | You can work, make your own decisions, and do not rely on means-tested support | [Disability](/blog/financial-planning-with-a-disability/), [one income](/blog/solo-agers-and-single-income-households/) |
| Belief | Products built on interest are acceptable to you | [Interest-free finance](/blog/interest-free-and-sharia-compliant-finance/) |

Two things stand out.

**Most people sit outside at least one row.** The International Labour Organization estimates that most of the world's workers are in informal employment, without the employer-run payroll a standard plan assumes. One-person households are the most common kind in many cities. Cohabiting without marrying is normal. The "default household" is the one the paperwork was written for. It is not the typical one.

**The rows interact.** A gap in one makes a gap in another worse. An unmarried partner who is also a migrant on a dependent visa. A carer with a broken pension record who then separates. A disabled adult whose parents leave an inheritance the ordinary way. The hardest situations in this series were never one failed default. They were two at once.

### The pattern in every fix

Read across the posts and the repairs come in only three kinds.

**A designation.** A document that replaces the default with a choice: a will, a beneficiary form, a power of attorney, an agreement. Most are cheap. Several are free.

**A structure.** A different way of holding money so that a rule does not bite: joint ownership, a trust, a protected account, a pension in the carer's own name, a holding account that turns uneven income into a salary.

**A workaround.** For the gaps no document closes: insurance sized to replace a benefit you are excluded from, a larger reserve to stand in for a safety net you lack, assets kept where your status is recognised.

Every new situation you meet will be some mix of the three. That is the portable part.

## Part two: the practice

### The assumption register

The post on the [financial dashboard](/blog/financial-dashboard/) described a small set of numbers worth tracking and how often to look at them. This is its companion for things that are not numbers.

An **assumption register** is one page. Each line has five parts.

| Assumption | True today? | How I know | If it fails | Last checked |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| My partner inherits my share of the home | No: we are not married and have no wills | Looked up the inheritance rules | Will, plus owning the home jointly | This month |
| My pension pays my partner if I die | Unknown | Have not read the scheme rules | Beneficiary form; life cover if the scheme refuses | Never |
| My employer provides my health cover | Yes | Contract | Cost of a policy in my own name is €210 a month | January |
| Someone could pay my bills if I were in hospital | No | Nobody has authority | Financial power of attorney, naming my sister | Never |
| My credit history is visible to lenders here | Partly: two years | Requested my credit file | One card, paid in full monthly | March |
| My income arrives every month | Yes | Employed, permanent contract | Six months of expenses held in cash | January |

Three things make a register work.

**"Unknown" is a valid answer and the most useful one.** A line you cannot answer is the next thing to find out.

**The fourth column is the plan.** It does not have to be in place yet. Writing down what would replace a default is most of the distance to arranging it.

**It is short.** Ten to fifteen lines cover most households.

### Building yours

Go through the six families in order and write down what you are relying on in each.

- **Relationship.** Who inherits? Who does each pension and policy pay? Who decides for me?
- **Family.** Who would look after my children, and who would manage their money? Who would the law call?
- **Income.** What stops if my job stops? What if I could not work for a year?
- **Country.** Is my status, or my partner's, tied to a job? Would my documents work where I live?
- **Health.** Who acts if I cannot? Does any support I receive depend on what I earn or own?
- **Belief.** Is there anything in my accounts I would not hold if I looked?

The one-hour audit in the first post of this series produces most of the answers.

### The annual review, and the life-event review

The post on [building an anti-bias financial life](/blog/building-an-anti-bias-financial-life/) argued for scheduled reviews over decisions made in the moment. The register belongs in that schedule.

**Once a year,** read down the page. Most lines will not have changed. You are looking for the one that has.

**After any of these, straight away:**

- A new relationship, a marriage, or the end of either
- A birth, an adoption, a new stepchild
- A death in the family
- A new job, a redundancy, or a move to self-employment
- A move to another country
- A new diagnosis, for you or someone you support
- An inheritance or other large sum

These are the moments at which a default changes without announcing it.

### Why this is for everyone

A reader who is married, employed, healthy, and living where they were born might think none of this was written for them.

Two reasons it was.

**Standard households have unchecked assumptions too.** A beneficiary form naming a former partner. No will. No power of attorney. One person who knows where everything is and has never written it down. None of those depends on being unusual.

**Defaults drift.** Almost nobody stays the default household for life. A marriage ends and you are single with children. A partner dies and you are a household of one. A job goes and you are freelance. A posting abroad resets your credit file. An illness changes what you can earn. In each case you cross from one side of this series to the other, often in a week, and usually at the moment you have least attention to spare.

A plan that was only ever checked against the default works until the day it is needed.

## Cross-continent notes

This series kept returning to one caution, and it bears repeating at the end.

**The categories travel. The specifics do not.** A will, a beneficiary form, a power of attorney, a trust, a protected account: some version of each exists almost everywhere. What each is called, what it can do, and how it must be signed is local.

**Inheritance freedom, relationship recognition, and the tax unit** are the three things that differ most between countries and matter most to these plans. They are the first to check after any move.

**Rules change.** Recognition of relationships, benefit thresholds, and pension credits have all shifted in the past decade in many countries and will again. The "last checked" column exists for this reason.

## Getting started

- **Write the register.** One page, six families, an hour.
- **Mark the unknowns.** Pick the one that worries you most and find the answer this week.
- **Do the free fixes.** Beneficiary forms. The list of accounts. An emergency contact on your phone.
- **Consider one paid fix.** For many people it is a will or a power of attorney, in whatever form local law requires.
- **Put a date in the calendar** to read the page again in a year.

A default is someone else's guess about your life, made before you were born and written into law. For a long time it may be close enough. The work of this series, and of any good financial plan, is to find out where it is not, and to put your own decision there.
