The Mastery post on pension income weighs a choice that many people face at retirement: take a lump sum, take a lifetime income, or take some of each. It treats the question as a trade between flexibility and security.

For a disabled person who receives means-tested support, that same choice has a third dimension the post never needed to mention. A lump sum is savings. Savings above a limit can end a benefit. The "flexible" option may switch off the income it was supposed to sit beside.

That is one example of something that runs through the whole of personal finance. Advice written for people who are not on means-tested support can do harm to people who are. Earn more. Save more. Build an emergency fund. Accept the inheritance. Each is sound in general and each can backfire.

The World Health Organization estimates that about one in six people worldwide lives with a significant disability. This is not a niche situation.

This post has two parts: how the cliff works and how to check for it, and the structures that let a disabled person build security anyway. It does not give thresholds. They differ by country, region, and benefit, and they change. It teaches what to ask.

## Part one: the benefit cliff

### Three kinds of support

Support for disabled people usually comes in three forms, and they behave differently.

- **Contribution-based.** You qualify because you paid into a system while working. What you earn or own afterwards usually does not matter.
- **Needs-based, not means-tested.** You qualify because of the effect of your condition, assessed medically or functionally. Income and savings are usually ignored.
- **Means-tested.** You qualify only if your income, your savings, or both are below set limits. **Means-tested** simply means that your own resources are counted before support is given.

Most people receive a mix. Only the third kind reacts to money. The first task is knowing which of your benefits is which.

### How a raise can make you poorer

Here is an invented but realistic example. The numbers are made up to show the mechanism and are not any country's rules.

Daniel receives a means-tested benefit of €900 a month. The rules let him earn up to €500 a month from work and keep the benefit in full. Above €500, the benefit stops.

| | Earnings | Benefit | Total monthly income |
|---|---|---|---|
| Working 10 hours a week | €500 | €900 | €1,400 |
| Working 12 hours a week | €600 | €0 | €600 |

Daniel took two more hours of work, earned €100 more, and is **€800 a month worse off**.

To get back to €1,400 from work alone he would need about 28 hours a week, nearly three times what he started with, which his condition may not allow.

This is a **benefit cliff**: a point where a small gain in income or savings causes a larger loss of support.

Not every system works this way. Some use a **taper**, where the benefit falls gradually, for example by 60 cents for every extra euro earned. A taper is gentler, but where several benefits taper at once the combined effect can still take most of each extra euro.

### The savings cliff

Income is one trigger. Savings are the other, and the one people see coming less often.

Many means-tested benefits have a limit on **capital**: savings, investments, and sometimes other assets. Go over it and eligibility ends until the money is spent down.

Events that can push someone over:

- An inheritance, even a modest one
- A gift from a parent or grandparent
- A compensation or insurance payment
- A back-payment of benefits that were owed
- A pension lump sum
- An emergency fund built up carefully, exactly as every finance article recommends

The last one is the cruellest. The post on the [emergency fund](/blog/emergency-fund/) gave three to six months of expenses as a common guideline. For someone under a capital limit, that guideline may be more than the rules allow them to hold.

### Passport benefits

One benefit often unlocks others: health cover, housing support, help with energy bills, free transport, a care package. Losing the first can close the door on the rest.

When working out what is at stake, count everything that depends on eligibility, not just the payment itself.

### The three-question check

Before any change (a job, more hours, a gift, an inheritance, a new savings habit) ask three questions of the agency that pays the benefit, or of a welfare rights adviser.

**1. What counts as income?**
Wages, certainly. But also gifts, help from family with bills, rental income, a partner's earnings? Many systems assess a couple together, so a partner's income can end an individual's benefit. Are some earnings disregarded?

**2. What counts as savings?**
Cash and investments usually do. A main home often does not. What about a car, a pension not yet drawn, money held in trust, a dedicated disability savings account?

**3. What happens at the limit?**
Is it a cliff or a taper? Is there a grace period or a trial period for starting work? If eligibility ends, how quickly can it be restored if the job does not work out?

Get the answers in writing where possible. Then do the arithmetic before the change, not after.

The aim of the check is to make work and saving possible. Many systems have work incentives, disregards, and trial periods built in, and people miss them because they assume any earnings are dangerous. The check finds the room that exists as well as the edge.

## Part two: building security anyway

Staying below every limit for life is not a plan. These are the structures that make more possible.

### Protected savings accounts

Some countries offer a savings account designed for disabled people. Money in it, up to a limit, is ignored when benefits are means-tested, and growth is often tax-free. The ABLE account in the United States is one example. Canada's Registered Disability Savings Plan is another, and adds government contributions.

Where such an account exists, many people use it first for savings, an emergency fund, and gifts from family, within its limits.

Where it does not, the answer to question two above shows which kinds of asset are exempt, and that list becomes the savings plan.

### Trusts

A **trust** is an arrangement where one or more people (the trustees) hold and manage money for the benefit of someone else (the beneficiary). The money legally belongs to the trust, not to the beneficiary.

A **special-needs trust**, called by different names in different places, is a trust written so that the money in it is not counted as the disabled person's own for means-testing. Trustees spend it on things that improve the person's life and that benefits do not cover: equipment, therapies, travel, a better wheelchair than the standard issue.

The design matters. If the beneficiary can demand the money, it is usually counted as theirs. These trusts generally give trustees discretion over what is paid and when.

This is a job for a specialist lawyer. A trust written badly can fail at the one thing it was for.

### Inheritance, done the right way round

This is the section for parents, grandparents, and siblings.

A parent who leaves €50,000 directly to a disabled adult child on means-tested support may achieve this: the child's benefits stop, the inheritance is spent on living costs that the benefits used to cover, and when it runs out the child reapplies, no better off. The gift replaced support it was meant to add to.

The same €50,000 left to a suitable trust can pay for extras for many years while support continues.

Three steps families commonly take, after checking local rules with a specialist:

- **Leaving money to the structure, not to the person.** The trust or the protected account is named in the will and on beneficiary forms.
- **Telling the rest of the family.** A generous grandparent who leaves a direct bequest can undo the plan. So can a well-meaning aunt.
- **Writing a letter of intent.** A plain document describing the person's routines, preferences, medical needs, and the people in their life. It has no legal force and it is what a future trustee or carer will rely on most.

Families often fund the trust with life insurance on the parents, so the money arrives when their own care stops.

### Help with decisions, without losing control

Some disabled people need support to manage money or make decisions. There is a range of ways to provide it.

**Supported decision-making.** The person keeps the legal right to decide and chooses trusted supporters to help them understand options and communicate choices. Many countries now recognise this formally.

**A power of attorney.** The person appoints someone to act for them, and can usually cancel it.

**Guardianship or conservatorship.** A court transfers decision-making power to someone else. It is sometimes necessary. It also removes rights that can be hard to get back.

Many disability advocates, and the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, favour trying the less restrictive options first. Practical tools help too: a benefit paid to an appointed person, a bank account with a second authorised user, automatic payments for bills.

### The other half of the budget: extra costs

Disability changes spending as well as income. Equipment, adapted transport, higher heating and energy use, specialist food, personal assistance, and insurance all cost more. Research by disability organisations in several countries, such as Scope's work in the UK, has found these extra costs can run to hundreds of euros or pounds a month, varying widely by condition.

Two consequences for planning:

- **Benchmarks need adjusting.** A rule such as "housing should be about a third of income" assumes the other two thirds are free for ordinary living. When a fixed slice goes on disability costs first, the real budget is smaller.
- **Extra-cost benefits are usually separate and often not means-tested.** They are meant to cover these costs and are frequently under-claimed.

The post on [insurance basics](/blog/insurance-basics/) covers the cover everyone needs. For a disabled person, buying new health, life, or income protection insurance can be difficult or expensive. Group schemes through an employer, which often accept members without medical questions, are valuable for that reason, and it is why many people keep existing policies going.

## If you are disabled and on one income

The post on [one-income households](/blog/solo-agers-and-single-income-households/) argued for a larger emergency fund and for naming a backup person in writing. Both still apply, with one change: to avoid affecting benefits, the fund needs to sit somewhere the means test ignores. A protected account or an exempt asset does the job an ordinary savings account cannot.

## Cross-continent notes

**United States.** Strict asset limits apply to the main means-tested programmes. ABLE accounts and special-needs trusts are the standard tools. Health cover is often tied to benefit eligibility, which raises the stakes.

**United Kingdom.** The main disability extra-cost benefit is not means-tested. Support for living costs is, with capital limits and a taper on earnings. Trusts for disabled people have their own tax rules.

**Canada.** The Registered Disability Savings Plan adds government grants and is largely disregarded by provincial means tests.

**Continental Europe.** Systems lean more on contribution-based and needs-based support, so cliffs are less common but still exist where social assistance tops income up. Many countries assess the household, not the individual.

**India and other lower-income countries.** Public support is limited and the family is the main safety net. Trust arrangements and guardianship provisions for disabled adults exist in law, and planning centres on who will manage money after the parents.

Everywhere, local disability organisations and welfare rights advisers know the rules better than any general source, and their advice is usually free.

## Getting started

- **Sort your benefits into the three kinds.** Only the means-tested ones react to money.
- **Ask the three questions.** Before any change to work, savings, or gifts.
- **Find out whether a protected account exists where you live,** and what its limits are.
- **If family might leave you money,** ask them to talk to a specialist before they write a will.
- **If you support a disabled relative,** write the letter of intent this year.

Good financial advice is advice that fits the rules you actually live under. For a disabled person on means-tested support, the first move is always to read the rules, and the second is to use the routes through them that already exist.
