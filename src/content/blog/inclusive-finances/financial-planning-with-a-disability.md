Daniel receives a [means-tested](#term-means-tested-benefit) benefit of €900 a month. The rules let him earn up to €500 a month and keep it in full; above €500, it stops. He takes two more hours of work a week:

| | Earnings | Benefit | Total monthly income |
|---|---|---|---|
| Working 10 hours a week | €500 | €900 | €1,400 |
| Working 12 hours a week | €600 | €0 | €600 |

He earned €100 more and is **€800 a month worse off**. To get back to €1,400 from work alone he would need about 28 hours a week, nearly three times what he started with, which his condition may not allow. The numbers are invented to show the mechanism, not any country's rules.

Standard advice says earn more, save more, build a cushion. For a disabled person on means-tested support, following it blindly can backfire, and the World Health Organization estimates about one in six people worldwide lives with a significant disability. This post covers how the cliff works, what to check before any change, and the structures that let a disabled person build security anyway. It gives no thresholds: they differ by country, region and benefit, and they change often.

## How a benefit cliff can leave you worse off after a raise or gift

Disability support usually comes in three kinds: **contribution-based** (you paid into a system while working), **needs-based** (assessed on the effect of your condition, with income and savings usually ignored) and **means-tested** (only if your income, savings or both are below set limits). A person may receive more than one kind, and the label alone is not enough: the question is whether that particular benefit tests income, savings or both.

Daniel's drop is a [benefit cliff](#term-benefit-cliff): a small gain in income or savings causes a larger loss of support. Some systems use a [taper](#term-taper) instead, withdrawing the benefit gradually, say 60 cents for each extra euro; gentler, though several benefits tapering at once can still take most of each extra euro.

Savings are the other trigger, and the one people see coming less often. Many means-tested benefits have a limit on capital, and going over it ends eligibility until the money is spent down. An inheritance, a gift from a grandparent, a compensation payment, a back-payment of benefits owed, a pension lump sum, or an emergency fund built exactly as the [emergency fund post](/blog/emergency-fund/) described can each push someone over. And one benefit often unlocks others, such as health cover, housing support, help with energy bills or a care package. Losing such a [passport benefit](#term-passport-benefit) can close the door on the rest, so the stake is everything that depends on eligibility, not just the payment.

<details class="quick-check">
<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: a grandparent gives €10,000 to a grandchild whose benefit has a €6,000 savings limit. What can happen?</summary>

Depending on the rules, the benefit may be reduced or stop until savings fall below the limit, and support linked to it may be affected too. The gift can end up replacing support it was meant to add to.

</details>

## What to check before any change: income, savings and the limit

Before any change, such as a job, more hours, a gift, an inheritance or a new savings habit, three questions to the agency that pays the benefit, or to a welfare rights adviser, do most of the work:

1. **What counts as income?** Wages, but also gifts, help with bills, rental income, a partner's earnings? Many systems assess a couple together. Are some earnings disregarded?
2. **What counts as savings?** Cash and investments usually; a main home often not. What about a car, an undrawn pension, money in trust, a dedicated disability savings account?
3. **What happens at the limit?** A cliff or a taper? A grace period or trial period for starting work? If eligibility ends, how fast can it be restored if the job does not work out?

Answers in writing, and the arithmetic done before the change rather than after, are what people commonly rely on. The check finds room as well as the edge: many systems have work incentives, disregards and trial periods that people miss because they assume any earnings are dangerous.

## When protected accounts or trusts may keep savings outside a means test

Staying below every limit for life is not a plan. Some countries offer a [protected savings account](#term-protected-savings-account) for disabled people with special tax or benefit treatment, which is programme-specific: fully exempt, partly exempt, or subject to balance, contribution or withdrawal rules. Examples include the US ABLE account and Canada's Registered Disability Savings Plan, which adds government contributions and which provinces and territories exempt fully or partly; the two are not identical. Where one exists, many people look at it first for savings, an emergency fund and family gifts, within its limits. Where none exists, the answer to the second question shows which assets are exempt, and that list becomes the savings plan.

A [trust](#term-trust) holds money for someone else's benefit, managed by trustees. A [special-needs trust](#term-special-needs-trust), named differently in different places, may, in some jurisdictions and if suitably drafted, keep its assets from being treated as the disabled person's own; which payments affect eligibility, and what trustees may pay for, such as equipment, therapies, travel or a better wheelchair than the standard issue, depend on the trust and the benefit rules. The design matters: if the beneficiary can demand the money, it usually counts as theirs, so these trusts generally leave payments to the trustees' discretion. Many families use a specialist lawyer, because a badly written trust can fail at the one thing it was for.

<details class="quick-check">
<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: why do special-needs trusts usually leave payments to the trustees' discretion?</summary>

In some systems, a right to demand payment can cause the money to be treated as the beneficiary's own. Whether trustee discretion prevents that depends on the trust and the means test.

</details>

## Families often leave money to a structure, not the person

A parent who leaves €50,000 directly to a disabled adult child on means-tested support may find the child's benefits stop, the inheritance is spent on living costs the benefits used to cover, and when it runs out the child reapplies, no better off. Where the law recognises an appropriate trust for means-testing, the same €50,000 in a suitably drafted trust may fund permitted extras without all of it counting as the child's own savings. Steps families commonly take, after checking local rules with a specialist:

- **Checking whether the will and each pension or insurance scheme can direct money to the intended trust or structure,** and whether that has the expected benefit and tax effect.
- **Telling the rest of the family,** because a generous grandparent's direct bequest, or a well-meaning aunt's, can undo the plan.
- **Writing a [letter of intent](#term-letter-of-intent):** a plain description of the person's routines, preferences, medical needs and the people in their life. It has no legal force, and it is what a future trustee or carer will rely on most.

Families often fund the trust with life insurance on the parents, so the money arrives when their own care stops.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Help with decisions, without losing control</summary>

Some disabled people need support to manage money or make decisions, and there is a range of ways to provide it. In [supported decision-making](#term-supported-decision-making), the person keeps the legal right to decide and chooses trusted supporters to help them understand options and communicate choices; many countries now recognise it formally. With a [power of attorney](#term-power-of-attorney), the person appoints someone to act for them and can usually cancel it. **Guardianship or conservatorship** transfers decision-making to someone else by court order: sometimes necessary, and it removes rights that can be hard to get back. Many disability advocates, and the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, favour trying the less restrictive options first. Practical tools help too: a benefit paid to an appointed person, a bank account with a second authorised user, automatic bill payments.

</details>

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">The other half of the budget: extra costs, insurance and one income</summary>

Disability changes spending as well as income: equipment, adapted transport, higher energy use, specialist food, personal assistance and insurance all cost more. Research by disability organisations in several countries, such as Scope in the UK, has found these extra costs can run to hundreds of euros or pounds a month, varying widely by condition. Rules of thumb such as "housing at about a third of income" assume the rest is free for ordinary living, so they need adjusting when a fixed slice goes on disability costs first. Extra-cost benefits are usually separate, often not means-tested, and frequently under-claimed.

New health, life or income protection cover can be difficult or expensive to buy with a disability, which is why employer group schemes, often without medical questions, are valuable and why many people keep existing policies going (the [insurance basics post](/blog/insurance-basics/) covers the types). On one income, the [solo agers post](/blog/solo-agers-and-single-income-households/) argued for a larger emergency fund and a backup person named in writing; both still apply, and many people hold the fund somewhere the means test ignores.

</details>

## Disability support differs by country

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Disability support in the US, UK, Canada, continental Europe and India</summary>

- **United States.** Strict asset limits apply to the main means-tested programmes. ABLE accounts and special-needs trusts are the standard tools. Health cover is often tied to benefit eligibility, which raises the stakes.
- **United Kingdom.** The main disability extra-cost benefit is not means-tested; support for living costs is, with capital limits and a taper on earnings. Trusts for disabled people have their own tax rules.
- **Canada.** The Registered Disability Savings Plan adds government grants and is largely disregarded by provincial means tests.
- **Continental Europe.** Systems lean more on contribution-based and needs-based support, so cliffs are less common, though they exist where social assistance tops up income. Many countries assess the household, not the individual.
- **India and some other countries with less comprehensive public provision.** Support varies by programme and region, and families may provide a large share of day-to-day support. Trusts and guardianship for disabled adults exist in law, and planning centres on who will manage money after the parents.

Local disability organisations, benefit agencies and welfare rights advisers may be able to explain the current rules; availability and cost vary.

</details>

Good financial advice fits the rules you actually live under. For a disabled person on means-tested support, the first move is reading those rules, and the second is using the routes through them that already exist.
