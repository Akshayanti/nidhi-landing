Priya is 48. She has not spoken to her parents or her brother in twenty years. Her family, in every sense that matters, is four friends she has known since her twenties. They have keys to her flat, they know her medical history, and last winter they took turns driving her to chemotherapy.

If Priya is unconscious in hospital tomorrow, staff may contact her brother, unless she has recorded whom to contact and, where local law allows, appointed someone to act.

In many countries, when nobody has been appointed, the law's defaults point at relatives: spouse, children, parents, siblings. For many people that is simply wrong. This post covers closing the distance between the family the law assumes and the family you have. The rules on who may visit, who is told what and who decides differ between countries and sometimes between hospitals, so it teaches what to ask for rather than a universal form.

## Next of kin follows blood and marriage, not closeness

[Next of kin](#term-next-of-kin) means the closest relative the law recognises. When something has to be decided and nobody has been appointed, institutions work down an order that in many countries looks broadly like this, with details differing by jurisdiction: a spouse or registered partner; adult children; parents; siblings; grandparents, aunts, uncles, nieces, nephews and cousins; then the state. Friends do not appear, nor in many places an unmarried partner, nor the neighbour who has looked in daily for ten years.

| Situation | What local default rules may determine |
|---|---|
| You are in hospital and cannot communicate | Who is contacted or consulted; formal decision-making authority is a separate question |
| You lose decision-making capacity | Who may apply or be appointed to manage particular affairs |
| You die | Who is notified, registers the death or administers the estate |
| Your funeral | Who has authority to arrange it |
| You die without a will | Who inherits |

The phrase **chosen family** comes from LGBTQ+ communities, where rejection by the family people were born into has been common enough that they built their own. It describes many others too: people estranged from relatives for any reason, single and childless adults whose closest ties are friendships, widowed people whose remaining relatives are distant, migrants whose legal family is on another continent. It is a [recognition gap](#term-recognition-gap) in its purest form: relationships that are real, long and load-bearing, which the law has no category for.

## Telling people your wishes does not give them authority

Many people in this position have told their friends what they want, and it feels settled. It is not. A hospital, a bank, a funeral director and a court each need to know who has authority, and they look in two places: a document, or the list. A friend who says "she told me she would not want this" has nothing to show; a brother who arrives after twenty years may have standing that the friend lacks. Hospital visiting is the sharpest example: in some places access and information are limited to legally recognised family unless the patient has named someone, so the friend who came every day can be asked to wait outside while a relative the patient had cut off is briefed. Spoken wishes still matter, because they tell the person you appoint what to do; they work only once that person has been appointed.

It depends on local law. A relative such as her brother may have decision-making authority in some systems; in others, clinicians decide and consult relatives or other permitted people. A valid healthcare appointment may instead give her chosen person authority or a formal role. Her spoken wishes guide whoever acts, but do not appoint that person.

## Splitting roles among friends, with a substitute for each

Legal and institutional defaults often direct several roles toward a spouse or other relative, though the exact powers differ. With chosen family, each job can go to the person who suits it:

| Role | What it involves | Who suits it |
|---|---|---|
| Health decisions | Talking to doctors, deciding under pressure, following your wishes when it is hard | The person who knows your values and stays calm |
| Money and admin | Paying bills, dealing with the bank and landlord while you cannot | The organised one, ideally nearby |
| Executor of the will | Months of paperwork after a death | Someone patient, preferably younger than you |
| Emergency contact and visitors | Being called first, being allowed in | Whoever is closest, in both senses |
| Funeral | Carrying out your wishes about your body and the ceremony | Someone who will stand firm if relatives object |

Three habits help it hold: a **substitute for every role,** because friends move, fall ill and die; **someone younger** among the people named; and **asking first,** because these are real burdens, then giving each person a copy and telling them where the rest are.

The documents are the ones the [unmarried couples post](/blog/unmarried-and-cohabiting-couples/) covered, with more than one person to name: a healthcare [power of attorney](#term-power-of-attorney) and an [advance directive](#term-advance-directive) for medical decisions; a **hospital visitation or patient advocate form** naming who may visit and be told about your condition, often free and lodged with your doctor; a financial power of attorney; a **will** naming who inherits and who carries it out, without which an estranged parent or sibling can take everything in many places; [beneficiary forms](#term-beneficiary-form) on pensions and insurance, which some schemes must follow and others treat as a guide ([when a beneficiary form is binding](/blog/when-the-default-plan-does-not-fit-you/#is-a-pension-beneficiary-form-binding)); and an **emergency card** in a wallet and on a phone's lock screen, naming who to call and saying a healthcare power of attorney exists.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Making the plan hard to overturn</summary>

Where a relative might contest your choices, a few habits strengthen the plan. **Correct local formalities:** a document that misses local signing, witnessing or registration requirements may fail, which is why many people in this position use a lawyer. **Saying the omission is deliberate:** a sentence in the will stating that you have intentionally made no provision for named relatives helps answer the argument that you forgot them. **Regular updates:** periodic review keeps names, assets and instructions current, though it does not by itself prevent a challenge. **Knowing the limits:** where [forced heirship](#term-forced-heirship) reserves a share for children or other relatives, a will cannot give everything to friends; pensions and life insurance with a named beneficiary, and jointly held assets with survivorship, usually fall outside the estate and become the main route, so whether a scheme treats its form as binding matters more. **Tax:** inheritance and gift taxes usually depend on relationship, and a friend is often in the highest band with the smallest allowance; a life policy written for the friend's benefit can cover the bill.

</details>

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Funding what you ask, and doing it together</summary>

Naming a friend as executor or attorney asks for their time and sometimes their money. Some people leave the executor a specific sum and say so in the will; some keep enough cash reachable by their financial attorney for a few months of bills; and a list of accounts, policies and contacts, described in the [solo agers post](/blog/solo-agers-and-single-income-households/), can make the difference between a manageable task and an impossible one for a friend with no legal right to search your papers.

Chosen families usually face the same gap from every side: if you need your friends named in your documents, they likely need you named in theirs. Some groups set aside a day, agree who holds which role for whom, and sign everything at once, spreading the legal cost and getting the awkward conversation done in one sitting. Pooled savings and informal loans between friends often have no clear legal framework if someone dies or a friendship ends, so clear ownership and anything substantial in writing help.

</details>

## Why the funeral often needs its own written plan

In many places the right to decide what happens to your body belongs to the next of kin or the executor, instructions in a will may not be binding, and the will is sometimes not read until after the funeral. The result can be a ceremony arranged by relatives who did not know you, in a tradition you left, under a name you stopped using, with your friends not invited. What helps: appointing a person to control funeral arrangements, where the law has a document for that; written, signed and dated wishes with copies held by the people who will be present, not only a lawyer; an executor from your chosen family who knows where the will is; and, where suitable and adequately protected, a prepaid funeral plan whose details the chosen person holds, with its legal effect and consumer protections checked locally.

Because they may not be binding, and the will is sometimes not read until after the funeral has been arranged by whoever the law puts in charge.

## Who decides, who inherits and who is told can vary by country

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Consent, inheritance, tax and privacy rules across countries</summary>

- **Decisions for an unconscious patient.** Some countries give the next of kin a legal right to consent; others give doctors the decision and consult relatives only for information. Where a healthcare attorney appointment is recognised and valid, it can give decision-making authority or a formal role in consultation.
- **Inheritance freedom.** Wide in many common-law countries; restricted by forced heirship across much of continental Europe, Latin America and countries applying religious inheritance law.
- **Inheritance tax.** Where it exists, the gap between the rate for a spouse or child and for an unrelated person is often very large.
- **Registered friendships.** A few jurisdictions let two people who are not a couple register a mutual support relationship with some rights. It is rare, and worth knowing about where it exists.
- **Privacy rules.** Medical confidentiality can mean a hospital tells a friend nothing without written consent; a visitation and information form is the usual way to change that.

</details>

The law's list is a guess about who loves you, made long ago. It can be replaced, one role at a time, with the names of the people who actually do.

If you were in hospital tonight, who would the law call, and is that who you would choose?
