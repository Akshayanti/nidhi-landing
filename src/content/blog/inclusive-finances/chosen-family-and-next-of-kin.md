The Mastery post on estate planning explains what happens to your money and your decisions if you make no plan: a set of default rules steps in. It describes those rules as a safety net, which for most readers they are.

The rules have one feature that post had no reason to dwell on. Every default points at a relative. Spouse, children, parents, siblings. The law's idea of who is close to you is fixed by marriage and blood.

For many people that is simply wrong.

Priya is 48. She has not spoken to her parents or her brother in twenty years. Her family, in every sense that matters, is four friends she has known since her twenties. They have keys to her flat. They know her medical history. They spent last winter taking turns driving her to chemotherapy.

If Priya is unconscious in hospital tomorrow, the person the doctors will call is her brother.

This post is about closing the distance between the family the law assumes and the family you have. The rules on who may visit, who is told what, and who decides differ between countries and sometimes between hospitals, so what follows teaches what to ask for and not a universal form.

## Part one: the list the law uses

### Next of kin

**Next of kin** means the closest relative the law recognises. When a decision has to be made for you and you have appointed nobody, institutions work down an order that, in many countries, looks broadly like this (the exact order and who counts differ by jurisdiction):

1. Spouse, or registered partner where that status exists
2. Adult children
3. Parents
4. Siblings
5. Grandparents, aunts, uncles, nieces, nephews, cousins
6. The state

Friends do not appear. Neither does an unmarried partner in many jurisdictions, nor a former partner you are still close to, nor the neighbour who has looked in on you daily for ten years.

The order is used in at least five situations:

| Situation | What the list decides |
|---|---|
| You are in hospital and cannot communicate | Who receives information, and in many systems who consents to treatment |
| You lose mental capacity | Who may be appointed to manage your affairs |
| You die | Who is notified and who registers the death |
| Your funeral | Who chooses what happens to your body |
| You die without a will | Who inherits |

This series calls the distance between your real household and what the law can see a **recognition gap**. Chosen family is that gap in its purest form: the relationships are real, long, and load-bearing, and the law has no category for them.

### Who this affects

The phrase **chosen family** comes from LGBTQ+ communities, where being rejected by the family you were born into has been common enough that people built their own. It describes many others as well:

- People estranged from relatives for any reason
- Single and childless adults whose closest ties are friendships
- Widowed people whose remaining relatives are distant
- Migrants whose legal family is on another continent
- Anyone whose nearest relative is not someone they would trust with a decision

### Why saying it out loud is not enough

Most people in this position have told their friends what they want. It feels settled. It is not.

An institution needs to know who has **authority**, and it looks in two places: a document, or the list. A friend who says "she told me she would not want this" has nothing to show. A brother who arrives after twenty years has legal standing.

Hospital visiting is the sharpest example. In some places, access and information are restricted to legally recognised family unless the patient has named someone. The friend who has been there every day can be asked to wait outside while a relative the patient had cut off is briefed by the doctors.

Spoken wishes still matter. They tell the person you appoint what to do. They only work once that person has been appointed.

## Part two: putting your people in charge

Each row of the table has a document that replaces the default. The post on [unmarried couples](/blog/unmarried-and-cohabiting-couples/) covers a will, beneficiary forms, and powers of attorney for a partner. The instruments are the same here. Two things are different: there is usually more than one person to name, and there may be a relative who will object.

### Split the roles

A spouse is typically handed every role at once. With chosen family you can do better and match each job to the right person.

| Role | What it involves | Who suits it |
|---|---|---|
| Health decisions | Talking to doctors, deciding under pressure, following your wishes when it is hard | The person who knows your values and stays calm |
| Money and admin | Paying bills, dealing with the bank and the landlord while you cannot | The organised one, ideally nearby |
| Executor of the will | Months of paperwork after a death | Someone patient, and preferably younger than you |
| Emergency contact and visitors | Being called first, being allowed in | Whoever is closest, in both senses |
| Funeral | Carrying out your wishes about your body and the ceremony | Someone who will stand firm if relatives object |

Three habits help this hold:

- **Name a substitute for every role.** Friends move, fall ill, and die.
- **Include someone younger than you** among the people named.
- **Ask first.** These are real burdens. Give each person a copy of the document and tell them where the rest are.

### The documents

**A healthcare power of attorney** names who decides on treatment. **An advance directive** records what you would want. Together they replace the list for medical decisions.

**A hospital visitation or patient advocate designation** names who may visit and be told about your condition. Some hospitals have their own form. It costs nothing and deals with exactly the scenario above. Lodge a copy with your doctor.

**A financial power of attorney** names who can manage your money.

**A will** names who inherits and who carries it out. Without one, the law's order applies in full, and in many jurisdictions an estranged parent or sibling can take everything.

**Beneficiary designations** on pensions and insurance name who is paid. These usually pass outside the will, which matters in the next section.

**An emergency card.** A card in your wallet and a note on your phone's lock screen naming who to call and stating that a healthcare power of attorney exists. It is what gets your friend telephoned in the first hour.

### The funeral is its own problem

This one surprises people. In many places the right to decide what happens to your body belongs to the next of kin or to your executor, and instructions in a will may not be binding. The will is sometimes not read until after the funeral.

The result can be a ceremony arranged by relatives who did not know you, in a tradition you left, under a name you stopped using, with your friends not invited.

What helps:

- **Appoint a person to control funeral arrangements,** where your jurisdiction has a document for that.
- **Write your wishes down, sign and date them,** and give copies to the people who will be present, not only to your lawyer.
- **Make sure your executor is one of your chosen family** and knows where the will is.
- **Consider a prepaid funeral plan** in your chosen person's hands. It settles many arguments in advance.

### Make it hard to overturn

Where a relative might contest your choices, a few habits strengthen the plan.

- **Use the correct local formalities.** Homemade documents are the ones that most often fail, which is why many people in this position use a lawyer.
- **Say in the will that the omission is deliberate.** A sentence stating that you have intentionally made no provision for named relatives removes the argument that you forgot them.
- **Update regularly.** A will reviewed every few years is harder to challenge than one signed once, long ago.
- **Know your limits.** Where **forced heirship** reserves a share of an estate for children or other relatives, a will cannot give everything to friends. Pensions and life insurance with a named beneficiary usually fall outside the estate, as do assets held jointly with a right of survivorship. In those countries, they are the main route for providing for chosen family.
- **Expect tax.** Inheritance and gift taxes are usually set by relationship, and a friend is often in the highest band with the smallest allowance. A life policy written for the friend's benefit can cover the bill.

### Fund what you are asking for

Naming a friend as executor or attorney asks for their time and sometimes their money.

- Some people leave the executor a specific sum for their trouble, and say so in the will.
- Some keep enough cash reachable by their financial attorney to cover a few months of bills.
- Keep a list of accounts, policies, and contacts where they can find it. The post on [one-income households](/blog/solo-agers-and-single-income-households/) describes this document. For a friend with no legal right to search your papers, it is the difference between a manageable task and an impossible one.

### Do it together

Chosen families usually face the same gap from every direction. If you need your friends named in your documents, they very likely need you named in theirs.

Some groups of friends set aside a day, agree who will hold which role for whom, and sign everything at once. It spreads the legal cost, makes sure nobody is left out, and gets the awkward conversation done in one sitting.

One caution on mixing money. Pooled savings and informal loans between friends are generous and have no legal framework if someone dies or a friendship ends. Keep ownership clear and write down anything substantial.

## Cross-continent notes

**Decision-making for an unconscious patient.** Some countries give the next of kin a legal right to consent. Others give doctors the decision and consult relatives only for information. In both, a named attorney changes who is heard.

**Inheritance freedom.** Wide in most common-law countries. Restricted by forced heirship across much of continental Europe, Latin America, and countries applying religious inheritance law.

**Inheritance tax.** Where it exists, the gap between the rate for a spouse or child and the rate for an unrelated person is often very large.

**Registered forms of friendship.** A few jurisdictions allow two people who are not a couple to register a mutual support relationship carrying some rights. It is rare, and worth knowing if you live in one.

**Privacy rules.** Medical confidentiality law means hospitals may refuse to tell a friend anything without your written consent. The visitation and information form is the fix.

## Getting started

- **Write down who the law would call today.** If the name is wrong, that is your reason.
- **Draw your own roles table.** Two names in every row.
- **Ask each person.**
- **Do the free things now:** the emergency card, the hospital form, the beneficiary forms.
- **Find out what the paid things involve where you live:** will, powers of attorney, funeral appointment. Forms and formalities are local.
- **Propose doing it as a group.**

The law's list is a guess about who loves you, made centuries ago. It can be replaced, one role at a time, with the names of the people who actually do.
