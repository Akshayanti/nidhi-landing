  - question: "Why does a career break cost more than the salary I give up?"
    answer: "Because several things stop at the same time as the salary and keep costing you for decades. Pension contributions stop, and so does the growth those contributions would have earned until retirement. Any employer match stops. When you return, you often come back on a lower salary than you would have reached, and every later percentage raise is calculated from that lower base. In many countries your state pension also depends on years of paid contributions. Added together, they can make the long-term cost materially larger than the salary missed during the break; the size depends on contribution rates, return assumptions, state pension rules and the salary after returning."
  - question: "What is the difference between the gender pay gap and the gender wealth gap?"
    answer: "The pay gap compares what women and men earn at a point in time, usually per hour. The wealth gap compares what they have accumulated over a lifetime: savings, investments, and pension entitlements. The wealth gap is larger because it adds up every year of lower pay, every year out of paid work, and every missed pension contribution, and then compounds the difference. In the EU in 2024, women's average pension income was 24.5% lower than men's, while their average gross hourly earnings were 11.1% lower."
  - question: "What is a caregiver credit?"
    answer: "It is a mechanism some state pension systems use to fill the gap that caregiving leaves in a contribution record. For a period spent caring for a young child or a dependent adult, the system treats you as if you had contributed. Rules differ widely: which kinds of care count, how many years are credited, whether you have to apply, and whether there is a deadline. Several systems credit years only if someone claims them, so it is worth checking even for years long past."
  - question: "Should we pay into the carer's pension while they are not earning?"
    answer: "Many couples see it as one of the most direct protections available. When one partner steps back so the household can function, the household receives the benefit and the carer alone bears the pension cost. Paying a contribution into the carer's own retirement account from the shared budget puts part of that cost back with the household. Many countries allow contributions to a non-earning person's pension, sometimes with tax relief; limits and rules differ, so local rules decide what is possible. For it to protect the carer, the account needs to be in the carer's own name. Whether a household can afford it during a break depends on its budget."

Ana is 35 and earns €40,000 a year. She and her employer each pay 5% of her salary into her pension, €4,000 a year in total. She steps out of paid work for five years to care for two children and returns at 40 on a salary 10% below where it would otherwise have been.

The [life events post](/blog/life-events/) counted the pay: €200,000 not earned during the break, and €4,000 a year less for the 25 years after it, another €100,000 by 65. It stopped with one line on pensions, "plus whatever it would have grown to". This post follows that line to the end, because the pension cost does not stop when the carer goes back to work. It runs to retirement, and nobody sees it while the decision is being made.

Pensions are mostly fed by paid work, and a great deal of necessary work is unpaid: someone looks after the baby, the parent with dementia, the brother who cannot live alone. The International Labour Organization estimates that women do about three quarters of unpaid care work worldwide. Nothing here argues against caring for someone; it argues for knowing the price, so a household can decide how to share it.

## A career break's pension cost keeps growing until retirement

Two pension lines sit under the pay a household sees.

**The contributions that never happened.** For five years, €4,000 a year did not go in: €20,000, half of it the employer's money. Money invested between 35 and 40 has 25 to 30 years to grow. At 5% a year after inflation (the middle of the long-run range for developed-market shares; a pension holding bonds would usually grow more slowly), it would have become about **€75,000** by 65.

**The pension on the lower base.** Contributions are a percentage of salary, so 10% less salary means €400 a year less going in for the rest of her career: €10,000 over 25 years, about **€19,000** at 65. The [growing your income post](/blog/growing-your-income/) showed how a lower base follows every later raise; pension contributions inherit the same gap.

| What it is | Not paid in | Missing from the pension at 65 |
|---|---|---|
| Contributions during the break | €20,000 | €75,000 |
| Lower contributions after returning | €10,000 | €19,000 |
| **Total** | **€30,000** | **€94,000** |

€30,000 never paid in becomes €94,000 missing at retirement, more than three times as much. In this simplified illustration in today's euros, the €300,000 of pay and the €94,000 of pension add up to about €394,000; it is not a present-value estimate, and tax, benefits and childcare costs are left out. The pension part is the share no payslip shows.

The figures are illustrative: a shorter break costs less, returning at full salary removes the second line, and contributions during the break shrink the first. The shape holds: the gap at retirement is several times the contributions missed. Not in the table is the [state pension](#term-state-pension), because it depends on the country; in many systems five missing years mean a permanently smaller one unless care is credited. Reduced hours follow the same pattern, scaled down and stretched out: part-time work for fifteen years can cost more than a five-year break.

<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: why is the €20,000 of skipped contributions worth about €75,000 at 65?</summary>

Because money paid in at 35 to 40 would have grown for 25 to 30 years. At an illustrative 5% a year after inflation, that growth roughly multiplies it by three to four.

</details>

## The wealth gap is wider than the pay gap

The [gender pay gap](#term-gender-pay-gap) compares what women and men earn at a moment, usually per hour. The [gender wealth gap](#term-gender-wealth-gap) compares what they have built up over a lifetime: savings, investments and pension rights. A 10% pay gap in one year is small. The same gap every year, plus years at zero, plus missed contributions, plus the growth on all of it, is a very different number. In the EU in 2024, women's average pension income was 24.5% lower than men's, while their average gross hourly earnings were 11.1% lower, according to Eurostat. The pay gap measures a moment; the wealth gap is the sum of every moment, with interest.

## Credits, contributions and a re-entry plan protect the carer

A household that chooses care is making a reasonable decision. The issue is who carries the cost: the benefit goes to the whole family, the pension gap to one person. The [money scripts post](/blog/money-scripts/) described unspoken beliefs about money; "it is all shared anyway" holds until a separation or a death, when accounts turn out to be in someone's name. Four tools put part of the cost back with the household:

1. **Claiming [caregiver credits](#term-caregiver-credit).** Some state pension systems treat years caring for a young child or a dependent adult as if contributions had been paid. Worth checking: which care counts (childcare more often than adult care); whether it is automatic or goes to whoever claims a particular benefit, so the wrong parent can end up with it; and whether past years can still be claimed. It costs nothing and is often missed.
2. **Keeping the carer's pension alive.** Many countries allow contributions to a non-earning person's retirement account, sometimes with tax relief (the [tax-advantaged accounts post](/blog/tax-advantaged-accounts/) covered the types). Even half of the first line, paid during the break, closes much of the gap, because those contributions have the longest to grow. It protects the carer when the account is in the carer's own name and the contribution is treated as a fixed household cost, like rent, rather than paid when money is left over; whether a household can afford it depends on its budget.
3. **Negotiating the way back.** The lower salary on return drives the second line and most of the pay cost, and it is the most negotiable part. Employers often price a returning carer on the old salary; the market has usually moved. Occasional freelance work, a qualification kept current and contact with former colleagues make a four-year gap read differently.
4. **Catching up afterwards:** a higher contribution rate for a period, using any catch-up allowances; buying back missing state pension years where allowed, which can be good value depending on the rules and how long the pension is drawn; and, for some people, sending the first few raises to the pension.

<details class="quick-check">
<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: which of the four tools costs nothing?</summary>

Claiming caregiver credits, where the system offers them. The others cost money now or need a negotiation.

</details>

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">If there is no partner to share the cost</summary>

A single parent, or someone caring alone for a relative, has no household to share the cost with. The same four tools apply with different weights. Credits often matter most, because they are free. Where it is feasible, some carers keep reduced paid hours to preserve income and a route back to fuller work. Many claim any carer's allowance or benefit they are eligible for, for its own sake and because it is sometimes what triggers the pension credit.

</details>

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Caring for a parent or a disabled relative</summary>

Care for a child has a rough timetable. Care for an ageing parent or a disabled relative can begin overnight after a fall or a diagnosis, last an unknown number of years, and arrive in the carer's fifties, usually the highest-earning and highest-saving years. Fewer pension systems credit it, and it more often means years of reduced hours than a clean break. Stepping back at 52 does less damage through compounding, with fewer years left for lost contributions to grow, and more through lost earnings, since those are peak years and returning at 57 is harder than at 40. The conversation worth having early is among siblings: when one provides the care and the others do not, the carer is subsidising the family from their own retirement, and shared contributions toward paid help or the carer's pension even that out.

</details>

## Care credits and pension protection vary by country

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Care credits and pensions in Europe, the UK, the US, India and elsewhere</summary>

- **Germany, France, Sweden and other contribution-based systems.** Years raising young children earn pension credits, in some cases several years per child; care for adults is credited under narrower conditions.
- **United Kingdom.** State pension credits come with claiming child benefit for a young child and with certain carer benefits, and go to the named claimant, which is where the wrong-parent problem comes from.
- **United States.** Social Security is based on the highest-earning years of a long career, so years at zero pull the average down, and there is no general caregiver credit. Married couples can contribute to a non-earning spouse's retirement account.
- **India and much of Asia, Africa and Latin America.** Where most people work outside formal pension systems, the carer's security depends on family assets and whose name they are in. Savings or property in the carer's own name may give some independent long-term security, though without the rules or guarantees of a state pension credit.
- **Parental leave.** Where leave is paid and shared between parents, breaks are shorter and more evenly split, and the gap is smaller; where it is short or unpaid, one parent usually absorbs all of it.

</details>

Care is work that every family and every economy depends on. Pension systems count it unevenly, so it is worth checking what the public system recognises and what gap remains for the household.
