The Mastery post on retirement planning describes how most people fund later life: a state pension built from years of contributions, a workplace pension built from a percentage of salary, and private savings on top. All three are fed by paid work. The more years you work and the more you earn, the more you get.

That design has a blind spot. A great deal of necessary work is unpaid. Someone looks after the baby, the parent with dementia, the brother who cannot live alone. The International Labour Organization estimates that women do about three quarters of all unpaid care work worldwide.

Pension systems mostly record those years as a gap.

The post on [life events](/blog/life-events/) counted the pay a parent gives up when a child arrives, and found it larger than everything the child costs directly. It stopped at the pay, with one line on pensions: "plus whatever it would have grown to". This post follows that line to the end, because the pension cost does not stop when the carer goes back to work. It runs all the way to retirement, and nobody sees it while the decision is being made.

Nothing here is an argument against caring for someone. It is an argument for knowing the price, so a household can decide how to share it.

## Part one: what a break does to a pension

Take one person and one decision.

Ana is 35 and earns €40,000 a year. She and her employer each pay 5% of her salary into a pension, €4,000 a year in total. She steps out of paid work for five years to care for two children, and returns at 40, on a salary 10% below where she would otherwise have been.

The pay side works exactly as the life events post showed: €200,000 of salary not earned during the break, and €4,000 a year less for the 25 years after it, another €100,000 by 65. Those are the numbers a household sees. Underneath them sit two pension lines that it usually does not.

### Line one: the contributions that never happened

For five years, €4,000 a year did not go into her pension. That is €20,000 of contributions, half of which would have been her employer's money.

Those contributions would also have grown. Money invested between 35 and 40 has twenty-five to thirty years to compound before retirement. At 5% real growth (growth after inflation), €4,000 a year for five years becomes about **€75,000** by age 65.

### Line two: the pension on the lower base

Her pension contributions are a percentage of salary, so a lower salary means lower contributions for the rest of her career. Ten percent of €4,000 is €400 a year less going in: €10,000 over the 25 years. With 5% real growth that is about **€19,000** less at 65.

The post on [growing your income](/blog/growing-your-income/) showed how a small difference in salary compounds across a career, because every later raise is a percentage of the base. A pension contribution is a percentage of the same base, so it inherits the same gap.

### The total

| What it is | Not paid in | Missing from the pension at 65 |
|---|---|---|
| Contributions during the break | €20,000 | €75,000 |
| Lower contributions after returning | €10,000 | €19,000 |
| **Total** | **€30,000** | **€94,000** |

€30,000 that was never paid in becomes €94,000 missing at retirement, more than three times as much. On top of the €300,000 of pay, that brings the cost of the break to about €394,000, and the pension part is the share no payslip ever shows.

These figures are illustrative. A shorter break costs less. A return at full salary removes line two. Contributions paid into the carer's pension during the break shrink line one, as part two shows. What the example shows is the shape: the pension gap at retirement is several times the contributions missed along the way.

One more line is missing from the table because it depends entirely on the country: the state pension. In many state pension systems, the amount you receive depends on how many years you contributed. Five missing years can mean a permanently smaller state pension, unless the system credits care.

### Reduced hours follow the same pattern

Many carers do not stop. They go part-time. The arithmetic is identical, scaled down and stretched out: lower pay, lower contributions, slower progression, for as long as the arrangement lasts. Part-time work that continues for fifteen years can cost more than a five-year break.

### Why the wealth gap is wider than the pay gap

Two measures are often confused.

The **gender pay gap** compares what women and men earn at a moment in time, usually per hour worked.

The **gender wealth gap** compares what they have accumulated over a lifetime: savings, investments, pension entitlements.

The second is much larger than the first, and Ana's example shows why. A pay gap of 10% in one year is small. The same gap applied to every year, plus the years at zero, plus the missed contributions, plus the compounding on all of it, is a very different number. In the European Union, the gap in pension income between women and men is roughly twice the gap in hourly pay.

The pay gap measures a moment. The wealth gap is the sum of every moment, with interest.

## Part two: protecting the carer

A household that chooses care is making a reasonable decision. The problem is who carries the cost. The benefit goes to the whole family. The pension gap goes to one person.

The post on [money scripts](/blog/money-scripts/) described the unspoken beliefs people hold about money. "It is all shared anyway" is one of them, and it is true right up until a separation or a death, when accounts turn out to be in someone's name.

Four tools put part of the cost back where it belongs.

### 1. Claim the credits

A **caregiver credit** is a mechanism in some state pension systems that fills the gap caregiving leaves in a contribution record. For years spent caring for a young child or a dependent adult, the system treats you as though you had paid in.

Three things to check where you live:

- **Which care counts.** Childcare is credited more often than care for an adult.
- **Whether it is automatic.** In several systems the credit goes to whoever claims a particular benefit, or has to be applied for. If the wrong parent is the named claimant, the credit can go to the partner who did not need it.
- **Whether past years can be claimed.** Some systems allow it, some have deadlines.

This costs nothing and is frequently missed.

### 2. Keep the carer's pension alive

The most direct fix is for the household to keep paying into the carer's own pension during the break.

Many countries allow contributions to the retirement account of a person who is not earning, sometimes with tax relief attached. The post on [tax-advantaged accounts](/blog/tax-advantaged-accounts/) covers the account types. The amount does not need to match what an employer would have paid. Even half of line one, contributed during the break, closes a large part of the gap, because those are the contributions with the longest time to grow.

Two conditions make this real protection: the account is in the carer's own name, and the contribution is treated as a fixed household cost, like rent, and not as something paid when there is money left over.

### 3. Negotiate the way back in

The lower salary on return drives line two and most of the pay cost, and it is the most negotiable part of the break.

The return to work is a salary negotiation, and the post on growing your income applies in full: research the current market rate for the role, not the rate you left on, and anchor there. Employers often price a returning carer on their old salary. The market has usually moved.

Staying loosely attached during the break helps: occasional freelance work, a professional qualification kept current, contact with former colleagues. A four-year gap and a four-year gap with evidence of activity are read differently.

### 4. Catch up deliberately

After returning, three moves recover lost ground:

- **A higher contribution rate for a period.** Several systems have catch-up allowances or let you use unused allowances from earlier years.
- **Buying back state pension years.** Where voluntary contributions for missing years are allowed, they are often cheap relative to the lifetime income they add.
- **Directing raises to the pension.** For the first few raises after returning, sending the increase to the pension closes the gap without cutting current spending.

### If there is no partner

A single parent or a person caring alone for a relative has no household to share the cost with. The same four tools apply, with different weights. Credits matter most, because they are free. Staying attached to paid work, even at low hours, matters more than for a couple, because it preserves both income and the route back. Any carer's allowance or benefit your country pays is worth claiming for its own sake and because it is sometimes what triggers the pension credit.

## Care for a parent is different

Care for a child has a rough timetable. Care for an ageing parent or a disabled relative does not.

- It can begin overnight, after a fall or a diagnosis.
- Nobody knows how long it will last.
- It tends to arrive in a carer's fifties, which are usually the highest-earning and highest-saving years.
- Fewer pension systems credit it.
- It more often means reduced hours for many years than a clean break.

Stepping back at 52 does less damage through compounding, because there are fewer years left for lost contributions to grow. It does more damage through lost earnings, because those are peak years, and a return to work at 57 is harder than at 40.

The conversation worth having early is among siblings. When one sibling provides the care and the others do not, the carer is subsidising the family from their own retirement. Shared contributions toward paid help, or toward the carer's pension, are ways of evening that out.

## Cross-continent notes

**Germany, France, Sweden, and other contribution-based systems.** Years spent raising young children earn pension credits, in some cases several years per child. Care for adults is credited under narrower conditions.

**United Kingdom.** Credits toward the state pension are attached to claiming child benefit for a young child and to certain carer benefits. They go to the named claimant, which is where the wrong-parent problem comes from.

**United States.** The public pension is based on the highest-earning years of a long career, so years at zero pull the average down, and there is no general caregiver credit. Contributions to a non-earning spouse's retirement account are permitted for married couples.

**India and much of Asia, Africa, and Latin America.** Where most people work outside formal pension systems, the carer's security depends on family assets and on whose name those assets are in. Holding savings and property in the carer's own name does the job a pension credit does elsewhere.

**Parental leave.** Where leave is paid and shared between parents, breaks are shorter and more evenly split, and the gap is smaller. Where leave is short or unpaid, one parent usually absorbs all of it.

## Getting started

- **Before a break:** run the pay and pension lines for your own salary. Decide as a household how the cost will be shared.
- **During a break:** claim every credit, in the right name. Keep a contribution going into the carer's own pension.
- **On returning:** negotiate from today's market rate. Raise the contribution rate for a few years.
- **Afterwards:** check your state pension record for gaps and whether they can still be filled.

Care is work. It produces something every family and every economy depends on. The pension system mostly fails to count it, so the household has to.
