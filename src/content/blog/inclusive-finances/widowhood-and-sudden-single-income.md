A retired couple have pensions of €1,400 and €1,100 a month and spend €2,300 together. The partner with the larger pension dies. On a rough equivalence-scale illustration, the survivor's comparable spending is about €1,600, roughly 70% of what the two spent, though their actual bills could be higher or lower. Their income falls to €1,870 if the scheme pays a 55% survivor pension, or to €1,100 if it pays nothing, which leaves them €500 a month short.

Losing a partner is two events at once. One is grief. The other is a financial restructuring: a household built on two people, often two incomes, rebuilt around one, arriving in the worst year of a life. The [windfalls post](/blog/windfall-management/) noted that money arriving with a loss is the hardest money to handle well. What helps most is knowing in advance how the tasks divide: a few have deadlines, and most do not. [Survivor benefits](#term-survivor-benefit), inheritance procedure and tax differ in every country and between married and unmarried partners, so this post teaches what to check rather than what anyone is entitled to.

## Which tasks after a death have deadlines and which can wait

**The first days.** Registering the death within the local deadline, and finding out how certificates or official extracts are issued; banks, insurers and pension schemes can say which original, certified or digital evidence they accept. Finding the will and any funeral instructions. Securing cash for two to three months: access to accounts in the dead person's sole name is often restricted until the estate is dealt with, while a joint account's treatment depends on its terms, local law and the bank, and a bank can say what it will release for funeral costs and living expenses.

**The first weeks.** Telling the employer (final pay, any death-in-service benefit, health cover that came through the job); every pension scheme and the state system, each asked whether anything is payable to a survivor and whether there is a time limit; and insurers, to claim on life policies and keep home and car cover going in the survivor's name. Keeping rent or mortgage, utilities and premiums paid, because a lapsed policy or missed payment creates a second problem. And checking tax deadlines for reporting the death and any inheritance return. Much of this can be handed to a friend or relative who asks what they can do.

Some survivor benefits expire if not claimed in time, which is why they sit on this list. [Probate](#term-probate) can take months; some jointly owned assets, pensions and policies may pass under their ownership or scheme rules rather than through the estate, and can be what a survivor lives on in the meantime, though the result and the evidence needed differ by country and provider.

**What can wait:** selling the home or buying another; moving to be near family; investing a life insurance payout or pension lump sum; paying off the mortgage with it; giving or lending to children or relatives; leaving a job or retiring early; changing whose name assets are in; and any financial product someone is offering to sell.

<details class="quick-check">
<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: a relative suggests using the life insurance payout to clear the mortgage next week. Deadline list or wait list?</summary>

Usually the wait list, unless the loan terms, arrears or an expiring option create a real deadline. Repaying is large and hard to reverse, so many people first check the mortgage terms, their cash needs, any penalties and what else the payout must cover.

</details>

## Why many advisers suggest waiting six to twelve months for major decisions

A common bereavement guideline is to postpone large, hard-to-reverse decisions when no real deadline applies. Six to twelve months is a rule of thumb, not a requirement: grief affects people differently, affecting concentration, memory and judgement, and housing, immigration, debt or care needs may make earlier action necessary. The windfalls post described pausing before deciding on a large sum; after a death, some people keep a payout in a simple, accessible account while they work out what it must cover. Deposit protection, inflation, tax, currency and provider risk are local, so the temporary arrangement and any protection limit need checking.

When relatives, advisers or salespeople push, one sentence covers all of them: *"I am not making any financial decisions until next spring."* People who have the survivor's interests at heart usually accept it.

## Why income often falls further than costs after a partner dies

Two people do not spend twice what one does: rent, heating, internet, a car and home insurance cost about the same. Common statistical equivalence scales, such as the one the [solo agers post](/blog/solo-agers-and-single-income-households/) described, put one adult's needs at roughly two-thirds to **70%** of a couple's for the same standard of living; they compare households and do not predict a particular survivor's bills. Income can fall much further, depending on where it came from and whether the relationship was legally recognised:

| The survivor's position | Income after the death | Monthly costs | Surplus or shortfall |
|---|---|---|---|
| Married, scheme pays a 55% survivor pension | €1,100 + €770 = €1,870 | €1,600 | +€270 |
| Married, scheme pays no survivor pension | €1,100 | €1,600 | -€500 |
| Not married, no survivor benefit | €1,100 | €1,600 | -€500 |

The figures are illustrative; survivor pensions range from nothing to the full amount. The same couple and the same loss differ by €770 a month for life, decided by a pension scheme rule and a line on a certificate; the [unmarried couples](/blog/unmarried-and-cohabiting-couples/) and [marriage not recognised](/blog/when-your-marriage-is-not-recognised-abroad/) posts cover what to put in place while both partners are alive. For a younger survivor the pattern is sharper: a salary stops completely, children's costs continue, and childcare the couple shared may have to be bought.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">What to check, and rebuilding the plan after the first year</summary>

**What to check:** the state system (survivor pensions, bereavement payments, benefits for children, often depending on marriage, age, children and the partner's contributions); every workplace pension, including from jobs the partner left years ago; life insurance, including cover attached to a mortgage, card, bank account or employer; and debts. In many systems, debts in the dead person's sole name are paid from the estate rather than inherited personally, while joint debts remain the survivor's, though rules vary, so it is worth confirming a debt really is yours before paying from your own money because a collector says so. The survivor's own will, beneficiary forms and powers of attorney may name the person who died. These are worth reviewing once the immediate deadlines are under control, rather than automatically postponing them for a year: a deceased beneficiary or attorney may leave the result to a substitute or a local default. Any replacement follows local formalities, and independent help can reduce the burden while concentration is limited.

**Rebuilding,** after six to twelve months, follows the same order as after a [separation](/blog/divorce-and-separation-finances/), for a different reason: a new budget from actual figures; a [cash flow forecast](/blog/cash-flow-forecasting/) for two years including the one-off costs after a death; the emergency fund resized for one income; the home, a better question in month twelve than month two; allocating the lump sum; and checking the survivor's own documents again after their earlier review.

**If the partner handled the money,** the survivor inherits a system they have never seen. A list of every account, policy, pension and regular payment, built from statements, post, email and tax returns, is the starting point. Many survivors find paid professional help worth it here; an adviser paid a fee for their time can reduce the conflict of commission on products sold, and anyone pressing to move or invest money in the first months deserves caution, however kind. For a couple where one person runs the finances, writing that list now and showing the other where it is may be the most useful thing they can do.

</details>

## How to make yourself hard to reach for scams aimed at the bereaved

Deaths are published, a payout may be on the way, and the survivor is exhausted and often handling money alone for the first time. The [scams post](/blog/recognizing-and-avoiding-financial-scams-and-fraud/) covers the general warning signs; the expander below lists approaches common after a bereavement.

Three habits many survivors use to make themselves hard to reach: **nothing is decided on the day it is proposed** (the spring sentence works here too); **you make the call,** on a number you looked up yourself, never one supplied by whoever got in touch; and **one other person knows** about any request for money before acting on it, because fraud depends on secrecy.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Approaches aimed at the bereaved</summary>

Common approaches include a debt you have never heard of, said to be owed by your partner, with pressure to pay quickly; an offer to recover unclaimed assets for a fee up front; an investment for the lump sum from someone who made contact; a friendship or romance that starts online in the months after and later involves money; and impersonation of a bank, pension scheme or government office asking to confirm details.

</details>

<details class="quick-check">
<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: a caller says your late partner owed €3,000 and gives a number to ring to arrange payment. What are the three habits here?</summary>

Decide nothing that day; find the creditor's contact details yourself rather than using the number given; and tell a trusted person before paying anything. A debt in your partner's sole name may also be the estate's, not yours.

</details>

## Survivor rules differ by country

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Survivor pensions, partners, tax and property rights across countries</summary>

- **Survivor pensions.** Common in state and workplace systems across continental Europe, often around half the partner's pension and sometimes reduced by the survivor's own income; narrower where retirement relies on individual accounts, where what passes is the balance.
- **Unmarried partners.** Some systems pay survivor benefits to registered or long-term cohabiting partners; many pay only to a legal spouse.
- **Inheritance tax.** Spouses are exempt or heavily favoured in most countries that have the tax; unmarried partners frequently are not.
- **Widows' property rights.** In some legal and customary systems a widow's claim to the home and land is weak, and her husband's relatives may take them; ownership records and a locally valid will may improve her position, though marital-property, inheritance, customary-law and enforcement rules decide how much.
- **Remarriage.** In some systems a survivor pension stops on remarriage or on moving in with a new partner, which is worth knowing before either.

</details>

Grief does not run to a timetable, and the paperwork does not wait for it. The kindest thing a financial plan can do is keep the urgent list short and give permission for everything else to wait.
