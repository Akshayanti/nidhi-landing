The Mastery post on generational wealth looks at money across lifetimes: how wealth is passed on, and how to make a transfer last. It treats a death as the moment the plan is carried out.

This post is about the person left standing at that moment.

Losing a partner is two events at once. One is grief. The other is a financial restructuring: a household built on two people, and often two incomes, has to be rebuilt around one. The second would be demanding in a good year. It arrives in the worst one.

The post on [windfalls](/blog/windfall-management/) made a related observation about inheritance: money that arrives with a loss is the hardest money to handle well.

What helps most is knowing, before it happens, how the tasks divide. A few things have deadlines. Most do not. Telling them apart is the whole skill.

Survivor benefits, inheritance procedure, and tax differ in every country, and between married and unmarried partners. This post teaches what to check. It cannot tell you what you are entitled to.

## Part one: two lists

### What has a deadline

These need doing in the first days and weeks. If you can, give most of them to someone else. A friend or relative who asks what they can do can do this.

**The first days**

- **Register the death** and order several certified copies of the death certificate. Every bank, insurer, and pension scheme will want one. Ten is not too many.
- **Find the will,** and any instructions about the funeral.
- **Secure cash for two to three months.** Accounts in the dead person's sole name are often frozen until the estate is dealt with. Joint accounts usually keep working. If the household money was in your partner's name, tell the bank at once and ask what it will release for funeral costs and living expenses.

**The first weeks**

- **Notify the employer.** Ask about final pay, any death-in-service benefit, and what happens to health cover that came through the job.
- **Notify every pension scheme and the state pension system.** Ask each the same question: is anything payable to a survivor, and is there a time limit for claiming?
- **Notify insurers.** Claim on life policies. Tell home and car insurers so that cover continues in your name.
- **Keep the essentials paid.** Rent or mortgage, utilities, insurance premiums. A lapsed policy or a missed mortgage payment creates a second problem.
- **Check the tax deadlines.** Many countries have a time limit for reporting a death to the tax authority and for filing an inheritance return.

A **survivor benefit** is income or a lump sum paid to you because your partner has died. It can come from the state, from a workplace pension, or from social insurance. Some of these expire if not claimed within a set period, which is why they sit on this list.

**Probate** is the legal process that confirms a will and gives someone authority to deal with the estate. It goes by other names elsewhere, and it can take months. Two kinds of asset usually bypass it: anything owned jointly with a right of survivorship, and any pension or policy with a named beneficiary. Those are often what a survivor lives on in the meantime.

### What should wait

Everything on this list feels urgent in the first weeks. None of it is.

- Selling the home, or buying another
- Moving to be near family
- Investing a life insurance payout or a pension lump sum
- Paying off the mortgage with the payout
- Giving or lending money to children or relatives
- Leaving a job or retiring early
- Changing whose name assets are in
- Any financial product someone is offering to sell you

A widely used guideline is **no major, irreversible decisions for six to twelve months.**

The reason is practical. Grief affects concentration, memory, and judgement for a long time. People in the first year describe making decisions they can barely remember. The big choices made early, selling a family home in the third month or handing a lump sum to a persuasive adviser, are the ones most often regretted.

The windfalls post recommended parking any large sum for 30 to 90 days before deciding. After a death, make it longer. A payout sitting in an ordinary insured savings account for a year loses a little to inflation. That is a small price for a decision made with a clear head.

### What to say when pressed

Relatives, advisers, and sales people will push. One sentence handles all of them:

*"I am not making any financial decisions until next spring."*

Pick a date and use it. People who have your interests at heart will accept it.

## Part two: the arithmetic of one

When the first weeks are over, there is a budget to rebuild. It helps to see why it is harder than it looks.

### Costs fall by less than half

Two people do not spend twice what one does. The rent is the same. So are the heating, the internet, the car, and the home insurance.

The post on [one-income households](/blog/solo-agers-and-single-income-households/) introduced the statistical adjustment for this: one person needs about **70%** of what a couple spends to live at the same standard.

### Income can fall by much more

How far income falls depends on where it came from and on whether the relationship was legally recognised.

Take a retired couple. One has a pension of €1,400 a month and the other €1,100. Together they have €2,500 and spend €2,300.

The partner with the larger pension dies. The survivor's costs fall to about 70% of €2,300: roughly **€1,600**.

| The survivor's position | Income after the death | Monthly costs | Surplus or shortfall |
|---|---|---|---|
| Married, scheme pays a 55% survivor pension | €1,100 + €770 = €1,870 | €1,600 | +€270 |
| Married, scheme pays no survivor pension | €1,100 | €1,600 | -€500 |
| Not married, no survivor benefit | €1,100 | €1,600 | -€500 |

The figures are illustrative. Survivor pensions range from nothing to the full amount, and 55% is one example of a common level.

The same couple, the same loss, and a difference of €770 a month for life, decided by a rule in a pension scheme and a line on a certificate. The posts on [unmarried couples](/blog/unmarried-and-cohabiting-couples/) and on [marriages that are not recognised](/blog/when-your-marriage-is-not-recognised-abroad/) explain why that line matters and what to put in place while both partners are alive.

For a younger survivor the pattern is sharper. A working partner's salary stops completely. Children's costs continue. Childcare that the couple shared may now have to be bought.

### What to check

- **The state system.** Survivor pensions, bereavement payments, and benefits for children. Eligibility often depends on marriage, on age, on children, and on the contributions your partner made.
- **Every workplace pension,** including schemes from jobs your partner left years ago. Many pay a survivor's pension or a refund of contributions.
- **Life insurance,** including cover attached to a mortgage, a credit card, a bank account, or an employer.
- **Debts.** In most systems, debts in the dead person's sole name are paid from their estate and are not inherited by the survivor personally. Joint debts remain yours. Do not pay a debt from your own money because a collector says you must. Check first.
- **Your own position.** Your will probably names the person who has died. So may your beneficiary forms and your powers of attorney. These belong on the wait-a-few-months list, and they do need doing.

### Rebuilding the plan

After six to twelve months, the restructuring can begin. It follows the same order as the post on [divorce and separation](/blog/divorce-and-separation-finances/), for a different reason:

1. **A new budget** on the new income, from actual figures.
2. **A cash flow forecast** for the next two years, including the one-off costs that follow a death. The post on [cash flow forecasting](/blog/cash-flow-forecasting/) shows how.
3. **The emergency fund,** resized for one income.
4. **The home.** Whether to stay is a real question. It is a better one in month twelve than in month two.
5. **The lump sum.** Now it can be allocated.
6. **Your own will, beneficiaries, and powers of attorney.**

### If your partner handled the money

In many couples one person runs the finances. When that person dies, the survivor inherits a system they have never seen.

Start with a list: every account, policy, pension, and regular payment you can find, from bank statements, post, email, and tax returns. Professional help is worth paying for here. Choose an adviser who charges a fee for their time, and not one paid by commission on products sold to you. Anyone recommending that you move or invest money in the first months is giving you the wrong advice, however kind they are.

If you are reading this while your partner is alive and you are the one who manages the money, the most useful thing you can do is to write that list now and show them where it is.

## The vulnerability window

Deaths are published. A payout may be on the way. The survivor is exhausted and often dealing with money alone for the first time.

Fraudsters know all of this. The post on [scams and fraud](/blog/recognizing-and-avoiding-financial-scams-and-fraud/) covers the general warning signs. These are the approaches aimed at the newly bereaved:

- **A debt you have never heard of,** said to be owed by your partner, with pressure to pay quickly.
- **An offer to recover unclaimed assets,** for a fee up front.
- **An investment for the lump sum,** from someone who contacts you.
- **A friendship or romance** that starts online in the months after the death and later involves a request for money.
- **Impersonation** of a bank, a pension scheme, or a government office, asking you to confirm details.

Three habits make you hard to reach:

- **Nothing is decided on the day it is proposed.** The spring sentence works here too.
- **You make the call.** Look up the organisation's number yourself. Never use contact details supplied by the person who got in touch.
- **One other person knows.** Tell a trusted friend or relative about any request for money before acting on it. Fraud depends on secrecy.

## Cross-continent notes

**Survivor pensions.** Common in state and workplace systems across continental Europe, often at around half the dead partner's pension, sometimes reduced by the survivor's own income. Narrower in countries that rely on individual retirement accounts, where what passes is the account balance.

**Unmarried partners.** Some systems pay survivor benefits to registered or long-term cohabiting partners. Many pay only to a legal spouse.

**Inheritance tax.** Spouses are exempt or heavily favoured in most countries that have the tax. Unmarried partners frequently are not.

**Widows' property rights.** In some legal and customary systems a widow's claim to the home and land is weak, and her husband's relatives may take them. Where that risk exists, title in her own name and a will made during the marriage are the protection.

**Remarriage.** In some systems a survivor pension stops on remarriage or on moving in with a new partner. It is worth knowing before either.

## Getting started

If this has just happened to you:

- **Do the deadline list, and only that.** Hand off what you can.
- **Put any lump sum in an ordinary savings account.**
- **Choose your date** for decisions, and say it to everyone who asks.

If it has not, and you have a partner:

- **Write the list** of accounts, policies, and pensions, and show each other where it is.
- **Check every beneficiary form.**
- **Find out what each pension pays a survivor,** and to whom.
- **Make sure each of you could reach cash** if the other's accounts were frozen.

Grief does not run to a timetable, and the paperwork does not wait for it. The kindest thing a financial plan can do is to make the urgent list short, and to give permission for everything else to wait.
