The post on [unmarried and cohabiting couples](/blog/unmarried-and-cohabiting-couples/) was about building a shared financial life on purpose: who owns what, who inherits, who decides. This post is about taking one apart.

A separation is the reverse of forming a household. One set of accounts, one home, one pension plan, and one budget have to become two. It usually has to happen while both people are under strain, which is the worst condition for careful arithmetic.

The legal side of divorce differs in every country and is not covered here. This post is not about custody, how maintenance is calculated, or court procedure. It covers the part that is much the same everywhere: the mechanics of dividing money, and the mistakes that cost the most.

More than any other post in this series, this one comes with a plain instruction. Decisions made here are hard to reverse and some carry real tax consequences. Use a qualified family lawyer where you live, and for pensions and property, a financial professional who works on separations.

## Part one: the order of operations

### Inventory before negotiation

The first step is a list, made before any conversation about who gets what.

For every asset and every debt, record four things:

- **What it is and where it is held.** Accounts, investments, pensions, property, vehicles, a business, valuables. Loans, cards, overdrafts, tax owed.
- **Whose name it is in.** Yours, your partner's, or both.
- **When it was acquired.** Before the relationship, during it, or by gift or inheritance. In many systems this changes how it is treated.
- **What it is worth now.** With a statement, valuation, or document to support it.

Pensions deserve particular care. They are often the largest asset after the home and the easiest to forget, because nobody sees a balance day to day. Ask each scheme for a current transfer or cash-equivalent value.

An inventory does two jobs. It stops assets being overlooked. And it turns a conversation about fairness into a conversation about a specific list, which is easier to have.

### Secure, do not hide

While the inventory is being built, people commonly take a few protective steps, subject to local law and any court orders:

- Copies of statements for every account, going back at least a year.
- Their own credit report, where one exists, to see every debt in their name, including joint ones they may have forgotten.
- An account in their sole name for their own income.
- Agreed limits on joint credit lines, or a lawyer's view on them, so new debt is not run up in both names.

There is a line here. Documenting and protecting is sensible. Moving, hiding, or spending down shared assets is not. Courts in most countries look back at transactions made around a separation and can reverse them or penalise the person who made them.

### Know which system applies

Married couples do not start from a blank page. Each country has a default regime for who owns what, and the starting point for a split comes from it. Three broad families cover most of the world.

| System | The default idea | Where it is common |
|---|---|---|
| Community property | Most things acquired during the marriage belong to both equally | Several states in the United States, France, Spain, much of Latin America |
| Equitable distribution | A court divides property fairly, which may not mean equally | Most states in the United States, England and Wales |
| Separate property with equalisation | Each keeps their own property, and the gains made during the marriage are evened out | Germany and Switzerland, with related forms elsewhere in Europe |

Two points matter more than the labels.

First, these are defaults. A marriage contract, where one was signed, can replace them.

Second, the regime usually distinguishes what was built during the marriage from what each person brought in or inherited. That is why the "when it was acquired" column in the inventory matters.

### If you were never married

None of these regimes applies to couples who lived together without marrying. In most places the starting point is ordinary property law: whose name is on it, plus any written agreement. The post on [unmarried couples](/blog/unmarried-and-cohabiting-couples/) explains why a cohabitation agreement matters for exactly this reason. Some countries do give long-term partners claims on separation, so check the local rule.

Everything in part two applies whether you were married or not.

## Part two: the three expensive mistakes

### Mistake one: treating equal values as equal assets

A settlement that gives each person "€200,000" sounds even. Whether it is depends on what the €200,000 is made of.

Consider three assets, each worth €200,000 on paper.

| Asset | Paper value | What reduces it | Roughly what it is worth to you | When you can use it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cash and savings | €200,000 | Nothing | €200,000 | Now |
| Equity in a €400,000 home | €200,000 | Selling costs of about 6% of the price (€24,000), plus upkeep while you hold it | About €176,000 if sold | When sold, or by borrowing against it |
| Pension | €200,000 | Tax on withdrawal, at say 20% (€40,000) | About €160,000 | At retirement age |

The figures are illustrative. Selling costs and pension taxation vary widely by country and by person. The pattern does not. Three assets with the same headline number differ by tens of thousands of euros in real value, and by decades in when the money can be used.

This cuts in more than one direction. A pension that is locked away and taxed later is also growing untaxed in the meantime, which has value for a younger person. A home is worth more than its sale price to someone who needs to live in it.

The point is to compare like with like. For every asset on the table, ask three questions:

- What is it worth after tax?
- What does it cost to hold or to sell?
- When can I actually use it?

The post on [mental accounting](/blog/mental-accounting/) described how people treat money differently depending on the label attached to it. A separation is where that habit is most expensive. "I kept the house" feels like winning. Whether it is depends on the arithmetic.

### The house in particular

There are three options for a shared home, and each has a different cash consequence.

**One person buys the other out.** The person staying needs to raise the other's share, usually by taking the mortgage into their sole name. The lender will assess that on one income. The monthly payment that two incomes carried easily can be unaffordable on one.

**Sell and split.** The cleanest break. Both people pay selling costs and both need somewhere new to live, at current prices and current interest rates.

**Keep co-owning for a time.** Often chosen so that children can stay put. It keeps two people financially tied together, so it needs a written agreement covering who pays what, who maintains it, and what triggers a sale.

Before choosing to keep the home, run a [cash flow forecast](/blog/cash-flow-forecasting/) on the new single income. Include the mortgage, insurance, property tax, and maintenance. A home that consumes most of one income leaves nothing for a pension or an emergency fund. The post on [property as an investment](/blog/real-estate-as-investment/) covers the running costs that are easy to underestimate.

### Mistake two: splitting a retirement account the wrong way

The post on [tax-advantaged accounts](/blog/tax-advantaged-accounts/) explained that retirement accounts get favourable tax treatment in exchange for rules about when money can come out.

Those rules do not pause for a divorce.

If the settlement says your spouse receives half your pension, and you respond by withdrawing half and transferring the cash, you walk into the trap the [account consolidation post](/blog/account-consolidation-and-financial-data-hygiene/) described for job changes: pension money paid out to you stops being pension money. Most tax systems see a withdrawal by you, with tax, possibly a penalty, and the loss of the tax-advantaged status for good.

Most countries with these accounts provide a formal mechanism to avoid this. A legal order or procedure moves part of one person's pension into the other's name and keeps it inside the tax-advantaged system.

- In the United States it is a **qualified domestic relations order** (a court order instructing a retirement plan to pay a share to a former spouse).
- In England and Wales it is a **pension sharing order**.
- In Germany, pension rights built during the marriage are equalised as part of the divorce itself.

The names differ. The principle is the same: pensions are divided through the mechanism designed for it, not by taking the money out. Find out what it is called where you live before anything is signed. This is the step in this post where a professional most clearly pays for themselves.

### Mistake three: leaving the old paperwork in place

A settlement divides assets. It does not update forms. Three kinds of paperwork stay exactly as they were unless someone changes them.

**Beneficiary designations.** The form on a pension or a life insurance policy often overrides a will, and in many systems a divorce does not cancel it. People die with a former spouse still named, and the money goes to them. Most people update every beneficiary form as soon as they are legally free to; some courts restrict changes while proceedings are open, so the timing is worth checking with a lawyer.

**Wills and powers of attorney.** A former partner may still be your executor, your heir, and the person authorised to make medical decisions for you. Rules on whether divorce revokes these differ by country, which is why many people replace them rather than rely on the rule.

**Joint debts.** A settlement can say that one of you will pay a joint loan. The lender did not sign the settlement. If payments stop, the lender can pursue either of you for the full amount, and the missed payments appear on both credit records. Paying joint debts off or refinancing them into one name, where affordable, and closing joint accounts and cards is the usual way to end that exposure.

## Rebuilding on one income

Once the division is done, the plan that ran on two incomes has to be rebuilt for one.

- **A new budget from zero.** Two homes cost more than one. Start from your actual single income and actual costs, not from half the old budget.
- **An emergency fund in your own name.** On one income, with no partner's salary as a fallback, many people aim for a larger fund than before. The post on [one-income households](/blog/solo-agers-and-single-income-households/) explains why and by how much.
- **Insurance.** Health cover that came through a spouse's employer may end. Life and income protection needs change, especially if you now support children alone.
- **Credit in your own name.** If loans and cards were mostly in your partner's name, your own file may be thin. In countries with US or UK style credit scoring, a card in your sole name, paid in full each month, is a common way to start rebuilding it; elsewhere, the local system decides what counts.
- **A new projection.** Retirement plans built on two incomes and one home no longer hold. Rerun the [projection](/blog/financial-projections/) with your new balance, your new savings rate, and your own retirement costs.

Rebuilding takes longer than dividing. Expect the first year to be about stability and the plan to come after.

## Cross-continent notes

**Marriage contracts.** Common and routinely enforced in much of continental Europe. Given weight but not always binding in England and Wales. Enforceability in the United States depends on the state.

**Pensions.** Some countries divide pension rights automatically on divorce. Others require one spouse to ask. In some, state pension entitlements can also be shared.

**The family home.** Several countries protect the right of a spouse, or of children, to stay in the home for a period regardless of whose name is on it.

**Cross-border marriages.** If you married in one country and live in another, more than one country's rules may be arguable. Which court hears the case can change the result, so take advice early.

**Religious and customary marriages.** A marriage made only under religious or customary rites may not be recognised by civil law in the country you live in, which can leave a spouse with the weaker position of an unmarried partner. If that may apply to you, find out before you need to know.

## Getting started

In order:

1. **Build the inventory.** Every asset and debt, with documents.
2. **Get advice.** A family lawyer, before you agree to anything.
3. **Compare assets after tax, costs, and access.** Not by headline value.
4. **Split pensions through the formal mechanism.** Never by withdrawal.
5. **Separate every joint debt and account.**
6. **Change every beneficiary, will, and power of attorney.**
7. **Rebuild the budget, the emergency fund, and the projection on one income.**

A separation is one of the largest financial events most people go through, and it arrives when clear thinking is hardest. The list exists so that the arithmetic does not depend on how you feel that week.
