The post on [geographic arbitrage](/blog/geographic-arbitrage/) treated moving countries as a strategy. Earn in one place, spend in a cheaper one, choose the visa that fits, reach financial independence sooner. It assumed a move that was chosen, funded, and planned.

Most people who cross a border are not running that play. They move for a job they need, to join a spouse, to study, to get away from danger, or because staying was not possible. They arrive without a remote salary or a choice of visas.

Whatever the reason, the same thing happens at the border. The financial infrastructure you spent years building stays behind.

This post is about that reset and how to rebuild. It is a financial planning post. It is not immigration advice, and where status or the right to work is in question, a qualified adviser or a recognised support organisation is the right source.

## What a border resets

Think about what a settled adult has without noticing: a credit file, a bank that knows them, a tax number, a record of pension contributions, a landlord reference, a phone contract. None of it was hard to get, because each piece helped unlock the next.

A border breaks the chain.

| What you had | What happens when you move |
|---|---|
| Credit history | Does not transfer. You arrive with no file. |
| Bank relationship | Usually does not transfer. A new account needs local documents. |
| Proof of address | Gone until you have a tenancy, which may require a bank account. |
| Pension contributions | Stay in the old country's system until claimed, often decades later. |
| Tax residency | May apply in both countries during the year you move. |
| Professional and rental references | Often not accepted from abroad. |

The earlier post on [credit scores](/blog/credit-and-credit-scores/) explained how a score is built from your repayment record. What it did not need to say is that the record is held nationally. The consequence for a newcomer is blunt. A person with two decades of perfect repayment in one country is, to a lender in the next, identical to an eighteen-year-old.

This series calls that a **recognition gap** (the distance between how your life actually works and what an institution is able to see, a term from [When the Default Plan Doesn't Fit You](/blog/when-the-default-plan-does-not-fit-you/)).

## Part one: building a financial identity from zero

Rebuilding has an order, because each step produces the document the next step asks for.

**1. Identification and a tax number.** Everything else hangs on these. Whatever residence document you hold, find out which tax or social insurance number you are entitled to and apply for it first.

**2. A basic bank account.** Ask for a basic or entry-level account by name. Many countries require banks to offer one with lighter document requirements. In the European Union, anyone legally resident has the right to a basic payment account, and that includes people with no fixed address and people seeking asylum. Where a large bank refuses, online banks and credit unions frequently accept a wider range of documents.

**3. Proof of address.** The classic trap is the loop: no account without an address, no tenancy without an account. It usually breaks at one of three points. An employer letter confirming your address. A registration certificate from the local authority. Or a letter from a hostel, shelter, or support organisation, which many banks accept for a basic account.

**4. Income on the record.** Have wages paid into the account. Three to six months of regular deposits is itself a form of financial history, and landlords and lenders will ask for it.

**5. Small, reported credit.** In countries with US or UK style credit scoring, a file is built by borrowing a little and repaying on time. Common starting points are a phone contract in your own name, a secured credit card (one backed by a deposit you provide), or a small credit-builder loan. In some countries rent payments can be reported to the bureaus on request. A common pattern is one recurring bill on the card, paid in full every month, with the rest of the limit unused. Other systems, such as Germany's SCHUFA or the Dutch BKR, work differently and do not require a credit card to build a clean record, so it is worth finding out how the local system scores people first.

**6. Check the file.** After six months, request your credit report. Errors are common for people with foreign names, several addresses, or transliterated spellings, and they are easier to correct early.

Two shortcuts are worth asking about. If your old bank operates in the new country, it may open an account or extend credit based on your record with it. And for some pairs of countries, services exist that translate a foreign credit file into a form local lenders accept.

### Keep the old country's paper trail

Before you leave, or as soon as you can after, collect what will be hard to get later:

- Statements of every pension and social insurance contribution, with your identification number in that system
- Your final tax assessment, and written confirmation of the date you stopped being resident
- Closing statements for bank accounts, or a decision to keep one open
- Your credit report from the old country, for the cases where it can be used

Many countries have agreements that let contribution periods in one country count toward a pension in another. Those agreements run on evidence. A folder you assemble now replaces months of correspondence in thirty years.

## Part two: the cost of sending money home

Around the world, migrants send hundreds of billions each year to family in their countries of origin. For many households it is a fixed monthly commitment, as regular as rent.

It is also one of the most overpriced transactions in everyday finance.

### Where the cost hides

A **remittance** (a transfer of money by a migrant to someone in their home country) has two costs, and only one is visible.

- **The fee.** Stated up front. Often a flat amount, which hits small transfers hardest.
- **The exchange rate margin.** The provider converts your money at a worse rate than the real market rate and keeps the difference. This is rarely shown as a cost and is frequently the larger of the two.

A transfer advertised as having "zero fees" can still be expensive if the rate is poor. The post on [managing money across currencies](/blog/managing-money-across-currencies/) covers how these margins work.

The only comparison that captures both is this: **for a fixed amount sent, how much does the recipient actually receive?**

### The route matters more than the brand

The cost of a transfer depends heavily on its **corridor**: the specific pair of sending and receiving countries. A busy corridor with many competing providers is cheap. A thin one with few options is expensive. The same company can be among the cheapest on one corridor and among the dearest on another.

The World Bank tracks prices across hundreds of corridors. The global average cost of sending the equivalent of $200 has been a little over 6% in recent years. The cheapest providers on competitive corridors charge a small fraction of that. The internationally agreed target is 3%.

### What the difference adds up to

Take a household sending €300 a month.

| Cost of each transfer | Lost per month | Lost per year | Lost over 10 years |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.5% (around the global average) | €19.50 | €234 | €2,340 |
| 2% (a competitive digital provider) | €6.00 | €72 | €720 |
| **Difference** | **€13.50** | **€162** | **€1,620** |

€1,620 is more than five months of the remittance itself. It buys nothing for the sender or the family. It is simply the price of not comparing.

### Four ways to bring the cost down

- **Compare the amount received, every few months.** Pricing changes and new providers enter corridors regularly.
- **Prefer account-to-account or mobile wallet transfers** over cash handed across a counter at both ends. Cash handling is the expensive part.
- **Send larger amounts less often** where the recipient can manage it. Fixed fees take a smaller share of a larger transfer.
- **Avoid paying by credit card.** Card issuers often treat a transfer as a cash advance, with its own fee and interest from day one.

Treat the remittance as a fixed line in your budget, next to rent. It is an obligation, not leftover money, and planning for it as one makes the rest of the budget honest.

## Tax in two countries

In the year you move, two countries may each consider you resident for tax. Most pairs of countries have a treaty to prevent the same income being taxed twice, with rules to decide which country goes first. Relief under a treaty often has to be claimed; it does not always happen by itself.

Three habits reduce the risk:

- **Record your dates.** Arrival, departure, and days spent in each country.
- **Keep evidence of where you lived and worked.** Tenancy agreements, employment contracts, travel records.
- **Declare foreign accounts where required.** Many countries require residents to report accounts held abroad, and penalties for not doing so can apply even when no tax was due.

The geographic arbitrage post goes further into tax residency. For anything beyond the simplest case, many people find a local tax professional in the year of the move worth the fee.

## When the move was not a choice

The plan above assumes documents, a legal right to work, and time. Many people have fewer of these.

**Refugees and people seeking asylum.** The first obstacle is usually identification. Find out which document your status provides and which institutions are obliged to accept it. Support organisations often know which local banks open accounts on it. Work rights may be restricted at first, which makes recording any permitted income especially valuable later.

**Family migrants.** A person who arrives on a partner's visa often has no income, no account, and no credit file in their own name. Every product sits in the sponsor's name. An account of your own, your name on the tenancy and on at least one bill, and a small credit line in your own name are protections as well as conveniences. If the relationship ends or the partner dies, a financial identity of your own is what you will need.

**Households without full legal status.** Banking access depends on the country. Some tax systems issue an identification number for filing that is separate from the right to work; the ITIN (Individual Taxpayer Identification Number) in the United States is one example of that category. Filing and paying tax builds a record that can matter later. Where a bank account is out of reach, cash carries real risks: theft, no proof of savings, and no record of income. Credit unions, community lenders, and rotating savings groups are the usual alternatives, each with its own limits.

**Everyone.** Keep a small reserve you can reach from either country, and a copy of every important document stored somewhere other than your home. ## Cross-continent notes

**European Union.** Legal residents have a right to a basic payment account. Pension periods in different member states are coordinated. Credit files remain national.

**United Kingdom.** Basic accounts are offered by the large banks. Credit history starts from zero. Registering on the electoral roll, where eligible, and a reported phone contract are common first steps.

**United States.** Credit scores drive renting, borrowing, and sometimes hiring. Secured cards and credit-builder loans are the standard starting point. Some lenders accept an ITIN in place of a Social Security number.

**Gulf states.** Residency is tied to employment, accounts may be frozen when a visa is cancelled, and there is usually no route to a local pension for foreign workers (end-of-service gratuity schemes are the closest equivalent). That is why many expatriates there keep savings portable from the start.

**India, the Philippines, Mexico, and other major receiving countries.** Many offer special account types for citizens working abroad, sometimes with tax advantages. They are worth understanding before choosing how to send money home.

## Getting started

- **If you have not moved yet:** build the paper trail folder before you go.
- **If you have just arrived:** tax number, basic account, proof of address, in that order.
- **If you have been here a while:** request your credit report and check it.
- **If you send money home:** price your next transfer with three providers and compare the amount received.

A border takes away a financial identity that was built almost by accident, one routine step at a time. Rebuilding it is the same steps, taken on purpose and in the right order.
