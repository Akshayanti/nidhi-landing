Someone has just moved countries. Back home they had twenty years of on-time repayments. In the new country a lender sees no credit file at all, the bank wants an address before opening an account, and the landlord wants a bank account before signing a lease.

The [geographic arbitrage post](/blog/geographic-arbitrage/) treated moving as a planned strategy. Most people who cross a border are not running that play: they move for a job they need, to join a partner, to study, or because staying was not possible. Whatever the reason, the financial infrastructure built over years stays behind. This post covers rebuilding it and the cost of sending money home. It is not immigration advice: where status or the right to work is in question, a qualified adviser or a recognised support organisation is the right source.

## Checking what credit history and banking carry across a border

A settled adult has things they rarely notice: a credit file, a bank that knows them, a tax number, a pension record, a landlord reference. Each piece helped unlock the next. A border breaks the chain.

| What you had | What happens when you move |
|---|---|
| Credit history | Usually not visible to local lenders. You may arrive with no local file. |
| Bank relationship | Usually does not transfer. A new account needs local documents. |
| Proof of address | Gone until you have a tenancy, which may require a bank account. |
| Pension contributions | Stay in the old country's system until claimed, often decades later. |
| Tax residency | May apply in both countries in the year you move. |
| Professional and rental references | Often not accepted from abroad. |

The [credit scores post](/blog/credit-and-credit-scores/) explained how a score is built from your repayment record. That record is usually held in a national system, so a local lender may see no file even after two decades of on-time repayment elsewhere. This series calls that a [recognition gap](#term-recognition-gap): the distance between how your life works and what an institution can see.

## Rebuilding follows an order

Each step produces the document the next step asks for.

1. **Identification and a tax number.** Everything else hangs on these, so many people find out first which tax or social insurance number their residence document entitles them to.
2. **A basic bank account.** Asking for a [basic payment account](#term-basic-payment-account) by name helps: many countries require banks to offer one with lighter document requirements, and in the European Union, consumers legally resident in the EU generally have a right to one, including people with no fixed address and asylum seekers, subject to identity, anti-money-laundering and other statutory checks. Online banks and credit unions often accept a wider range of documents than large branch banks.
3. **Proof of address.** The loop (no account without an address, no tenancy without an account) usually breaks at one of three points: an employer letter confirming your address, a registration certificate from the local authority, or a letter from a hostel, shelter or support organisation.
4. **Income on the record.** Wages paid into the account; regular deposits create evidence of income, though the period and documents a landlord or lender accepts vary.
5. **Small, reported credit, where the local system works that way.** In US or UK style scoring, a file grows from borrowing a little and repaying on time: a phone contract in your own name, a [secured credit card](#term-secured-credit-card) or a small credit-builder loan. Systems such as Germany's SCHUFA or the Dutch BKR work differently and need no credit card for a clean record, so it helps to learn how the local system scores people first.
6. **Checking the file.** Once a local file exists, checking the report can reveal errors, which are common with foreign names, several addresses or transliterated spellings, and easier to fix early.

<details class="quick-check">
<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: why does the order matter?</summary>

Because each step can supply evidence the next one asks for: identity and tax records help with an account, an account or an accepted address document helps with housing, and recorded income starts a local paper trail.

</details>

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Two shortcuts, and the paper trail to keep from the old country</summary>

**Shortcuts.** If your old bank operates in the new country, it may open an account or extend credit based on your record with it. For some pairs of countries, services translate a foreign credit file into a form local lenders accept.

**The paper trail.** Before leaving, or soon after, it helps to collect what will be hard to get later:

- Statements of every pension and social insurance contribution, with your identification number in that system
- Your final tax assessment, and written confirmation of the date you stopped being resident
- Closing statements for bank accounts, or a decision to keep one open
- Your credit report from the old country, for the cases where it can be used

Many countries have agreements that let contribution periods in one country count toward a pension in another. Those agreements run on evidence: a folder assembled now replaces months of correspondence in thirty years.

</details>

## Comparing remittances by the amount actually received

Migrants send hundreds of billions a year to family in their countries of origin, for many households a fixed monthly commitment as regular as rent. A [remittance](#term-remittance) has two costs, and only one is visible: the **fee**, often a flat amount that hits small transfers hardest, and the [exchange rate margin](#term-exchange-rate-margin), the gap between the market rate and the rate the provider gives you, which is rarely shown and often the larger cost. A "zero fee" transfer can still be expensive (the [currencies post](/blog/managing-money-across-currencies/) covered how margins work). The one comparison that captures both: **for a fixed amount sent, how much does the recipient actually receive?** A practical test is to fix the amount, check at least three providers on the same route, and compare only what arrives in the recipient's currency.

Cost depends on the [corridor](#term-corridor), the pair of sending and receiving countries. Busy corridors with many providers are cheap; thin ones are expensive; the same company can be cheap on one and dear on another. The World Bank's tracking puts the global average cost of sending the equivalent of $200 a little over 6% in recent years, against an internationally agreed target of 3%; the cheapest providers on competitive corridors charge a small fraction of that.

For a household sending €300 a month:

| Cost of each transfer | Lost per month | Lost per year | Lost over 10 years |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6.5% (around the global average) | €19.50 | €234 | €2,340 |
| 2% (a competitive digital provider) | €6.00 | €72 | €720 |
| **Difference** | **€13.50** | **€162** | **€1,620** |

€1,620 is more than five months of the remittance itself, and it buys nothing for the sender or the family.

<details class="quick-check">
<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: one provider charges no fee, another charges €3. Which is cheaper?</summary>

You cannot tell from the fee. Send the same amount through both on paper and compare what the recipient would receive in their currency; the exchange rate margin often outweighs the fee.

</details>

Common ways people bring the cost down: comparing the amount received every few months, since prices change and providers enter corridors; account-to-account or mobile wallet transfers rather than cash at both ends; larger, less frequent transfers where the recipient can manage it, so fixed fees take a smaller share; and not paying by credit card, which issuers often treat as a cash advance with its own fee and interest from day one. Many households also budget the remittance as a fixed line next to rent, an obligation rather than leftover money.

## When the move was not a choice

The order above assumes documents, a legal right to work and time. Many people have fewer of these. For refugees and people seeking asylum, the first obstacle is usually identification and work rights. For family migrants on a partner's visa, the gap is often an account, a tenancy and a credit file in their own name. For households without full legal status, banking access depends on the country, and cash carries specific risks.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Refugees and family migrants</summary>

**Refugees and people seeking asylum.** The first obstacle is usually identification: which document your status provides and which institutions must accept it. Support organisations often know which local banks open accounts on it. Work rights may be restricted at first, which makes recording any permitted income especially valuable later.

**Family migrants.** Someone who arrives on a partner's visa often has no income, no account and no credit file in their own name, with every product in the sponsor's name. An account of their own, their name on the tenancy and on at least one bill, and a small credit line in their own name are protections as well as conveniences: if the relationship ends or the partner dies, a financial identity of their own is what they will need.

**Everyone.** A small reserve reachable from either country, and copies of important documents stored somewhere other than home. </details>

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Households without full legal status</summary>

Banking access depends on the country. Some tax systems issue an identification number for filing that is separate from the right to work; the US ITIN (Individual Taxpayer Identification Number) is one example. A tax filing may create a useful record, but whether someone must or can file, and what that record affects, needs advice for their status and country. Where a bank account is out of reach, cash carries real risks: theft, no proof of savings, no record of income. Credit unions, community lenders and [rotating savings groups](#term-rotating-savings-group) are the usual alternatives, each with its own limits.

</details>

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Tax residency in the year you move</summary>

In the year you move, two countries may each treat you as a [tax resident](#term-tax-residency). Some country pairs have a tax treaty that allocates taxing rights or provides relief from double taxation, but many do not. Even where a treaty applies, relief often has to be claimed rather than happening automatically. Three habits reduce the risk: recording arrival and departure dates and days in each country; keeping evidence of where you lived and worked (tenancy agreements, employment contracts, travel records); and declaring foreign accounts where required, since penalties can apply even when no tax was due. The geographic arbitrage post goes further into residency tests; beyond the simplest case, many people find a local tax professional worth the fee in the year of the move.

</details>

## Accounts and records by region

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Accounts for citizens working abroad</summary>

India, the Philippines, Mexico and other major receiving countries offer special accounts for citizens working abroad, sometimes with tax advantages, worth understanding before choosing how to send money home.

</details>

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Banking, credit and pensions by region</summary>

- **European Union.** Legal residents generally have a right to a basic payment account, subject to identity and anti-money-laundering checks. Pension periods in different member states are coordinated. Credit files remain national.
- **United Kingdom.** Large banks offer basic accounts. Credit history starts from zero; registering on the electoral roll, where eligible, and a reported phone contract are common first steps.
- **United States.** Credit scores drive renting, borrowing and sometimes hiring. Secured cards and credit-builder loans are the usual start. Some lenders accept an ITIN in place of a Social Security number.
- **Gulf states.** Residency is tied to employment, accounts may be frozen when a visa is cancelled, and foreign workers usually have no route to a local pension (end-of-service gratuity schemes are the closest equivalent), which is why many keep savings portable from the start.

</details>

A border takes away a financial identity that was built almost by accident, one routine step at a time. Rebuilding it is the same steps, taken on purpose and in order.
