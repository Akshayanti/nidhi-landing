Sofia and Lena have been together for twelve years. They live in a flat Lena bought before they met, and Sofia has paid half of every mortgage payment since she moved in. They have never married and never written a will. Lena dies in an accident at 41.

In many countries, this is what happens next. If the local inheritance order puts Lena's parents next, the flat may pass to them, and Sofia may have no automatic right to stay. She might try to prove a claim from twelve years of mortgage payments, but without anything in writing the result is uncertain and expensive, and the payments can look, legally, like a gift. Lena's pension pays nothing, because its rules name a spouse. Depending on local law and hospital rules, staff may turn to Lena's mother rather than Sofia. If Lena's parents give Sofia a share of the flat, it may be taxed as a gift between unrelated people. Nobody intended any of this; it is what the defaults produce when a couple is not married.

The [insurance optimisation post](/blog/insurance-optimization/) noted in passing that a spouse is often the default beneficiary while an unmarried partner has to be named. This post is the full version of that sentence, for couples who have chosen not to marry and couples for whom marriage is not available. The legal instruments have different names and force in every country, so it teaches the categories; for the documents themselves, a local professional is the usual route.

## What marriage switches on automatically

A wedding is a legal event as well as a personal one. On that day a set of defaults, what happens when you make no choice, begins to apply. Eight are common:

| What marriage usually gives automatically | What an unmarried partner usually gets |
|---|---|
| Inheritance if there is no will | Nothing, in many jurisdictions |
| Survivor benefits from state and workplace pensions | Often nothing, unless the scheme allows a nomination |
| Authority to make medical decisions | None; next of kin is a blood relative |
| Joint or favourable tax treatment | Taxed as two unrelated individuals |
| Tax-free or low-tax gifts and inheritance between partners | Often the rates for unrelated people |
| A defined legal regime for shared property | Whoever's name is on the asset owns it |
| A framework for dividing assets if the relationship ends | No framework; ordinary property and contract law |
| A route to residency in a partner's country | Frequently none |

[The first post in this series](/blog/when-the-default-plan-does-not-fit-you/) argued that every [default assumption](#term-default-assumption) carries a guess about who you are. Here the guess is simple: a couple is a married couple.

## Living together rarely creates the rights of marriage

Many couples believe that living together long enough creates the rights of marriage. In most places it does not. England and Wales have no such status, however long a couple has lived together. In the United States, only a small number of states recognise common-law marriage, and they require more than living together. Australia, New Zealand and several Canadian provinces do give long-term cohabiting couples substantial rights after a qualifying period. Registered and civil partnerships, where they exist, cover varying parts of the bundle: some give joint tax treatment but no automatic inheritance.

The distance between how a couple lives and what the law sees is a [recognition gap](#term-recognition-gap). The way to find yours is to check each row of the table for the country you live in.

## Five documents rebuild most of those protections

Five documents rebuild or strengthen most rows. They cannot make a spouse-only pension pay an unmarried partner, create married tax treatment or provide a partner-residency route, so those gaps need separate planning.

1. **A will** answers the inheritance row. Where [forced heirship](#term-forced-heirship) reserves a share for children or other relatives, a will cannot give a partner everything, but it can give the part that is free to give. Pensions, life insurance and jointly owned property often pass outside the will, so they need their own arrangements.
2. **A [beneficiary form](#term-beneficiary-form)** on a pension, life policy or some investment accounts decides or guides who receives that money outside the estate; depending on the scheme it may be binding or only a guide for its trustees. The common check: is anyone named, is it your partner or a parent or former partner from years ago, and does the scheme let you name an unmarried partner at all? Some pension schemes pay survivor benefits only to a spouse, a gap to cover another way. Checking is usually free and quick, and it is the item most often missed; the scheme's rules decide whether a nomination is allowed and how much force it has.
3. **A clear record of how you own your home.** In common-law countries, [joint tenancy](#term-joint-tenancy) means the whole property passes to the survivor automatically, whatever a will says; under [tenancy in common](#term-tenancy-in-common), each owns a share, which can be unequal, and each share passes under its owner's will or to their relatives. Neither is universally better: joint tenancy passes ownership to the survivor automatically, while tenancy in common records separate shares that can go to someone else, such as children from an earlier relationship. Tax, creditor, mortgage and forced-heirship rules can change the result. Civil-law countries use different concepts, and some offer clauses giving a surviving co-owner the whole property.
4. **A [cohabitation agreement](#term-cohabitation-agreement),** a contract recording who owns what, who contributes what to the home and what that earns, how shared debts are split, and what happens to the home and shared property on separation. It matters most where contributions are unequal: one owns the flat, one earns much more, one stops work to raise children. Enforceability varies by country, and agreements carry more weight when each partner has had independent advice.
5. **A [power of attorney](#term-power-of-attorney)** for money and one for health (a healthcare power of attorney is also called a healthcare proxy). Without them, an unmarried partner can be shut out of the hospital room and the bank account. Each partner usually needs their own set, naming the other.

<details class="quick-check">
<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: the home question. What three things decide what happens to a shared home if one partner dies?</summary>

How it is owned and in whose name; whether a share goes to the other partner automatically or to the heirs; and who has paid what, written down.

</details>

For Sofia and Lena, with one name on the title, three items would have changed much of what happened: a will, a recorded ownership share for Sofia, and powers of attorney.

## Tax treatment is a gap to plan around, not fix

One row resists paperwork. In many tax systems, married couples can file jointly, pass assets to each other freely during life and leave everything to each other with little or no inheritance tax, while unmarried partners are often taxed as strangers, with small allowances and high rates. No form changes that, but people plan around it:

- **Insurance to pay the bill.** A life policy one partner owns on the other's life, sized to the expected tax, means the survivor need not sell the home to pay it. Who owns the policy matters, so it is usually set up with advice.
- **How assets are held.** Ownership from the start changes what passes on death, though adding an owner later can itself be a taxable gift, and survivorship does not necessarily remove inheritance tax. Local rules decide.
- **Pension and insurance benefits.** Some systems tax these differently from estate assets, though naming a beneficiary does not by itself guarantee better treatment.
- **Lifetime gifts within allowances,** where yearly allowances exist.

Whether the saving from marrying or registering a partnership outweighs a couple's reasons for not doing so is a personal decision; knowing the number helps.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Shared money while you are both alive</summary>

**Joint debts are owed in full by each of you.** A joint loan or joint account overdraft is usually a case of [joint and several liability](#term-joint-and-several-liability): the lender can pursue either partner for the whole amount, not half, and separating does not change that.

**Accounts in one name belong to that person.** If the household savings sit in one partner's account, the other has no automatic claim to them. Splitting savings between two names, or a joint account for shared goals, keeps the record matching reality.

</details>

## Country rules decide which protections apply

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Country notes from England to Australia</summary>

- **England and Wales.** No cohabitation status. Wills, declared shares in property and pension nominations do the work.
- **France.** A civil partnership (PACS) gives joint income tax treatment but no automatic inheritance, so partners need wills.
- **Germany, Italy, Spain.** Forced heirship limits how much a will can leave to a partner, and tax allowances for unmarried partners are far smaller than for spouses.
- **Australia, New Zealand, parts of Canada.** De facto partners gain significant property and inheritance rights after a qualifying period. Documents still help, because they remove the need to prove the relationship.
- **United States.** Rules are set state by state. Beneficiary forms carry particular weight because so much wealth sits in retirement accounts.

</details>

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Marriage unavailable or unrecognised</summary>

Where marriage is available, the choice is the same for any couple. Where it is not, or where a marriage made elsewhere is not recognised, every item in this post matters much more, because no route switches the bundle on. A later post in this series is devoted to that situation.

</details>

Marriage is one way to get these protections. It is not the only way; it is the only one that happens without anyone deciding.
