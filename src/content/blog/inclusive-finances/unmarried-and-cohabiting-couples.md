The post on [insurance optimisation](/blog/insurance-optimization/) said life insurance can be dropped once a spouse could maintain their lifestyle without you. It added one sentence in passing: a legal spouse is often the default beneficiary, and an unmarried partner has to be named deliberately. This post is the full version of that sentence, because it is true of far more than insurance. A legal spouse is usually the automatic beneficiary of almost everything. The policy, the pension, the home.

An unmarried partner is usually the automatic beneficiary of nothing.

Sofia and Lena have been together for twelve years. They live in a flat Lena bought before they met. Sofia has paid half of every mortgage payment since she moved in. They have never married and have never written a will.

Lena dies in an accident at 41.

In many countries, this is what happens next. The flat passes to Lena's parents. Sofia has no automatic right to stay in it. The twelve years of payments she made are, legally, generosity. Lena's pension pays nothing, because the rules name a spouse. The hospital spoke to Lena's mother, not to Sofia. And if Lena's parents choose to give Sofia a share of the flat, it may be taxed as a gift between people who are unrelated.

Nobody intended any of this. It is simply what the defaults produce when a couple is not married.

This post sets out what marriage switches on automatically and how to build each piece on purpose. It covers couples who have chosen not to marry and couples for whom marriage is not available. The legal instruments have different names and different force in every country, so what follows teaches the categories. For the documents themselves, a local professional is the usual route.

## Part one: the bundle marriage switches on

A wedding is a legal event as well as a personal one. On that day a set of **defaults** (what happens when you make no choice) begins to apply.

| What marriage usually gives automatically | What an unmarried partner usually gets |
|---|---|
| Inheritance if there is no will | Nothing, in many jurisdictions |
| Survivor benefits from state and workplace pensions | Often nothing, unless the scheme allows a nomination |
| Authority to make medical decisions | None; next of kin is a blood relative |
| Joint or favourable tax treatment | Taxed as two unrelated individuals |
| Tax-free or low-tax gifts and inheritance between partners | Often the rates for unrelated people |
| A defined legal regime for shared property | Whoever's name is on the asset owns it |
| A framework for dividing assets if the relationship ends | No framework; ordinary property and contract law |
| A route to residency in a partner's country | Frequently none |

The idea behind this series, introduced in [When the Default Plan Doesn't Fit You](/blog/when-the-default-plan-does-not-fit-you/), is that every default carries an assumption about who you are. Here the assumption is simple: a couple is a married couple.

### The common-law marriage myth

Many couples believe that living together for long enough creates the rights of marriage. In most places it does not.

- England and Wales have no such status, however long a couple has lived together.
- In the United States, only a small number of states recognise common-law marriage, and they require more than shared living.
- Australia, New Zealand, and several Canadian provinces do give long-term cohabiting couples substantial rights after a qualifying period.
- Registered and civil partnerships, where they exist, cover varying parts of the bundle. Some give joint tax treatment but no automatic inheritance.

The gap between how a couple lives and what the law sees is a **recognition gap**. The way to find yours is to take the table above and check each row for the country you live in. Do not assume.

## Part two: the five documents that rebuild it

Each row of the bundle can be replaced by a **deliberate designation**: a written choice that does what the default would have done.

### 1. A will

A will is the direct answer to the inheritance row. It names who receives what you own.

Two things to know:

- **It has limits in some countries.** Where **forced heirship** applies (a share of the estate reserved by law for children or other relatives), a will cannot give everything to a partner. It can still give the part that is free to give.
- **It does not control everything.** Pensions, life insurance, and jointly owned property often pass outside the will. Those need their own designations.

Estate planning as a whole (arranging in advance what happens to what you own) gets a full post later in this series. For an unmarried couple, the will is the piece that cannot wait.

### 2. Beneficiary designations

Pensions, life insurance, and many investment accounts ask you to name who receives the money if you die. That form usually overrides the will.

Go through every account and policy and check three things:

- Is anyone named at all?
- Is it your partner, or a parent or former partner from years ago?
- Does the scheme allow an unmarried partner to be named? Some pension schemes pay survivor benefits only to a spouse. If yours does, that is a gap to cover another way.

This is free, takes an evening, and is the most commonly missed item on the list.

### 3. How you own your home

Title decides what happens to a home, and it is set when you buy.

In common-law countries there are two main forms.

**Joint tenancy with right of survivorship.** You own the whole property together. When one of you dies, the other automatically owns all of it. No will is needed and none can change it.

**Tenancy in common.** Each of you owns a distinct share, which can be unequal: 50/50, 70/30, whatever you record. Each share passes under its owner's will or, without one, to their relatives.

Neither is better. Joint tenancy protects the survivor simply. Tenancy in common lets you record unequal contributions and leave your share to someone else, such as children from an earlier relationship.

Civil-law countries use different concepts, and some offer clauses that give a surviving co-owner the whole property. The question to ask is the same everywhere: **if one of us dies, does that share go to the other automatically, or to the heirs?**

If only one of you is on the title, as with Sofia and Lena, the other's contributions need to be written down. Otherwise they are hard to prove.

### 4. A cohabitation agreement

Married couples who separate have a legal framework for dividing what they built. Unmarried couples have ordinary property law, which looks at whose name is on what.

A **cohabitation agreement** is a contract between you that fills the gap. It typically records:

- Who owns which assets now, and how new ones will be owned
- Who contributes what to the home, and what that contribution earns
- How shared debts are split
- What happens to the home and shared property if you separate

It matters most where contributions are unequal. One of you owns the flat. One of you earns much more. One of you stops working to raise children. Those are the situations where the partner with less on paper is most exposed.

Enforceability varies by country. These agreements carry more weight when each of you has had independent advice.

### 5. Powers of attorney

Two documents cover the decision-making row.

**A financial power of attorney** names who can manage your money and deal with institutions for you if you cannot.

**A healthcare power of attorney**, also called a healthcare proxy, names who makes medical decisions for you.

Without them, an unmarried partner can be shut out of both the hospital room and the bank account. Each partner usually needs their own set, naming the other.

## What documents cannot rebuild: tax

One row resists paperwork. In many tax systems, married couples can file jointly, pass assets to each other freely during life, and leave everything to each other with little or no inheritance tax. Unmarried partners are often taxed as strangers, with small allowances and high rates.

You cannot sign a form to change that. You can plan around it.

- **Insurance to pay the bill.** A life policy that one partner owns on the other's life, sized to cover the expected tax, means the survivor is not forced to sell the home to pay it. Ownership of the policy matters, so set it up with advice.
- **How assets are held.** Assets owned jointly from the start may avoid a later transfer that would be taxed.
- **Beneficiary designations.** In some systems, pension and insurance payouts are taxed differently from inherited assets.
- **Lifetime gifts within allowances.** Where annual allowances exist, using them steadily moves wealth without a bill.

Whether the saving from marrying or registering a partnership outweighs your reasons for not doing so is a personal decision. It helps to know what the number is.

## Shared money while you are both alive

Two everyday points are easy to miss.

**Joint debts are owed in full by each of you.** A joint loan or a joint account overdraft is usually **joint and several**: the lender can pursue either of you for the whole amount, not half. A separation does not change that.

**Accounts in one name belong to that person.** If the household savings sit in one partner's account, the other has no automatic claim to them. Splitting savings between two names, or holding a joint account for shared goals, keeps the record matching reality.

## Cross-continent notes

**England and Wales.** No cohabitation status. Wills, declared shares in property, and pension nominations do the work.

**France.** A civil partnership gives joint income tax treatment but no automatic inheritance, so partners need wills.

**Germany, Italy, Spain.** Forced heirship limits how much a will can leave to a partner, and tax allowances for unmarried partners are far smaller than for spouses.

**Australia, New Zealand, parts of Canada.** De facto partners gain significant property and inheritance rights after a qualifying period. Documents are still worth having, because they remove the need to prove the relationship.

**United States.** Rules are set state by state. Beneficiary designations carry particular weight because so much wealth sits in retirement accounts.

**Same-sex couples.** Where marriage is available, the choice is the same as for any couple. Where it is not, or where a marriage made elsewhere is not recognised, every item in this post matters much more. A later post in this series is devoted to that situation.

Two companion pieces extend this post to households of more than two: one on owning a home as a group of three or more, and one on households with more than two partners.

## Getting started

- **Tonight, for free:** check the beneficiary on every pension, policy, and account.
- **This month:** find out how your home is owned, and write down who has contributed what.
- **This quarter:** wills and powers of attorney for both of you, in the form your country requires.
- **If contributions are unequal:** a cohabitation agreement, with independent advice for each of you.
- **Once:** look up what your country's tax system does to an unmarried survivor, so the number is not a surprise.

Sofia and Lena needed one will, one beneficiary form, and one page recording Sofia's share of the flat. An evening's work and a modest fee would have changed everything that followed.

Marriage is one way to get these protections. It is not the only way. It is just the only one that happens without anyone deciding.
