Two couples have identical finances: the same flat in one partner's name, the same savings, the same pensions, fifteen years together. In each, the partner who owns the flat dies suddenly at 44.

The first couple was married. In many countries the flat passes to the surviving spouse, the pension pays a survivor benefit, and the bank talks to the surviving spouse the same week.

The second couple never married. In many countries the flat now passes by law to the dead partner's parents or siblings, the pension may pay nothing because its rules say "spouse", and anything that does reach the surviving partner may be taxed as a gift between strangers.

Nothing about the money was different. The only difference was which household the rules were written for.

## A default is a guess about your household

A **default** is what happens when you make no choice. Die without a will, and a fixed set of rules decides who inherits. Leave a pension's beneficiary form blank, and the scheme's rulebook decides who is paid. Become unable to make decisions with nobody appointed, and a court or a statutory list decides who speaks for you.

Defaults are useful: many people never write a will, and something sensible happening beats nothing happening. But each default is a guess about what you would have wanted, made with one household in mind. As the [life events](/blog/life-events/) post put it, the costly mistake is treating a foreseeable event as a surprise.

## Five defaults built for one kind of household

These defaults mostly imagine two married adults with shared children, a steady local employer and a life in one country.

| Default | What it assumes | Who it misses |
|---|---|---|
| Inheritance without a will | Your heirs are your spouse and blood relatives | Unmarried partners, chosen family, stepchildren |
| Survivor benefits in pensions | The person who depends on you is your legal spouse | Cohabiting partners, partners in unrecognised marriages |
| The tax unit | A couple is a married couple | Unmarried couples, who are often taxed as two strangers |
| Benefits through work | You have an employer who withholds tax and funds a pension | Freelancers, gig workers, informal workers |
| Credit and banking history | Your financial record was built here and is continuous | Anyone who moved countries or works in cash |

If that household is yours, the defaults mostly do a reasonable job. If any part of it is not, some may guess wrong, and people often find out at the worst moment.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">The household the rules imagine, and how many people it leaves out</summary>

Read enough pension rulebooks, tax codes and inheritance statutes and the same picture appears. The imagined household has:

- Two adults who are legally married to each other
- Children who are the biological or adopted children of both
- One or two salaried jobs with a stable local employer
- A working life spent in one country
- Conventional, interest-bearing banking and borrowing
- Family nearby who will step in when someone is ill

None of the groups in the table's "Who it misses" column is unusual. The International Labour Organization estimates that about 58% of the world's workers, more than two billion people, were in informal employment in 2023. Cohabiting without marrying is common among younger couples in much of Europe. Hundreds of millions of people live outside the country they were born in. The "default household" is one shape among many. It just happens to be the one the paperwork was drafted for.

</details>

## Three words this series uses

Every later post in this series leans on these three terms.

**Default assumption.** The guess a rule or product makes about who you are, such as "the beneficiary is the spouse".

**Deliberate designation.** A written choice, in a form the institution accepts, meant to replace or influence the default: a will, a **beneficiary designation** (the form naming who receives a pension or insurance payout) or a power of attorney (a document naming who may act for you).

**Recognition gap.** The distance between how your household works and what the law or an institution recognises: a partner who is family in every practical sense but a stranger on paper, or a marriage valid in one country and ignored in the next.

Naming a recognition gap shows where a designation, a workaround or local advice could help, though some gaps cannot be fully closed.

<details class="quick-check">
<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: you never filled in the beneficiary form on your pension. Who decides who is paid?</summary>

The scheme's own rules: that is the default. A beneficiary form may decide who is paid or only guide the scheme's decision, depending on the scheme and local law.

</details>

## Three questions find the defaults that do not fit

The audit asks three questions, in order, of any account that pays out and any rule or product.

**1. What is this assuming about me?** Read the rule, not the brochure. Who does the pension pay if you die? Who inherits under the rules where you live?

**2. Does that assumption hold for my household?** For your actual life, not the tidy version. Would the person you rely on be recognised here?

**3. If not, what goes in its place on purpose?** Sometimes a five-minute form, sometimes a document that needs a professional, sometimes a workaround such as extra insurance or a larger cash buffer.

The audit does not tell you what to decide. It shows which decisions are currently being made for you.

## One €80,000 pension, three different answers

Take one workplace pension worth €80,000 and run the audit for three people.

**A married employee.** Many schemes pay a survivor benefit to the legal spouse. The assumption holds, though many people still fill in the beneficiary form, because rules change and marriages end.

**An employee with a long-term unmarried partner.** The assumption fails. Many schemes pay a spouse or, failing that, the estate, and inheritance rules may leave the partner out entirely. Where the scheme accepts nominations, a form naming the partner may decide or influence who is paid; the scheme's rules say whether it is binding. Where it does not, a common workaround is life cover owned by the partner, plus a will where local rules leave room for one.

**A freelancer with no workplace pension.** There is no scheme to audit, so the default is that nothing is contributed or paid.

<details class="quick-check">
<summary class="disclosure">Quick check: is a missing default safer than a wrong one?</summary>

Not necessarily. With no scheme, there is no employer contribution and no survivor benefit, so a missing default can leave a bigger gap than a wrong one. One possible replacement is a retirement account opened and funded individually, with a named beneficiary where the account allows it.

</details>

## Many of the fixes are free

A list of defaults that fail you can look like a list of expensive problems. Mostly it is not. Deliberate designations fall into three cost bands:

- **Free, in an evening:** beneficiary forms, a written record of who owns what, a list of every account.
- **Modest:** a will, powers of attorney, a written agreement between partners.
- **Hard:** gaps no document closes, where the tools are workarounds.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">What each cost band involves</summary>

**Free, in an evening.** Naming beneficiaries on pensions, insurance and investment accounts. Writing down who owns what share of a shared home or car, and checking that the title or registry says the same. Keeping a list of every account, somewhere a trusted person can find it.

**Modest, and worth doing properly.** A will. Powers of attorney for money and for health decisions. A written agreement between partners about shared property. These need the form your jurisdiction requires, and many people find a local professional worth the cost.

**Hard: gaps no document closes.** A marriage that is not recognised, a status that blocks access to banking, a tax system that treats a partner as a stranger. Here the tools are workarounds. Insurance can stand in for a benefit you are excluded from. A bigger emergency fund can stand in for a missing safety net. In some places, whose name an asset is held in from the start affects later tax and inheritance, though changing it has its own consequences and needs local advice.

</details>

Many useful changes sit in the first band, though which protection matters most depends on the household and local law. The common failure is not cost. It is not knowing the default exists.

## The one-hour audit: five lists

A first pass takes about an hour with a notebook.

1. **Every account that pays out.** Who is named as beneficiary today? "Nobody" or "an ex" are the answers people most often want to change.
2. **What you own jointly.** Whose name is on each item, and what happens to your share if you die?
3. **Who inherits with no will.** Look up the rules where you live and compare that list with the people you would choose.
4. **Who could act for you.** If you were unconscious tomorrow, who could pay your rent, and who would a hospital listen to?
5. **What you would lose with your job.** Health cover, future pension contributions, income protection and, in some countries, residency status. Existing pension balances and some rights may continue.

The output is a short list of mismatches, not a plan. Many cost nothing to fix, though a beneficiary choice still deserves thought.

## The rules change at the border

The shape of the problem is the same everywhere; the details vary by country. Treat every specific in this series as an example of a category. The category travels. The specific does not.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Why a will does less in some countries</summary>

In England and Wales, much of the United States and other common-law systems, a will can leave almost everything to almost anyone. In much of continental Europe and Latin America, **forced heirship** rules (a share of the estate reserved by law for children, and sometimes a spouse) limit what a will can do. A will still matters there, but it works inside tighter limits.

</details>

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">When living together starts to count</summary>

Some countries give long-term cohabiting couples rights close to those of spouses after a set number of years. Others give them none, however long they have lived together. Registered partnerships sit in between, and the rights they carry vary widely.

</details>

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">How countries tax couples differently</summary>

Some systems tax individuals, some tax couples jointly, and several give married couples large exemptions on gifts and inheritance that unmarried partners do not get.

</details>

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Where benefits through work are the exception</summary>

In some economies most workers have formal contracts and social insurance. In others the majority do not, and "benefits through work" describes a minority.

</details>

## Where this series goes next

A common order is the one-hour audit first, then the free fixes, then the one paid item that matters most for that household, which depends on your country, your relationships and your work.

Later posts take the audit into specific situations: working without an employer, borrowing and investing without interest, moving countries, relying on one income, and sharing a life without a marriage certificate.

The defaults were never neutral. They were someone's best guess about a household that may not be yours. Which of the five is guessing about yours?
