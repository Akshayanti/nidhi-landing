The Mastery post on international retirement makes a simple point about money: the same retirement costs very different amounts in different countries, and moving can stretch a pension a long way. The earlier post on [geographic arbitrage](/blog/geographic-arbitrage/) made the same case for working years.

Both treat a move as a question of prices, taxes, and visas. Both assume that the household arriving in the new country is the same household that left.

For some couples it is not. A marriage is a legal status, and a border can change it.

Jonas and David married eight years ago in a country where that is an ordinary thing to do. Jonas is offered a job in another country. They move. Under the law of the new country, they are two unrelated men sharing a flat.

Nothing happened to their relationship. But David is not Jonas's next of kin there. He would not inherit by default. He cannot join Jonas's employer health plan. He is in the country on a separate visa that depends on his own job. If Jonas is taken to hospital, staff may not tell David anything.

This post is about that situation and how to plan for it. Same-sex couples meet it most often. It also reaches couples married only under religious or customary rites, and some couples of different nationalities. It is a financial planning post about a legal fact. It does not list which countries recognise which marriages, because the list changes and because recognition is often partial in ways a list hides.

## Part one: the marriage that does not travel

### Three things a destination can do

When you arrive married, the new country does one of three things.

| What the destination does | What it means |
|---|---|
| Recognises the marriage in full | Your rights are those of any married couple there |
| Treats it as a civil union or registered partnership | You get that status and its narrower set of rights |
| Gives it no legal effect | You are, legally, unrelated individuals |

The second case needs care. **Marriage recognition** and **partnership recognition** are different things. A registered partnership may give inheritance rights but not a survivor pension. It may give tax treatment but no immigration rights. The name of the status tells you little. The list of rights attached to it tells you everything.

There is a further wrinkle. Recognition can differ by purpose within a single country. A marriage may count for residency, because a court ruling requires it, and still not count for inheritance or parentage.

### What disappears

The post on [unmarried couples](/blog/unmarried-and-cohabiting-couples/) set out what marriage switches on automatically. Where a marriage is not recognised, all of it can switch off:

- Inheritance if there is no will
- Survivor benefits from the state pension and many workplace schemes
- Authority to make medical decisions and receive information
- Joint tax treatment, and tax-free transfers between spouses
- Cover as a dependant on an employer's health insurance
- The right to live in the country as the spouse of a resident
- Legal parentage of a child born to or adopted by the other spouse

In the words of this series, this is a **recognition gap** (the distance between how your household works and what the law where you live can see). What makes this version dangerous is that it opens without anyone doing anything. There is no letter. The rights are simply not there when you reach for them.

### The recognition check

Before accepting a job or choosing a place to retire, find the answers to six questions for the specific destination.

**1. What status will our marriage have there?**
Full marriage, a partnership, or nothing. If a partnership, get the list of rights.

**2. Can my spouse live and work there because of our marriage?**
If not, which visa will they hold, what does it depend on, and what happens to it if their job ends?

**3. If one of us dies there without a local will, who inherits?**
And what tax would the survivor pay, as a spouse or as an unrelated person?

**4. Who will a hospital treat as next of kin?**
And which document would change that?

**5. Will the employer's benefits treat us as a family?**
Health cover, relocation support, pension survivor benefits, life cover. Employer policy is sometimes more generous than local law, and sometimes it is where the problem shows first.

**6. If we have children, will both of us be their legal parents there?**
This is the question with the highest stakes and the least room for improvisation.

Good sources: the destination's official government guidance, your own country's embassy or consulate there, the employer's human resources and relocation teams, and a local lawyer. Organisations that support LGBTQ+ people and established expatriate groups often know how things work in practice, which can differ from how the rules read.

The check can change the decision. It can also change the terms. An employer who wants you there may fund a visa for your spouse, private health cover, or legal fees, if you ask before you sign.

### The visa is a financial issue

The geographic arbitrage post described many visa types and treated a spouse's right to come along as settled. A **reunification visa** (one that lets a family member join a resident) depends on how the destination defines family.

Where the marriage is not recognised, the trailing spouse needs their own route. That has consequences well beyond paperwork:

- **Their right to stay depends on their own job or their own funds.** Lose the job, and they may have to leave.
- **They may not be allowed to work,** which turns a two-income household into a one-income household for the length of the posting.
- **Time in the country may not count toward long-term residency** in the way a spouse's would.

A one-income household with a dependent partner who has no independent right to stay is a fragile arrangement. The post on [one-income households](/blog/solo-agers-and-single-income-households/) covers how to size the emergency fund. Couples in this position often also size it to cover the cost of both of them leaving at short notice.

## Part two: protections that do not need recognition

Rights that come from status vanish when the status does. Rights that come from **ownership** and **contract** do not. A form naming a beneficiary pays that person because the form says so. A deed in two names belongs to two people. Neither asks who you are to each other.

The usual approach is to move as much as possible from the first kind to the second.

### The stack

The unmarried couples post lists five documents: a will, beneficiary designations, a clear record of how the home is owned, an agreement between partners, and powers of attorney for money and health. A couple whose marriage is not recognised needs the same set. It is worth having even where the marriage is recognised today, since it holds whatever a future move or a change in the law does.

This post adds the part that matters across borders: what makes those documents actually work in another country.

### What makes a document portable

**Make it locally, or re-make it locally.** A power of attorney from home is often not accepted by a bank or hospital abroad. They want their own country's form, in their own language. Of all the documents, **healthcare and financial powers of attorney** are the most important to redo after a move, because they are the ones that stand in for a spouse's authority on an ordinary Tuesday.

**Translate and legalise what you bring.** Official documents generally need a certified translation and proof that they are authentic. Between countries that belong to the Apostille Convention, that proof is a standard certificate called an **apostille**. Elsewhere it means legalisation through a consulate. Bring certified copies of the marriage certificate even where it is not recognised: it is evidence of the relationship, and some institutions will act on it.

**Carry the papers.** A hospital at two in the morning will not search a registry. A card in a wallet naming the person to call and stating that a healthcare power of attorney exists, with a copy on a phone, does more than a perfect document in a drawer at home.

### Wills across borders

Two countries may each claim a say over what happens to your estate: the one you live in and the one where an asset sits, especially property.

- **One will per country where you hold significant assets** is the usual approach. Each must be written so that it does not revoke the other. That is a drafting detail that goes wrong often.
- **Forced heirship** may apply. Where the law reserves a share for children or other relatives, a will cannot give everything to a spouse the law does not recognise as one.
- **You may be able to choose which law applies.** Under European Union succession rules, a person can state in their will that the law of their nationality should govern their estate. For someone whose home country would recognise the marriage, that choice can matter a great deal. It does not apply in every member state and needs local advice.

### Keep the contract-based assets strong

- **Private pensions and life insurance.** Many couples name each other on every form, by name and not as "my spouse", and check whether the scheme pays survivor benefits to a partner it does not recognise as married. Where it does not, life insurance is a common way to fill the gap.
- **Where the assets sit.** Assets kept in a country that recognises your marriage stay under rules that treat you as married. There can be good reasons to leave savings and pensions at home when you move, weighed against tax, currency and access trade-offs.
- **Joint ownership.** Property and accounts held in both names are evidence of a shared life and, in many systems, pass to the survivor directly.
- **Tax.** Where you are taxed as strangers, the tools are the same as for unmarried couples: life insurance sized to the likely bill, lifetime gifts within allowances, and care over whose name assets are in.

### Children

Where both of you are legal parents at home, check whether both are in the destination. If one is not, that parent may be unable to consent to medical treatment, travel alone with the child, or keep custody if the other dies.

Adoption orders and court judgments confirming parentage are generally recognised more widely than a birth certificate alone. This is the area where specialist legal advice before moving is least optional.

## When you cannot leave, or do not want to

Not everyone in this position moved. Some couples live where the law changed around them, or where marriage was never available and a ceremony abroad has no effect at home. The same principle holds: build on ownership and contract, keep documents current under local law, and hold some assets where they are protected if you can.

## Cross-continent notes

This post deliberately names no list of countries. Some patterns are stable enough to describe.

**Recognition is a spectrum.** Full recognition, recognition as a lesser status, recognition for some purposes only, and none. The second and third are common and easy to mistake for the first.

**Regional courts matter.** In some regions a court ruling requires member states to recognise a marriage for specific purposes, such as the right to live there, without requiring recognition for everything else.

**Employers vary more than laws.** Large international employers often extend benefits to partners whatever local law says. Smaller local employers usually follow the law.

**Risk is not only financial.** In some countries same-sex relationships are criminalised. A financial plan is not the right tool for that decision, and nothing here should be read as suggesting it is.

## Getting started

- **Before you move:** run the six-question check. Ask the employer what it will do about the gaps.
- **Before you travel:** certified, translated, legalised copies of the marriage certificate, and of any adoption or parentage orders.
- **In the first month:** local powers of attorney for health and money. A card in each wallet.
- **In the first year:** a local will that works alongside the one at home. A review of every beneficiary form.
- **Ongoing:** weigh whether to keep a share of assets where your marriage is recognised, alongside the tax and currency trade-offs.

A marriage certificate is a promise two people made and a status one country granted. The promise goes everywhere you do. The status has to be rebuilt, document by document, wherever it is not honoured.
