Jonas and David married eight years ago in a country where that is an ordinary thing to do. Jonas is offered a job in another country, and they move. Under the law of the new country, they are two unrelated men sharing a flat.

Nothing happened to their relationship. But there, David is not Jonas's next of kin. He would not inherit by default, cannot join Jonas's employer health plan, and is in the country on a separate visa that depends on his own job. If Jonas is taken to hospital, staff may not tell David anything.

The [geographic arbitrage post](/blog/geographic-arbitrage/) treated a move as a question of prices, taxes and visas, assuming the household that arrives is the household that left. For some couples it is not, because a marriage is a legal status and a border can change it. Same-sex couples meet this most often; it also reaches couples married only under religious or customary rites, and some couples of different nationalities. This post does not list which countries recognise which marriages, because the list changes and recognition is often partial in ways a list hides.

## A marriage can lose its legal effect when you cross a border

A destination does one of three things with a marriage made elsewhere:

| What the destination does | What it means |
|---|---|
| Recognises the marriage in full | Your rights are those of any married couple there |
| Treats it as a civil union or registered partnership | You get that status and its narrower set of rights |
| Gives it no legal effect | You are, legally, unrelated individuals |

The middle case needs care: [partnership recognition](#term-partnership-recognition) is not marriage recognition. A registered partnership may give inheritance rights but not a survivor pension, or tax treatment but no immigration rights; the list of rights tells you more than the name. Recognition can also differ by purpose within one country: a marriage may count for residency, because a court ruling requires it, and not for inheritance or parentage.

Where a marriage is not recognised, everything the [unmarried couples post](/blog/unmarried-and-cohabiting-couples/) listed can switch off: inheritance without a will, survivor pensions, medical decisions and information, joint tax treatment and tax-free transfers, cover on an employer's health plan, the right to live in the country as a spouse, and legal parentage of a child. This is a [recognition gap](#term-recognition-gap), and what makes this version dangerous is that it opens without anyone doing anything. There is no letter; the rights may simply not be there when you reach for them.

## The recognition check tests a destination before you move

Six questions, answered for the specific destination before accepting a job or choosing where to retire:

1. **What status will our marriage have there?** Full marriage, a partnership with which rights, or nothing.
2. **Can my spouse live and work there because of our marriage?** If not, which visa, what it depends on, and what happens if their job ends.
3. **If one of us dies there without a local will, who inherits,** and is the survivor taxed as a spouse or a stranger?
4. **Who will a hospital treat as next of kin,** and which document would change that?
5. **Will the employer's benefits treat us as a family?** Health cover, relocation, pension survivor benefits, life cover. Employer policy is sometimes more generous than local law.
6. **If we have children, will both of us be legal parents there?** The question with the highest stakes and the least room for improvising.

Useful sources are official government guidance, your own country's embassy or consulate there, the employer's relocation team and a local lawyer; LGBTQ+ support organisations and established expatriate groups often know how rules work in practice. The check can change the decision, and it can change the terms: an employer who wants you there may fund a spouse's visa, private health cover or legal fees, if asked before signing.

A [reunification visa](#term-reunification-visa) lets a family member join a resident, and depends on how the destination defines family. Where the marriage is not recognised, the trailing spouse needs their own route, with consequences beyond paperwork: their right to stay depends on their own job or funds; they may not be allowed to work, turning two incomes into one for the posting; and their time in the country may not count toward long-term residency as a spouse's would. A one-income household with a partner who has no independent right to stay is fragile. ## Ownership and contract can protect you where status does not

Rights that come from status can vanish when the status does. Rights based on ownership or contract may depend less on marital status, though their effect still depends on the document and local law: a beneficiary form may be binding or advisory, and joint ownership does not necessarily include survivorship. A common planning exercise is to identify which protections can be documented independently of marital status, using the five documents from the unmarried couples post: a will, beneficiary forms, a clear record of how the home is owned, an agreement between partners, and powers of attorney for money and health. They are worth having even where the marriage is recognised today, since they can help after a future move or a change in the law.

- **Private pensions and life insurance:** many couples name each other on every form by name, not as "my spouse", and check whether the scheme pays survivor benefits to a partner it does not recognise as married; where it does not, life insurance is a common way to fill the gap.
- **Where assets sit:** assets kept in a country that recognises the marriage stay under rules that treat the couple as married, weighed against tax, currency and access trade-offs.
- **Joint ownership:** property and accounts in both names document ownership, but whether a deceased owner's share passes directly to the survivor depends on the form of ownership and local succession law.
- **Tax:** where couples are taxed as strangers, the tools are those of unmarried couples: life insurance sized to the likely bill, lifetime gifts within allowances, and care over whose name assets are in.

## What makes a will or power of attorney work in another country

**Making it, or re-making it, locally.** A power of attorney from home is often not accepted by a bank or hospital abroad, which want their own form in their own language. Healthcare and financial [powers of attorney](#term-power-of-attorney) are the most important to redo after a move, because they stand in for a spouse's authority on an ordinary Tuesday.

**Translating and legalising what you bring.** A receiving authority may ask for a certified translation and authentication. Where the Apostille Convention applies, an [apostille](#term-apostille) authenticates the document's signature or seal; elsewhere another legalisation process may apply. Neither guarantees that the document has the legal effect you want. Certified copies of the marriage certificate are worth bringing even where it is not recognised: it is evidence of the relationship, and some institutions act on it.

**Carrying the papers.** A hospital at two in the morning will not search a registry. A card in a wallet naming the person to call and stating that a healthcare power of attorney exists, with a copy on a phone, does more than a perfect document in a drawer at home.

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Wills across borders</summary>

Two countries may each claim a say over an estate: the one you live in and the one where an asset, especially property, sits. Some cross-border estates use one coordinated will and others separate wills for particular countries or assets; local advisers coordinate them so that one does not unintentionally revoke another. [Forced heirship](#term-forced-heirship) may reserve a share for children or other relatives, so a will cannot give everything to a spouse the law does not recognise. In EU states taking part in the Succession Regulation, a person can generally choose the law of a country of their nationality to govern their succession. Ireland and Denmark do not take part, family status is outside the regulation's scope, and public-policy or other local rules may still matter, so the effect for a particular couple needs specialist advice.

</details>

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Children, and couples who did not move</summary>

**Children.** Where both partners are legal parents at home, it matters whether both are in the destination. A parent who is not may be unable to consent to treatment, travel alone with the child, or keep custody if the other dies. An adoption order or parentage judgment may add evidence beyond a birth certificate, but whether the destination recognises it needs checking before travel or a move, and this is where specialist advice before moving can matter most.

**Couples who did not move.** Some live where the law changed around them, or where marriage was never available and a ceremony abroad has no effect at home. The same principle holds: build on ownership and contract, keep documents current under local law, and, where possible, hold some assets where they are protected.

</details>

<details class="lesson-depth">
<summary class="disclosure">Recognition is a spectrum, and employers vary more than laws</summary>

- **Recognition is a spectrum:** full, as a lesser status, for some purposes only, or none. The middle two are common and easy to mistake for the first.
- **Regional courts matter:** in some regions a court ruling requires member states to recognise a marriage for specific purposes, such as residence, without requiring it for everything else.
- **Employers vary more than laws:** large international employers often extend benefits to partners whatever local law says; smaller local employers usually follow the law.
- **Risk is not only financial:** in some countries same-sex relationships are criminalised. A financial plan is not the right tool for that decision.

</details>

A marriage certificate is a promise two people made and a status one country granted. The promise goes everywhere you do. The status has to be rebuilt, document by document, wherever it is not honoured.
