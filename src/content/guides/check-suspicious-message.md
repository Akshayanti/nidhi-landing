answer:
  - "You often can't tell for certain from the message itself. The usual check is to stop, use nothing in the message (no links, numbers or replies), and contact the company or person through a way you find yourself."
  - "Pressure to act now, a request for a code, password or payment, and an unusual way to pay are common signs of a scam."
scope:
  can: "Check a message calmly before you act on it."
  cannot: "Prove that a message is genuine. Passing these checks lowers the risk; it doesn't remove it."
protective:
  heading: "Already sent money, or shared a code or password?"
  now:
    - "Contact your bank or payment provider through its official app or a number you already trust, not one from the message. If someone has remote access to your device, disconnect it from the internet and use another device."
    - "Tell them what happened. Ask whether the payment can be stopped and how to secure your account."
    - "From a device you trust, change any password you shared and any password you reuse elsewhere, starting with email and banking. Turn on two-step sign-in where you can."
  canWait:
    - "Save the messages, phone numbers and screenshots."
    - "Report it to the police or your country's fraud reporting service."
    - "Watch your accounts and statements for anything you didn't do."
  note: "Getting money back isn't guaranteed. Speed can matter, so contacting the payment provider promptly helps. Be wary of anyone who then contacts you offering to recover the money for a fee: that is a common second scam."
needs: []
taskTime: "a few minutes"
form: steps
steps:
  - action: "Pause. Don't click, reply, call back or pay yet."
    why: "Scams rely on speed. A real bank, tax office or delivery company can wait while you check."
  - action: "Look for the warning signs."
    why: "Common ones: contact you didn't expect, a deadline, a request for a code, password, PIN or remote access, payment by gift card, crypto or a transfer to a \"safe account\", and an offer that sounds too good. One sign is a reason for care; several together are a strong warning."
  - action: "Check through a way you find yourself."
    why: "Sender names and caller ID can be faked. Use the number on your card, the official app, or a website address you type yourself, and ask: \"Did you send this?\""
    caution: "Don't use the links or phone numbers in the message, even when they look official."
  - action: "If it says it's from family or a friend in trouble, call them on the number you already have."
    why: "Urgent requests for money from \"a new number\" are a common scam pattern. A familiar voice, or the right answer to a personal question, isn't proof on its own: voices can be imitated and personal details found online."
  - action: "If your own check confirms it's a scam, report it, then delete it."
    why: "Many phones and email apps have a \"report junk\" option, and some countries have a number for forwarding scam texts. Reporting helps others get warned."
